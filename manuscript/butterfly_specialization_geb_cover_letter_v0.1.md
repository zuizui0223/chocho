# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography

**Working draft v0.1**

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Research Article, **“Anthropogenic host redistribution expands butterfly resource geography across the specialization spectrum,”** for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

Butterfly specialization is commonly summarized along a specialist–generalist axis, but global change acts directly on the geography of the resources underlying that axis. We therefore ask how anthropogenic redistribution of larval host plants changes butterfly resource geography, whether broad generalists receive a disproportionate gain, and whether similar aggregate gains are assembled through the same or different host portfolios.

Previous work has linked butterfly diet breadth to geographic ranges and shown that introduced hosts can facilitate range changes in particular systems. Our contribution is the complementary resource-side reconstruction: we compare native and contemporary larval-resource geography under one framework across 239 butterfly species and then separate the magnitude of opportunity gain from the concentration of host contributions. Introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed opportunity for 206/239 species and increased aggregate resource geography from 26,530 to 41,083 species × region units (+54.9%), yet proportional expansion was essentially unrelated to host-family breadth. The architecture of that expansion differed sharply: in the conservative host-taxonomy-adequate subset, the largest contributing host accounted for a median 74.7% of added opportunity in one-family butterflies versus 31.3% in butterflies using six or more host families. Thus specialists and generalists can gain similar total opportunity through contrasting interaction portfolios.

We then tested an independently frozen extension: whether broader host-family diets weaken climatic filtering within available host-resource geography. After frozen quality gates and cross-fitting, 23 of 24 informative species had filtering scores above the neutral value, but the predicted release with increasing host-family breadth was not supported after controlling for contemporary resource breadth. We retain that negative independent result rather than retuning the prediction, because it sharpens the paper’s central conclusion: taxonomic diet breadth, spatial resource opportunity, and abiotic filtering are partially coupled but non-interchangeable dimensions.

We believe the manuscript fits *Global Ecology and Biogeography* because it identifies a broad-scale consequence of human redistribution that is not visible from either consumer ranges or diet breadth alone: plant redistribution can substantially expand ecological opportunity while leaving the proportional gain nearly unrelated to taxonomic generalism, because specialists and generalists assemble that gain through contrasting interaction portfolios. The resulting hierarchical framework generalizes beyond butterflies to other consumers whose resources are being redistributed globally.
