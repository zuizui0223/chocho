# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography

**Working draft v0.1**

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Research Article, **“Butterfly specialization is hierarchical: host portfolios structure resource opportunity while climate filters realized geography,”** for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

Ecological specialization is commonly summarized along a specialist–generalist axis, but that summary can collapse several different biological quantities. In this study, we separate family-level larval host breadth, species-level host portfolio richness, geographic resource breadth, anthropogenic resource-opportunity architecture, and climatic filtering of realized distributions.

Previous work has linked butterfly diet breadth to geographic ranges and shown that introduced hosts can facilitate range changes in particular systems. Our contribution is to reconstruct native and contemporary larval-resource geography under one framework across 239 butterfly species, and then separate total opportunity gain from the concentration of host contributions. Introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed opportunity for 206/239 species and increased aggregate resource geography from 26,530 to 41,083 species × region units (+54.9%), yet proportional expansion was essentially unrelated to host-family breadth. The architecture of that expansion differed much more: in the conservative host-taxonomy-adequate subset, the largest contributing host accounted for a median 74.7% of added opportunity in one-family butterflies versus 31.3% in butterflies using six or more host families.

We then tested an independently frozen extension: whether broader host-family diets weaken climatic filtering within available host-resource geography. After frozen quality gates and cross-fitting, 23 of 24 informative species had filtering scores above the neutral value, but the predicted release with increasing host-family breadth was not supported after controlling for contemporary resource breadth. We retain that negative independent result rather than retuning the prediction, because it sharpens the paper’s central conclusion: taxonomic diet breadth, spatial resource opportunity, and abiotic filtering are partially coupled but non-interchangeable dimensions.

We believe the manuscript fits *Global Ecology and Biogeography* because it uses broad-scale comparative and spatial analyses to derive a general ecological framework rather than a taxon-specific natural-history conclusion. The study also addresses a general consequence of human activity: redistribution of plant resources can alter ecological opportunity without simply erasing specialization, because similar aggregate opportunity may be assembled through very different interaction portfolios.

The manuscript is approximately 4,150 words of main text with a structured abstract below 300 words and five rendered display figures. The analyses are fully reproducible from versioned code, frozen input identities, workflow receipts, and externally sourced public datasets.
