# Port-linked botanical assembly accompanies disproportionate butterfly host-resource expansion on islands

**Research Article draft v0.1 | 5 November 2026 | Intended journal: Journal of Biogeography**

**Status:** Draft only. Not submitted, not coauthor-approved. Global GEB manuscript remains unchanged. All “resource gains” below are *reconstructed potential host availability*, not measured butterfly colonization.

## Abstract

**Aim:** Plant introductions alter the geography of potential insect resources without necessarily changing insect dispersal. We asked whether butterfly larval-host resource opportunities expand disproportionately on islands and whether port-associated introduced floras are specifically enriched in butterfly hosts.

**Location:** Global WGSRPD Level 3 insular and mainland botanical regions; supplementary island-pair comparisons.

**Taxon:** A fixed network of 239 butterflies and their documented larval hosts.

**Methods:** We reconstructed native-only and contemporary butterfly × region host opportunities from pinned plant distributions and host links. We compared islands and mainland regions, and associated modern indexed port counts with introduced-plant and distinct larval-host taxon richness, adjusting for area, broad biogeography, native resources and botanical recording coverage. We then conditioned host richness on all introduced flora and compared host and butterfly assemblage similarities among islands.

**Results:** Potential resources increased by **118.9% on islands** versus **40.9% on mainland regions**. An area–biogeographic comparison retained an insular excess of 10.7 percentage points in introduced-resource share. Among 98 insular regions with botanical effort data, port counts correlated positively with all introduced plants (β = **+0.244**, descriptive *p* = 0.0012) and introduced larval-host taxa (β = **+0.164**, *p* = 0.011). A positive host-specific residual was absent after conditioning on all introduced flora (β = −0.042, *p* = 0.093). Among 51 island landmasses, candidate port-host and later butterfly assemblage similarities were positively correlated (ρ = **+0.300**, 1,275 non-independent pairs), but the earlier butterfly correspondence was stronger.

**Main conclusions:** Port-rich insular regions have broader introduced floras encompassing more potential butterfly hosts, without a detectable additional positive host-specific port association. These are observational resource-geography patterns, not evidence of ship-borne introduction, realized host use or butterfly colonization.

## Introduction

Biogeographical barriers are often examined through the capacity of animals to cross them. For herbivorous insects, however, arrival is only one requirement for persistence: a larval host must also occur within reach at a biologically suitable place and time. Human activity redistributes plants across established natural barriers. Such redistribution can change the potential resource geography of a butterfly even when its flight ability and known diet remain constant.

Islands offer a particularly strong contrast. Their native floras differ from mainland source pools, and some islands have limited native plants from the larval-host taxa known for geographically widespread butterflies. Introducing a plant can therefore add multiple potential butterfly × island resource combinations. But the large-scale consequence remains uncertain: do human-associated plant movements expand resource opportunities especially on islands, and where in the island system do those new resources accumulate?

Ports are candidate entry points for organisms and propagules, but their ecological interpretation is ambiguous. Port-rich regions also contain cities, roads, ornamental plantings, trade-related land use and unequal botanical collecting effort. Hence a positive port–larval-host association can arise without ports preferentially transporting host plants. A stronger question distinguishes a **general introduced-flora accumulation mechanism** from **host-specific port enrichment**.

We addressed this distinction at three complementary analytical grains. First, we compared introduced-host-mediated resource opportunity in insular and mainland botanical regions, using the same butterfly–host relations but different plant-geographical states. Second, we tested whether the number of indexed ports covaries with all introduced plant species, introduced larval-host taxa and the residual abundance of host taxa after accounting for the wider flora. Third, we compared whether islands sharing port-indexed candidate hosts also share later and earlier butterfly assemblage records.

We predicted that, if ports index the broader assembly of introduced flora, their counts would be positively associated with both total introduced plant richness and the subset of larval-host taxa, but **not** with a positive host-specific residual after the total introduced flora was considered. Conversely, if ports were specifically enriched for larval-host plants, a positive conditional port–host association should remain. Neither prediction, as presently measured, identifies the actual historical carrier of plants or a causal impact of ports on butterfly colonization.

## Materials and methods

### Biological sources and island geography

We held known plant–butterfly larval-host relationships fixed for **239 butterfly species** (pinned HOSTS sources). Accepted plant identities and contemporary native versus introduced distributions came from a pinned World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP) source. The geographical units were World Geographical Scheme for Recording Plant Distributions (WGSRPD) Level 3 botanical regions. Such units may represent entire archipelagos and must not be interpreted as single physical islands or individual ports.

The strictly classified source contained **126 insular**, **237 mainland** and six mixed Level 3 regions. Mixed regions were excluded. The island classification included the Canary Islands but did not mistake the US state of Rhode Island for an isolated botanical island. The insular and mainland groups had **123** and **236** regions, respectively, with a positive contemporary reconstructed opportunity.

For butterfly species b and region r, a *native-only resource opportunity* meant that at least one accepted larval-host taxon was native to r. A *contemporary resource opportunity* meant that at least one accepted host was indexed in the contemporary flora of r. The introduced-added opportunity was their difference at fixed butterfly–host identities. Presence of one host somewhere in a Level 3 region can produce a positive resource cell; this does **not** measure host abundance, co-occurrence with butterflies, phenology, nutritional suitability or realized occupancy.

### Island versus mainland resource expansion

Within each class, we summed native and contemporary binary butterfly × region units and calculated added/native × 100 as a pooled proportional expansion. We also compared introduced-added/contemporary opportunities as a bounded added-resource share, accommodating regions with zero native-only host resources.

To reduce the island–mainland area and biogeography mismatch, we calculated the difference in mean added-resource share within shared WGSRPD Level 1 botanical regions. A stricter comparison retained only common Level 1 × log-area quartile strata and weighted differences by the smaller number of island and mainland regions. **4,999 within-stratum label permutations** were descriptive tests of the observed association, not a randomised intervention or evidence that spatial dependence had been eliminated.

### Ports, introduced floras and source recording effort

We aligned the published World Port Index (WPI) modern port inventory to WGSRPD Level 3 regions. Across **123 resource-active insular regions**, **982 indexed ports** were recorded: 91 regions had at least one indexed port and 32 had none. Zero *indexed* ports does not prove the historical absence of shipping or maritime access. We transformed port counts as log₂(1 + ports).

Entire introduced vascular-plant richness was calculated from accepted WCVP regional status. Introduced larval-host taxon richness was counted as **distinct introduced plant taxa** matching at least one host of the fixed butterfly panel; duplicate butterfly–host relationships did not multiply the plant count. Therefore, introduced larval-host taxa are a subset of all introduced plant taxa, whereas potential butterfly resource cells count a different butterfly × region quantity.

GBIF indexing of all plants provided an estimate of botanical recording coverage. Only **98** insular regions had usable effort data; failed queries were not coded as no botanical effort. Region-level regression analyses compared log(1 + introduced plants) and log(1 + introduced larval-host taxa) with log₂(1 + ports), conditioning on region polygon area, broad Level 1 botanical region, the corresponding native flora/host-resource baseline and log(1 + indexed botanical records). Port exposure labels were permuted within Level 1 × within-Level 1 area halves (4,999 draws). Permutation probabilities are described as *descriptive* because the regions are historically, geographically and taxonomically dependent.

To test host specificity, a nested host-richness model also included log(1 + all introduced flora richness). This yields a conditional port–host association **at fixed observed total introduced flora**, not a causal mediation coefficient: the introduced plant pool may itself be downstream of human infrastructure, and the host pool is necessarily a subset of it.

### Host and butterfly assemblage records on islands

A separate fixed island network linked port-indexed candidate introduced larval-host plants with earlier and later butterfly index records on **51 island landmasses**. We compared Jaccard similarity of the port-host species lists with Jaccard similarity of later and earlier butterfly assemblage indices across **1,275 pairs**. Spearman correlations and a descriptive partial-rank correlation adjusting for between-island geographical distance and earlier butterfly-assemblage similarity were calculated.

These 1,275 observations are *pairs among the same 51 endpoints*, not 1,275 independent islands. Later records mean newly indexed butterflies, not necessarily first arrival or proven breeding. Plant data pooled across 2001–2024 can include observations after the butterfly record and therefore cannot establish a prospective introduction sequence.

### Ancillary tests and inference boundary

Exploratory tests of butterfly disturbance association, colonial exchange histories, port proximity to wastewater infrastructure, plant nutrient-affinity indicators, host first-indexing dates and butterfly wing proxies are retained as context or limitations. They were not used to construct a causal model of port-to-host-to-butterfly arrival. A modern WPI port, contemporary introduced-plant distribution and first GBIF record together cannot identify the year or vector of plant arrival.

## Results

### Islands show disproportionate potential resource expansion

The insular regions had **4,829** native-only butterfly × region resource units and **10,570** contemporary units, adding **5,741** opportunities through contemporary introduced-host geography: a pooled increase of **118.9%** over the native baseline. The mainland group increased from **21,215** to **29,901**, with **8,686** added units, corresponding to **40.9%**. The introduced-addition share of contemporary opportunities was **54.3%** in insular regions versus **29.0%** on mainland regions.

The weighted island–mainland difference in mean added-resource share was +0.144 within broad Level 1 botanical geography. When restricting to area-quartile × Level 1 strata that contained both classes, the difference remained **+0.107** (10.7 percentage points; descriptive permutation *p* = 0.0002). Ten insular regions had contemporary opportunity but zero native-only opportunity in the fixed butterfly panel. These percentages refer to the *reconstruction of candidate resource cells*, not populations of butterflies.

### More ports correspond to broader introduced floras and more host taxa

Within the **98 botanical-effort-complete insular regions**, indexed port counts were positively associated with introduced vascular-plant richness (β = **+0.2445**, descriptive within-stratum permutation *p* = **0.0012**) after controlling for area, native flora, Level 1 geography and GBIF botanical indexing. On the log(1 + species) response scale, one additional unit of log₂(1 + ports) corresponded to a fitted 27.7% multiplicative difference in (1 + introduced plant richness).

Introduced larval-host taxa showed a positive association with the same indexed port exposure (β = **+0.1639**, descriptive *p* = **0.011**), equivalent to a fitted 17.8% multiplicative difference in (1 + distinct introduced host taxa) per exposure unit. These coefficients measure modern geographical associations, not the effect of increasing actual shipping traffic or propagule movement.

### The relationship does not preferentially select larval hosts

Once log(1 + introduced flora richness) was added to the host-taxon model, the positive port association was not retained: the effort-complete model gave β = **−0.0415**, descriptive permutation *p* = **0.093**. Thus, among islands with comparable broad introduced-flora richness, there was **no detectable positive additional association** of ports with larval-host taxa.

The unadjusted host–port relationship and its conditional disappearance are compatible with host plants accumulating as part of the overall introduced flora. They do **not** independently establish that ports produced these introductions or that the total introduced flora statistically mediates a known causal port effect. The conditional β is slightly negative, and the direction should not be reframed as proven host suppression.

### Candidate host geography corresponds with butterfly assemblage geography

Between the 51 island landmasses, similarity in candidate port-associated host assemblages correlated with similarity in later-indexed butterflies (Spearman **ρ = +0.300** across 1,275 non-independent pairs). Adjusting descriptively for geographical distance and previously indexed butterfly similarity gave partial-rank **ρ = +0.215**. However, the raw host correlation with **earlier** butterfly assemblage similarity was **stronger** (**ρ = +0.418**).

These results demonstrate a positive observational correspondence of host and butterfly index geography, but not an association specific to newly arriving butterflies. The combined records do not reveal actual movement among islands or successful reproduction at newly documented locations.

### Additional mechanistic tests remain unidentifiable or exploratory

A secondary butterfly disturbance-affinity analysis suggested stronger potential resource gains in disturbance-associated butterflies (full trait-panel adjusted coefficient +0.147; genus-bootstrap 95% interval +0.011 to +0.287), but a stricter host-taxonomy adequacy subset was uncertain (+0.062; interval −0.069 to +0.191). This was not adopted as an established taxon-general mechanism.

Only **12 fully documented case rows, two species and eight region cells** in the latest literature-based screen had all host-climate-port-wing proxies and prior indexed host evidence, insufficient to identify which compatibility condition was last to become available. The wing-size × port question had only **13** observed resource-opportunity cells and could not support a multivariable interaction. In a separate analysis of **78 WGSRPD regions**, the first indexed larval-host year was about **8.72 years earlier** per unit increase in log(1 + botanical index effort), descriptive permutation *p* = **0.0004**. These records therefore cannot be treated as independent measurements of plant residence duration or first introduction.

## Discussion

### Human redistribution changes the opportunity landscape most where native resources are restricted

The disproportionate reconstruction of introduced-host-mediated opportunities on islands provides a resource-centred view of geographical isolation. It does not imply that more butterflies have crossed the sea, but that their *possible* regional larval-host resources have changed especially strongly relative to insular native baselines. The association persists across the portion of island–mainland comparisons that overlap in broad biogeography and area, while unmeasured differences in climate, land use and source recording remain.

Because the response is binary at a botanical-region level, even one host indexed anywhere in a region can add a potential resource unit. The resource increase does not capture host population abundance, life-stage quality, microgeographical co-occurrence, phenology or the existence of butterfly breeding populations. This difference between potential resources and realized ecological outcomes is central to the interpretation.

### Ports identify botanical assembly, not preferential importation of butterfly food plants

The two positive port–plant models and the conditional host model tell a more coherent story together than separately. Indexed ports mark islands that also have larger introduced vascular floras. Larval-host plant taxa are more numerous on those islands, but **their positive port relationship is not above and beyond the introduced flora as a whole**. Selective enrichment of plants merely because they are butterfly hosts is unnecessary to describe the results.

This interpretation is parsimonious but explicitly observational. Modern ports may correlate with introduced ornamental plantings, urbanisation, gardens, botanical collecting effort, economy, geography and trade routes, as well as with propagule arrival. The original country-to-country transport or plant-import events were not dated or observed, and modern WPI port inventories are retrospective proxies rather than records of colonial-era shipping.

The results point to a general ecological proposition: the geography of a dependent insect's potential resources can be reshaped by processes moving the *plant resource community* even when no change is observed in the insect's inherent dispersal ability or feeding links. Port-rich landscapes are plausible nodes in this process, but the evidence identifies geographical correspondence rather than the physical vector.

### Butterfly composition offers concordance, not a proven colonisation sequence

The island-to-island Jaccard result links different types of records: introduced-host candidate plant lists and butterfly observation assemblages. Its positive correlation is compatible with the expectation that plant-resource composition constrains some butterfly possibilities. Crucially, the stronger relationship with butterflies indexed in the earlier period indicates that this is also a persistent biogeographical association, not an arrival-specific effect. Pair dependence, spatial confounding and overlapping plant/butterfly recording periods prevent causal inference.

A decisive future test would combine externally documented local plant introductions, source-specific butterfly breeding evidence, climate compatibility and arrival risk, using independent observation effort for all four. The present data do not contain a sufficiently complete series to estimate which of those conditions became sufficient last or whether shipping substitutes for butterflies' own flight capacity.

### Colonial histories, disturbance and nutrient proxies are contextual rather than primary mechanisms

Colonial-era trading systems and horticultural transfers could help explain why introduced plants are shared among remote islands. However, the available empire-history overlays omit some relevant empires and their source-specific host-plant similarity relationships are not stable after geographical stratification. We treat this as a plausible historical contributor, not a quantitatively identified mechanism.

A separate line finds associations of port infrastructure and modelled wastewater production with the relative recording of candidate hosts; another finds positive nutrient-affinity associations among port-indexed host taxa. Yet national sewage-N pathway models and 3km human-excretion grids do not demonstrate terrestrial nitrogen delivery to plants, while agricultural N-input potential is itself strongly linked to land use and outcome definition. These are informative alternatives and modifiers, not a demonstrated sewage → host → butterfly pathway. Disturbance-related butterfly traits similarly provide a possible filter but are insufficiently robust to headline the present paper.

### Limits and implications

This study cannot claim ship-borne introduction, establishment of introduced hosts at particular harbour facilities, a host residence time, nutritional support from sewage, or new butterfly populations. Port richness, modern regional alien status and apparent first occurrence dates do not supply historical causal order. Area and broad biogeography controls do not eliminate socioeconomic, spatial or botanical sampling effects. The biological interpretation should therefore remain at the level of **introduced plant assembly and reconstructed larval-resource opportunity**.

Nevertheless, the distinction between host-specific selection and broader introduced-flora accumulation is useful for island biodiversity science. It suggests that the ecological consequences of plant redistribution may propagate through interactions even where specific imported plants were not the explicit target of transport or management. That proposition is consistent with the current geography and is a priority for future independent historical and field tests.

## Conclusions

Potential butterfly larval-resource geography has expanded much more strongly in islands than mainland botanical regions. More ports coincide with richer introduced floras and more introduced larval-host taxa, but the port association does not retain a positive host-specific excess after the whole introduced flora is considered. The concordance of island host and butterfly assemblage records provides another observational dimension, while arrival, shipping and biological establishment remain unresolved.

**The principal finding is a geographical resource-assembly pattern, not a causal demonstration of butterfly colonisation.**
