# Plant globalization expands and homogenizes butterfly larval-resource geography
**Running title:** Plant globalization and resource niches


## Abstract

**Aim:** To test whether human redistribution of larval host plants merely enlarges butterfly resource opportunity or also homogenizes regional resource assemblages and increases geographic resource overlap among butterfly species.

**Location:** Global.

**Time period:** Contemporary host distributions; butterfly occurrences 2010–2026.

**Major taxa studied:** Butterflies and larval host plants.

**Methods:** For 239 butterflies, we held host identities fixed and reconstructed native-only and contemporary host-resource geography. We quantified expansion, regional resource-assemblage similarity, pairwise resource-envelope overlap and exact shared-host geography. Fixed-margin nulls preserved added opportunity per butterfly and region. A 32-species climate-stratified panel was reused for secondary occurrence validation.

**Results:** Introduced host ranges expanded reconstructed resource geography for 206/239 butterflies and increased aggregate butterfly × region coverage by 54.9%. Mean similarity among regional butterfly resource assemblages increased from 0.277 to 0.462 (+66.8%), exceeding a fixed-margin null median of 0.453 (p = 0.002). Mean pairwise overlap among butterfly resource envelopes increased from 0.222 to 0.328 (+47.4%), also exceeding fixed-margin expectation (0.321; p = 0.002). Exact shared-host butterfly-pair × region units increased from 62,473 to 141,885 (+127.1%); 920 of 986 host-sharing pairs gained new shared-resource regions. Introduced hosts also recovered 66/115 outside-native butterfly × region observations (57.4%).

**Main conclusions:** Plant globalization changes both the amount and structure of butterfly resource space. Moving already-used hosts can simultaneously relax geographic resource limitation and homogenize resource opportunity without host switching. Shared-resource geography therefore increases opportunities for both resource filtering and potential competition, outcomes that require local abundance and performance data to distinguish.

**Keywords:** biotic homogenization, butterflies, global change, host plants, introduced species, resource geography, species interactions
---

## 1. Introduction

Consumer distributions are constrained not only by climate and dispersal but by the geography of the resources on which consumers depend. For herbivorous insects, larval host plants define a particularly strong niche dimension: host availability can limit butterfly distributions, and changes in host use can accompany range expansion (Slove & Janz 2011; Braschler & Hill 2007; Lancaster 2020; Neu et al. 2021; Rashid et al. 2026). This literature usually asks how properties of the consumer—especially specialization—shape geographic range. Yet the same interaction can change geographically even when the consumer does not change, because the resource itself moves.

Human transport has redistributed plants at a global scale. More than 13,000 plant species have naturalized outside their native ranges, and non-native naturalizations have substantially homogenized regional floras (van Kleunen et al. 2015; Daru et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2021). Yang et al. (2021) further showed that floristic convergence is pronounced between geographically distant, climatically similar regions. For butterflies, such redistribution can place an already-known larval host in regions where that resource was historically absent. Regional studies show that exotic plants can become larval hosts and sometimes accompany butterfly range expansion or altered phenology (Graves & Shapiro 2003; Braga 2023). The immediate increase in potential resource area is only one possible consequence, however. If the same widely transported plants are used by many consumers, plant globalization could also make previously distinct regional resource assemblages more similar and increase the geographic overlap of consumer resource niches.

This second consequence connects biotic redistribution to community ecology. Species introductions can homogenize ecological interaction networks across regions (Fricke & Svenning 2020), but a different mechanism is possible even when interaction identities remain fixed: moving existing partners can change **where the same interaction links are simultaneously available**. In butterflies, host sharing may promote co-occurrence through resource filtering or, when resources are limiting, increase the scope for interspecific competition. A regional analysis of Japanese butterflies found that host sharing was associated more strongly with co-occurrence than with competitive exclusion, highlighting that shared resources can filter consumers into the same places without implying realized competition (Nakadai et al. 2018). Whether anthropogenic host redistribution has globally expanded such shared-resource geography is unknown.

Butterflies provide a tractable system because host-use records can be linked to native and introduced plant distributions. We define a **geographic resource envelope** as the union of regions containing a butterfly's known larval hosts. Holding host identities fixed isolates the spatial consequences of partner redistribution. The same reconstruction describes regional butterfly resource assemblages and geographic overlap among butterfly resource envelopes.

We ask four ecological questions. First, how much does plant redistribution expand butterfly larval-resource geography, and which hosts account for that added opportunity? Second, does this expansion homogenize regional resource assemblages and butterfly resource geography beyond what is expected from the amount of opportunity added to each butterfly and region? Third, does introduced-host geography increase exact shared-host resource exposure among butterflies, including across butterfly families, and how redundant is the added resource network to host removal? Fourth, does the newly available host geography align with contemporary butterfly occurrences beyond structural overlap expectations? We retain taxonomic diet breadth as a secondary modifier—testing whether broad generalists receive disproportionate proportional gain—rather than treating specialist–generalist status as the primary ecological response.

If plant globalization only adds idiosyncratic resources, resource envelopes may expand without making regions or consumers more similar. In contrast, redistribution of widely shared hosts predicts simultaneous **resource release and resource homogenization**: individual butterflies gain geographic opportunity while regional resource assemblages and consumer resource envelopes converge. Increased exact shared-host geography would then create a second prediction for local communities: greater opportunity for resource-based co-occurrence and, where resources are limiting, greater potential competition exposure.
## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Conceptual design: larval host breadth, resource geography and butterfly occurrence

We organized the analysis around three related but non-equivalent quantities. **Taxonomic interaction breadth** describes which kinds of larval hosts a butterfly uses, measured here as host-family richness. **Resource geography** describes where those known hosts occur, represented by the union of their geographic distributions. **Realized butterfly geography** describes where the butterfly is actually observed. Human redistribution enters between the first two quantities: moving a known host can alter a butterfly's resource geography without changing its taxonomic interaction breadth.

We therefore first reconstructed each butterfly's resource geography under native-only and contemporary host distributions and measured the difference as anthropogenic resource gain. We then asked whether redistribution changes the **structure** of resource space: similarity among regional butterfly resource assemblages, overlap among butterfly resource envelopes, and the geography over which butterfly pairs share exact host species. Host-family breadth was retained as a secondary modifier of proportional gain, and contemporary butterfly occurrences provided a secondary check on realization of the added opportunity.

The LepTraits-derived descriptor panel contained 339 species (Shirey et al. 2022). Resource analyses used 239 species with positive host-family breadth and at least one reconstructed native host-resource WGSRPD3 unit. A conservative subset of 215 species also required the number of resolved host species to equal or exceed LepTraits host-family count. A separate 32-species panel was used for occurrence validation and climate analysis; it had been selected to balance the subsequent climate test, not optimized for validation. Twenty-four species passed the climate quality criteria.

The resource reconstruction is descriptive and uses fixed LepTraits, HOSTS and WCVP inputs. The 32-species panel was assembled separately for the climate test, using eligibility and analysis rules fixed before its ecological responses were evaluated; the occurrence analysis is a secondary use of that panel. Resource homogenization, shared-resource exposure, host-removal stress tests, the host-contribution decomposition and later null-model sensitivities are post-hoc analyses and are interpreted accordingly.

### 2.2 Reconstructing native and contemporary resource geography

Larval host records came from HOSTS and plant taxonomy and distributions from WCVP (Robinson et al. 2023; Govaerts et al. 2021). Species-level host names were resolved to accepted WCVP taxa. Under WCVP terminology, introduced distributions are wild non-native records (including casual, naturalised and invasive occurrences), not cultivation records; plants that are only cultivated or merely planted without self-reproduction are not classified as introduced.

For each butterfly, the **native resource envelope** was the union of WGSRPD level-3 units in which any resolved host was native, extant and non-doubtful. The **contemporary resource envelope** additionally retained introduced host distributions. Host identities were held fixed between the two envelopes, so their difference isolates redistribution of already-known resources rather than host switching or expansion of the butterfly's recorded diet. WGSRPD3 is a coarse regional framework (Brummitt et al. 2001), so these envelopes represent geographic resource opportunity rather than local host occupancy.

As a crop-host sensitivity, we repeated the reconstruction after excluding host taxa listed in the FAO Indicative Crop Classification, first by exact botanical binomial and then with an intentionally over-conservative rule that also excluded all congeners when the FAO list specified a crop as spp. Host-family breadth was kept fixed as the specialization predictor; only the reconstructed resource envelopes and host contributions were recalculated (Supplementary Table S7).

We calculated native and contemporary resource breadth, introduced-added units, the contemporary/native ratio, log resource expansion, and the introduced share of contemporary geography. In a post-hoc descriptive decomposition, each butterfly × WGSRPD3 unit newly added by introduced host distributions contributed one unit of credit divided equally among the host species that added that unit. Credits therefore summed exactly to the aggregate number of added butterfly × region units. We summarized the concentration of those credits across host plants using cumulative top-k shares, the Gini coefficient and the inverse-Herfindahl effective contributor number. As a taxonomic sensitivity, contributing plant species were first collapsed to unique genera within each butterfly × region unit and the unit credit was then divided equally among those genera. This decomposition is explicitly contribution-weighted: a plant can accumulate credit by being used by more focal butterflies, by adding more geographic units, or both. We therefore use it to describe which resources generate the reconstructed opportunity, not as a sample of independent plant-level effects.

### 2.3 Resource homogenization, shared-resource exposure and removal stress

We quantified whether added resource geography changed compositional structure rather than only area. For each WGSRPD3 region, we represented the set of focal butterflies for which at least one known larval host was available under native-only and contemporary host distributions. We calculated mean pairwise Jaccard similarity among all 62,835 pairs of the 355 regions with a non-empty native resource assemblage; ten regions that acquired focal-butterfly resources only after introduced host ranges were added lacked a native baseline and were excluded from this paired homogenization comparison. We also calculated pairwise Jaccard overlap among the geographic resource envelopes of all 28,441 butterfly pairs.

To separate compositional convergence from the mechanical effect of adding opportunity, we randomized the added butterfly × WGSRPD3 incidence matrix by degree-preserving swaps, retaining each butterfly's number of added regions, each region's number of added butterfly opportunities and native incidences as structural exclusions. We used 499 matrices and add-one Monte Carlo p values. This conservative null asks whether the observed arrangement produces more homogenization than expected from those margins.

We next quantified **potential interspecific resource-sharing exposure**. A butterfly-pair × WGSRPD3 unit was counted when both butterflies were documented to use at least one exact same host species and that host occurred in the region. We compared native-only and contemporary counts, partitioned newly created shared-resource geography into within- and between-butterfly-family components, and for every butterfly × region resource opportunity counted how many other focal butterflies shared at least one exact host in that region. These metrics describe potential resource co-use, not realized competition: the data contain no local abundance, feeding intensity or demographic response.

Finally, we quantified structural redundancy of the added resource network. For each added butterfly × region unit we identified all introduced host species capable of generating that unit. We recorded the fraction supported by a single host and simulated removal of introduced-range contributions from hosts ranked by their fractional contribution to total added opportunity. Random host sets of the same size (499 draws) provided a descriptive benchmark. Because hosts were ranked using the observed contribution itself, targeted-versus-random differences are interpreted as network concentration and redundancy, not as an independent causal test or a site-specific management prescription (Supplementary Table S8).

### 2.4 Robustness analyses for host identity, specialization and taxonomic structure

We used matched-host nulls to test whether apparent host-identity effects could arise from host richness, plant-family composition, starting native range and the prominence of candidate plants in HOSTS. Because conclusions about host-identity excess were sensitive to how strongly network prominence was weighted, we do not interpret these nulls as identifying a butterfly-specific host-choice mechanism; the full parameter grid is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

We tested whether the host-family-breadth association with proportional expansion was sensitive to the finite 369-unit WGSRPD3 support, geographic composition and taxonomic non-independence. Post-hoc analyses progressively excluded broad native resource envelopes, repeated associations within dominant WGSRPD level-1 regions, fitted butterfly Family as a random intercept, and used the Kawahara et al. (2023) time-calibrated butterfly phylogeny for exact species matches. As a descriptive check on whether resource expansion was confined to one butterfly lineage, we also summarized the fraction of species with any introduced-host expansion within each LepTraits butterfly Family and interpreted only families represented by at least 10 focal species. For the phylogenetic sensitivity we report both a fixed Brownian model (lambda = 1) and a Pagel-lambda model in which residual phylogenetic covariance is estimated from the data. Because the estimated lambda was close to zero, we treat the estimated-lambda model as the data-adaptive phylogenetic correction and the fixed Brownian model as a boundary sensitivity rather than privileging lambda = 1.

An exploratory plant-level analysis related HOSTS consumer degree to plant geographic expansion across 8,909 HOSTS-WCVP plants (Supplementary Table S2). Because HOSTS includes moths as well as butterflies, this is a Lepidoptera-wide network measure rather than a butterfly-specific trait. We treat it only as a possible explanatory axis because degree may reflect ecological host prominence, study intensity, and reverse causation if introduced plants acquire additional consumer records after redistribution.

Within-butterfly portfolio-concentration metrics were retained only as a secondary structural diagnostic because effective contributor number and maximum single-host share are bounded by host number. This diagnostic is distinct from the across-plant concentration of total added opportunity described above. A separate European host-inventory sensitivity is reported in Supplementary Table S9.

### 2.5 Secondary occurrence validation

Butterfly occurrences for 2010–2026 were obtained from GBIF (GBIF.org 2026) for the separately assembled 32-species panel and mapped to WGSRPD3 units. We counted occurrence units outside each species' native host-resource envelope and asked how many entered the contemporary envelope after introduced host ranges were retained.

Two post-hoc nulls tested whether recovery exceeded geometric overlap expected from larger envelopes. The first preserved native-envelope size, the number of introduced-added units and the number of outside-native occurrence units, but placed added units uniformly among non-native WGSRPD3 units. The second additionally preserved the number of added units within each WGSRPD level-1 region. Aggregate null distributions used 199,999 hypergeometric draws. We then treated butterfly species as the replication unit: 99,999 region-matched null draws were used for the mean and median species-level recovery fractions, 50,000 species-cluster bootstrap resamples quantified uncertainty, and every informative species was removed in turn to assess leave-one-species-out sensitivity.

### 2.6 Secondary climate analysis

A separate pre-specified analysis tested whether broader host-family diets weaken climatic filtering within contemporary host-resource opportunity. Climate covariates were drawn from CHELSA (Karger et al. 2017, 2021), and twenty-four species passed the fixed quality criteria. Because this analysis addresses realization of opportunity rather than the paper's primary resource-redistribution question, its cross-fit design, geographic-matching sensitivities, effect-size precision and full results are reported in Supplementary Methods S1, Supplementary Table S5 and Fig. S1.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Plant globalization expands butterfly resource geography through uneven host contributions

Introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed resource opportunity for 206 of 239 species (86.2%) and increased aggregate butterfly × WGSRPD3 coverage from 26,530 to 41,083 units, an addition of 14,553 (+54.9%; Fig. 1a). Expansion occurred across the diet-breadth spectrum—84.5% of one-family species, 79.7% of two-family species, 88.0% of species using three to five families and all species using six or more families expanded—and affected 83.5–90.2% of species in each of the five major butterfly families represented by at least 10 species (Supplementary Table S4). Excluding FAO-defined crop hosts still yielded a 47.0% aggregate increase, or 46.0% under the conservative genus-expanded exclusion (Supplementary Table S7).

Added opportunity was strongly uneven across plants. Fractional attribution identified 670 contributing host species; the top 10 accounted for 25.1% of added units and only 38 species were needed to reach half. Collapsing hosts to genera retained the concentration: 25 of 431 genera accounted for half of all added opportunity (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table S6). The largest contributors included *Medicago sativa*, *Poa pratensis*, *Oxalis corniculata*, *Cynodon dactylon* and *Dactylis glomerata*. Twenty-six of the 38 half-credit hosts contributed opportunity to butterflies in at least two butterfly families, and 71.5% of the credit carried by those 38 hosts came from such cross-family resources.

Taxonomic diet breadth did not identify the main beneficiaries of this expansion. Host-family breadth was nearly unrelated to proportional gain (Spearman rho = 0.008; 49,999-bootstrap 95% CI -0.111 to 0.128; Fig. 1b). A strict post-hoc ±0.10 equivalence test was narrowly inconclusive (p = 0.078), so we treat the association as small and imprecisely bounded rather than exactly zero. Geographic, taxonomic, phylogenetic, finite-support and Poaceae-exclusion sensitivities did not reveal a consistent broad-generalist advantage (Supplementary Information).

### 3.2 Plant globalization homogenizes regional resource assemblages and butterfly resource geography

Regional butterfly resource assemblages became substantially more similar after introduced host ranges were retained (Fig. 3a). Across 62,835 pairs of 355 WGSRPD3 regions, mean Jaccard similarity increased from 0.277 under native host geography to 0.462 under contemporary host geography, a 66.8% increase; median similarity increased from 0.233 to 0.466.

Most of this raw convergence was expected from the large amount of resource opportunity added, but the observed arrangement of additions produced a further, statistically resolved excess. The fixed-margin null—which preserved every butterfly's number of added regions and every region's number of added butterfly opportunities—had a median mean similarity of 0.453 (95% interval 0.4533–0.4536). The observed 0.462 exceeded all but the add-one bound of 499 randomizations (p = 0.002). Thus redistribution homogenized regional resource opportunity primarily by adding widely distributed resources, with a modest additional convergence attributable to the particular spatial arrangement of those additions.

The same pattern appeared when the unit of comparison was the butterfly rather than the region (Fig. 3b). Mean Jaccard overlap among the geographic resource envelopes of all 28,441 butterfly pairs increased from 0.222 to 0.328 (+47.4%). A fixed-margin null gave a median of 0.321 (95% interval 0.3214–0.3216; p = 0.002). Plant globalization therefore expanded individual resource envelopes while simultaneously making butterfly resource geography more similar.

### 3.3 Introduced hosts expand shared-resource exposure and create a low-redundancy resource network

Geographic overlap in **exact shared hosts** increased even more strongly (Fig. 3c). Butterfly pairs that shared at least one known host generated 62,473 butterfly-pair × WGSRPD3 units under native host geography and 141,885 under contemporary geography, an increase of 127.1%. Of the 986 butterfly pairs with any exact shared-host relationship, 920 gained at least one new region of shared-resource geography. Introduced ranges created 79,412 novel shared-resource pair × region units; 41,543 (52.3%) occurred between butterflies from different families and 37,869 within families.

Newly added resource space was also more crowded in terms of potential exact-host co-use. A native butterfly × region resource opportunity shared at least one exact host with a mean of 4.71 other focal butterflies (median 3), whereas an introduced-added opportunity shared a host with a mean of 5.71 others (median 4). The fraction of opportunities with no other focal butterfly sharing an exact host fell from 18.3% in native resource space to 9.9% in introduced-added resource space. These values quantify potential interspecific resource exposure, not realized competition.

The added network was broad but often locally non-redundant. Of the 14,553 added butterfly × region units, 8,574 (58.9%) were supported by only one contributing introduced host, and the median number of contributing hosts per added unit was one. In a coarse global stress test, removing introduced-range contributions of the 38 hosts that together supplied half of fractional credit eliminated 5,812 added units (39.9%), affected 123 butterflies, caused 56 to lose at least half of their added opportunity and 16 to lose all of it. Random sets of 38 contributing hosts lost a median of 433 units (95% interval 169–1,124). Because the 38 hosts were selected by their observed contribution, this contrast is a description of network concentration and redundancy rather than an independent causal removal test (Supplementary Table S8).

### 3.4 Added resource geography aligns with contemporary butterfly occurrence

Thirty-one of 32 species in the separately assembled climate-stratified panel yielded contemporary GBIF records. Across the panel, 115 butterfly species × WGSRPD3 observations occurred outside native host-resource envelopes. Adding introduced host distributions recovered 66 of these units (57.4%; Fig. 2a).

Twenty-three species had at least one outside-native occurrence and 18 recovered at least one unit. Recovery exceeded geometric expectation at both pooled and species levels (Fig. 2b): a region-matched null had median aggregate recovery of 49 units (95% interval 43–56), compared with 66 observed, and mean species-level recovery was 0.583 versus a null median of 0.385 (95% interval 0.308–0.470), with no exceedance in 99,999 draws. Removing the largest contributor, *Pyrgus communis*, still left 44/93 units recovered versus a null median of 30; removing *Pieris brassicae* left 62/111 versus a null median of 46 (Supplementary Table S3).

The occurrence analysis does not establish that butterflies used introduced hosts locally or that host introduction caused colonization. It does show, however, that the resource geography created by introduced hosts aligns with contemporary butterfly presence more often than expected from structural overlap alone.

## 4. Discussion

Plant globalization changed both the **amount** and the **structure** of butterfly larval-resource space. At the species level, introduced host ranges added 54.9% more butterfly × region opportunity and expanded the resource envelopes of 86.2% of focal species. At the community level, the same redistribution increased similarity among regional resource assemblages by 66.8%, increased geographic overlap among butterfly resource envelopes by 47.4%, and more than doubled the geography in which butterfly pairs shared exact host species. A secondary occurrence analysis showed that this reconstructed opportunity is not purely cartographic: introduced-host geography recovered 57.4% of butterfly observations outside native host-resource envelopes. The ecological consequence of moving interaction partners is therefore two-sided: **resource release for individual consumers and resource homogenization among consumers and regions**.

### 4.1 Plant globalization expands resource space while reducing its geographic distinctiveness

Expansion itself is expected when introduced ranges are added to native host distributions; the non-trivial result is that expansion changed compositional structure. Regional resource assemblages moved from a mean Jaccard similarity of 0.277 to 0.462, while butterfly resource envelopes became 47.4% more similar to one another. The fixed-margin null shows that most of this raw homogenization follows from the scale and regional distribution of added opportunity: preserving exactly how many regions each butterfly gained and how many butterfly opportunities each region gained produced high expected similarity. Yet the observed regional similarity (0.462) still exceeded the null median (0.453), and butterfly resource-envelope overlap likewise exceeded its fixed-margin expectation. The extra effect is modest, but it demonstrates that the identity and arrangement of redistributed resources add convergence beyond expansion alone.

Plant-flora homogenization, particularly among geographically distant and climatically similar regions, is already established (Daru et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2021); interaction-network homogenization has also been documented in plant–frugivore systems (Fricke & Svenning 2020). The present contribution is narrower: projecting redistribution through fixed, documented butterfly–host links reveals changes in potential consumer-resource geography and exact shared-host exposure, without assuming that actual butterfly communities have homogenized. Here no new butterfly–host links were required: **existing interaction partners were moved**, and that alone made the geographic resource environments of consumers more alike. The mechanism therefore differs from interaction homogenization driven by the appearance of novel local links.

The contribution structure also has two scales. A small upper tier of plants generated much of the added area—38 hosts supplied half of all fractional credit—but homogenization was not confined to those hosts. Adding only the top 38 raised mean regional similarity from 0.277 to 0.406, while the remaining long tail alone raised it to 0.404. Thus the **magnitude** of expansion is concentrated, whereas **convergence** is distributed more broadly through the host network.

Taxonomic diet breadth is secondary to this structural result. Proportional expansion was nearly unrelated to host-family richness, and one-family specialists expanded almost as frequently as broad generalists. Globalization therefore does not simply add more opportunity to butterflies with more dietary options; it reorganizes opportunity according to the redistribution histories of the particular plants to which butterflies are connected.

### 4.2 Shared-resource geography links range opportunity to community assembly

The strongest change in potential interaction exposure occurred among butterflies that already shared host species. Exact shared-host butterfly-pair × region units increased by 127.1%, and 920 of 986 host-sharing pairs gained new shared-resource regions. More than half of the newly created shared-resource units joined butterflies from different families. Introduced-added resource opportunities were also associated with more other focal butterflies sharing an exact host than native opportunities (mean 5.71 versus 4.71; median 4 versus 3).

These patterns do **not** demonstrate stronger competition. Host sharing can generate at least two opposing ecological outcomes. First, a host acts as an environmental filter: butterflies using the same plant are more likely to find suitable resources in the same places. Consistent with this mechanism, Nakadai et al. (2018) found that host sharing among Japanese butterflies was associated more with geographic co-occurrence than with competitive exclusion. Second, when host biomass is locally limiting, increased co-use can increase the scope for interspecific resource competition. The same redistribution can therefore facilitate geographic co-occurrence while intensifying local interaction exposure.

This duality yields testable predictions. At the **intraspecific** level, establishment of a suitable introduced host should relax resource limitation and potentially increase carrying capacity or permit individuals to spread across additional host patches. At the **interspecific** level, the strongest changes in abundance or performance should occur where several host-sharing butterflies newly overlap on the same introduced resource and host supply is limiting. If resource filtering dominates, host-sharing species should colonize the same newly available regions without negative abundance responses; if competition dominates, abundance or larval performance should decline with increasing density of other consumers after controlling host quantity and quality. Local time series, larval abundance and host-biomass data can distinguish these alternatives; the global presence data used here cannot.

### 4.3 Resource-network fragility provides a conservation hypothesis, not a removal prescription

Plant redistribution may create new opportunity without creating equivalent redundancy. Nearly 59% of added butterfly × region units were supported by only one contributing introduced host. In the global stress test, removing the introduced-range contributions of the 38 highest-contributing hosts erased 39.9% of all added opportunity, caused 56 butterflies to lose at least half of their gain and eliminated all added opportunity for 16. This concentration makes a clear conservation prediction: abrupt loss or removal of a heavily used introduced host should matter most where consumer populations have come to depend on it and where alternative larval hosts are absent.

That prediction is not an argument for retaining invasive plants. A documented population of *Euphydryas editha* became dependent on introduced *Plantago lanceolata* and went locally extinct after grazing cessation changed its habitat, although ancestral *Collinsia* remained locally available (Singer & Parmesan 2018). Another population retained the ability to use ancestral hosts after adopting *Plantago* (Haan et al. 2021). Thus mapped host redundancy does not ensure functional fallback. Exotic hosts can also be poor-quality substitutes or ecological traps (Yoon & Read 2016; Braga 2023). The targeted-removal contrast is structural, not causal: hosts were ranked by contribution and consumer preferences were unobserved. Before invasive-plant removal, local management should test realized host use, development and usable native alternatives, restoring suitable replacements where needed.

The coarse WGSRPD3 resolution is appropriate for global biogeographic inference but not for site-level management. Regional dependency signals should therefore be treated as hypotheses for local validation. More generally, the results show why invasion impacts cannot be represented by a simple beneficial-versus-harmful classification: the same introduced host can increase geographic resource opportunity, homogenize consumer resource niches, alter potential competitor exposure and still have poor demographic quality.

### 4.4 Added resource geography is partially reflected in contemporary occurrence

More than half of butterfly species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes were brought inside the envelope when introduced host ranges were retained. The result survived species-level nulls and removal of the largest contributing species. This correspondence is consistent with some redistributed resources becoming ecologically relevant to contemporary butterfly geography, while leaving multiple filters between resource availability and realized distribution.

Dated introductions, locality-level host use, abundance and performance can test whether shared-resource geography produces colonization, coexistence, competition or traps.

## 5. Limitations

All resource-envelopes are potential rather than realized ecological niches. HOSTS is incomplete and geographically uneven, and a butterfly recorded on a host somewhere is not assumed to use that host in every region where the plant occurs. WCVP introduced status describes contemporary distribution status rather than the timing or pathway of introduction. We therefore infer changes in **resource opportunity and potential resource sharing**, not causal historical effects on butterfly colonization, abundance or fitness.

The homogenization and shared-resource analyses are post-hoc. WGSRPD3 is coarse, and ten regions that lacked any native focal-butterfly resource assemblage could not enter the paired regional-similarity comparison. The fixed-margin null deliberately preserves both butterfly-specific expansion and regional accumulation. Consequently, the large raw increase in Jaccard similarity should not be interpreted as an entirely host-identity-specific effect: the excess over fixed-margin expectation is statistically resolved but small. Its value is in showing that actual redistribution adds some convergence beyond the strong structural homogenization expected from widespread resource addition.

Potential resource-sharing exposure is not realized competition. The analysis lacks local host abundance, butterfly density, phenology, per-capita consumption, natural enemies and host quality. Shared host geography may promote co-occurrence through niche filtering, competition when resources are limiting, or both. The global removal scenarios likewise quantify network sensitivity, not demographic effects or site-specific management outcomes.

The secondary occurrence validation is presence-side and opportunistic. Its 32-species panel was constructed for a stratified climate test rather than specifically for occurrence validation; GBIF non-observation is not absence, and co-occurrence of a butterfly with an introduced known host does not prove local larval use. The panel includes species with non-native butterfly populations, including *Pieris brassicae* (Phillips et al. 2020); leave-one-out analyses did not change the qualitative conclusion.

Diet-breadth, phylogenetic, crop-exclusion, plant-prominence and climate analyses are retained as secondary robustness or boundary tests. The near-zero host-breadth slope does not prove exact equivalence, and network-wide HOSTS degree remains a joint proxy for ecological prominence and recording intensity. These limitations do not affect the arithmetic reconstruction of resource geography but constrain causal interpretation of why particular plants or butterflies contribute most strongly.

## 6. Conclusions

Plant globalization has not merely enlarged the map of potential larval resources for butterflies; it has changed the structure of that resource space. Across 239 species, introduced host ranges increased aggregate butterfly × region opportunity by 54.9%, raised similarity among regional resource assemblages by 66.8%, increased pairwise butterfly resource-envelope overlap by 47.4% and more than doubled the geography of exact shared-host exposure. Contemporary butterfly occurrences partially tracked this newly available geography.

The ecological consequence is a dual one. For individual species, redistributed hosts can relax geographic resource limitation. For communities, the same host movements homogenize resource environments and increase the places where consumers share resources. Thus global change can alter both niche opportunity and interaction exposure **without changing consumer diet identities**. Whether this produces coexistence, competition, demographic rescue or ecological traps depends on local abundance and host quality, but the large-scale reorganization of resource geography is already detectable.

## Data and Code Availability

For double-anonymous peer review, the analysis code, frozen scientific protocols, de-identified result receipts and figure-source files needed to reproduce the reported analyses are supplied with this submission as an anonymized supplementary review archive.

A separate anonymized Table S9 source-data and reproducibility addendum accompanies that archive, containing BCE/Clarke-derived candidate host-association records, WCVP taxon crosswalks and geographic/null-model sensitivity outputs. These are source-dependent literature summaries, not new independent field feeding observations.

The large external datasets are obtained from their original providers (LepTraits, HOSTS, WCVP, GBIF, CHELSA and WGSRPD) using the versions, identifiers and query rules described in the Methods. The secondary occurrence validation used 53,434 GBIF occurrence records. An exact-ID archival download requested those same records (https://doi.org/10.15468/dl.pp5nc9); GBIF returned 53,144 records, indicating that 290 records used in the frozen analysis were no longer returned by GBIF at archival download time.

A stable public archival snapshot of the analysis package will be deposited before publication; the public version of this statement will then cite that archive.
