## Working paper title and central question

**Recommended:** Ports, island floras and the redistribution of butterfly larval-host opportunities

**Alternative, emphasizing the new ecological insight:** Native-host scarcity amplifies potential butterfly resource gains from introduced plants on islands

**Question:** When human-associated plant redistribution changes the availability of existing larval hosts, why is the reconstructed relative resource gain so large on islands, how is plant supply related to ports and marine geography, and how much does that reconstructed potential correspond to independently recorded butterflies?

**Main finding:** The large island relative gain is **baseline-dependent**, not evidence for more efficient island gap filling or for new butterfly colonization. Present-day ports associate with introduced whole floras, and independent butterfly observation data contain a **small, geographically conditional** additional host signal.

4.2. **Plant supply is not transport inference**: ports, alien whole-flora and different marine-geographic filters; no evidence for causal ship or colonial-delivery path.

## One-paragraph original English abstract spine (edit to fit journal instructions)

Human redistribution of plants may reshape consumer resources without evolutionary changes in diet, yet the geographic opportunities and realized consumer responses are often conflated. We linked documented larval hosts of 239 butterfly species to native and contemporary vascular-plant distributions, comparing islands and mainlands and evaluating port-associated floristic assembly. Introduced hosts added 14,553 potential butterfly–botanical-region combinations (+54.9%). Relative gains were much greater on islands (+118.9%) than mainlands (+40.9%), largely because native-host coverage was lower on islands rather than because introductions filled a larger proportion of missing opportunities. Port-rich island regions had richer introduced floras, and introduced floras were compositionally more similar among islands than native floras. Independent butterfly records contained additional but much smaller predictive information from known-host geography, with geographically heterogeneous transfer and sensitivity to observation intensity. Independent observations documented that an island endemic butterfly can use an introduced plant as a larval host, while taxonomic coverage limits applying the global source-panel mean to island endemics. We conclude that plant redistribution changes the potential geography of butterfly resources, but neither contemporary port associations nor butterfly indexing establishes the historical transport and recruitment pathways responsible for island colonization.
