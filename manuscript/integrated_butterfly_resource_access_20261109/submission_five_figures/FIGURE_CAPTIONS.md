# Figure captions — five-main-figure candidate

These captions are transcribed from the author-local submission candidate whose document hashes are recorded in word_candidate_manifest.json.

Figure 1. Native-host scarcity amplifies proportional island resource gains. The fixed panel comprises 239 butterflies across 126 insular and 237 mainland WGSRPD3 botanical regions. (a) Native coverage: native-host-covered butterfly–region combinations divided by all evaluated combinations. (b) Gap filling: newly introduced-host-covered combinations divided by initially empty combinations. (c) Relative gain: the same additions divided by native-host-covered combinations. Introduced-only hosts supply 54.3% (5,741/10,570) of currently covered island combinations and 29.0% (8,686/29,901) of mainland combinations. After matching area and broad geography, 24.1% of island gaps and 22.9% of mainland gaps were filled. These are mapped opportunities, not observed butterfly distributions (Appendix S2).

Figure 2. Port-associated plant supply and contrasting island floras. (a) Coefficients of log₂(1 + port count) for log(1 + plant richness), adjusted for area and TDWG Level1 (126 botanical island regions). Whiskers show HC3 95% intervals; the directly paired introduced-minus-native contrast includes zero. (b) Median Jaccard similarity of native and introduced plant assemblages across 7,875 pairs of the same 126 regions (7,874 defined introduced-flora comparisons; one empty-union pair was excluded). These are contemporary floristic comparisons, not changes through time. See Appendix S3 and Figures S3.1–S3.2 for sensitivity analyses and marine geography.

Figure 3. Mainland isolation and mapped butterfly host resources. (a) Model-estimated numbers of butterflies with at least one native or any native/introduced known host at 100, 1,000 and 4,000 km (126 regions, 239 butterflies). Standardized distance slopes: native −0.1676 (nine-division clustered p = 0.014), contemporary +0.0192 (HC3 p = 0.751). (b) Introduced-only share at the same three distances, estimated in a separate log-odds model of 123 resource-active regions (β = +0.4251; clustered p = 0.014); these are not ratios of panel (a). Panels (a,b) show only reported point estimates, without prediction intervals or interpolated curves. (c) Native coverage at species, genus and family rank; filled circles and open squares show base and MEM10 models, with nine-Level1 clustered 95% intervals. Species-minus-family contrast −0.155 (p = 0.008; MEM10 plus clustering p = 0.059). (d) Paired contemporary-minus-native standardized slope difference and nine-division clustered 95% interval. Models adjust for ports, area, latitude and Level1 (Appendix S3). Higher-rank matches do not establish host suitability.

Figure 4. Resource gains are concentrated among host plants and uneven among butterflies. (a) Cumulative contributions of 670 plants to 14,553 additional butterfly–region combinations, dividing each addition equally among contributing hosts. The minimum number reaching half of additions is 38 (Gini = 0.7774). Points are reported top-k summaries; dotted segments are visual guides, not measurements of intermediate ranks. (b) Global regions with native hosts versus newly covered island regions for all 239 butterflies (Spearman ρ = 0.579; partial ρ = 0.357 after catalogue-depth controls). The broadest 59 and narrowest 60 butterflies account for 39.6% and 11.2% of island additions. (c) Pooled island gap-filling fractions by increasing native-resource extent: 644/7,221, 1,077/6,850, 1,749/6,322 and 2,271/4,892 (60, 60, 60 and 59 species). This is not proportional gain over native coverage or observed feeding (Appendices S2 and S4).

Figure 5. Recorded butterflies and mapped resource geography. Orange circles show introduced-only shares among recorded butterfly–region combinations with mapped hosts (224 shared butterflies, 84 active island regions, 2001–2024); symbol area increases with the number of recorded combinations. Solid and dashed curves are back-transformed adjusted log-odds fits for recorded and mapped-potential shares, respectively, on these same 84 regions, holding the other standardized covariates and division composition at their means. Distance coefficients are +0.439 (eight-division clustered p = 0.040) and +0.257 (p = 0.037). The pooled near/far quartile shares are 70/612 (11.4%) and 27/44 (61.4%), representing 32/32 and 12/32 regions. Stricter recording-effort analyses are uncertain (Appendix S5, Table S8). The curves do not establish occupancy, feeding, colonization or demographic effects.

## Supporting figures

Figure S3.1. Sensitivity of the direct port–origin contrast. The response is log(1 + introduced plant richness) − log(1 + native plant richness), with unstandardized log₂(1 + port count) as predictor. All models include log area and TDWG Level1. Geographic adjustment adds mainland distance and absolute latitude; effort adjustment adds indexed plant-record volume. Points and whiskers are estimates and nine-Level1 clustered 95% t intervals. Blue indicates 126 regions; orange indicates the common 98-region effort cohort. The five estimates are not independent replications; all intervals include zero.

Figure S3.2. Undirected marine electrical resistance and floristic prediction. The 104-region source comprises 5,355 island pairs. Relative MSE improvements beyond the original geographic/richness baseline are shown for one island held out and for both endpoints unseen (1,089 test pairs in five folds). Whiskers are descriptive resampling intervals; for the original one-island test the reported interval endpoints are approximate. In the strict test, both origins improved in three of five folds; the introduced-minus-native interval crossed zero. Resistance represents uniform-ocean geometry, not measured currents. A distinct shortest-water-path model provided information for both origins (Appendix S3).

Figure S5.1. Recorded community prediction and geographic transfer (224 butterflies). Relative held-out MSE improvement from adding documented host-flora similarity to a model already including other introduced flora. (a) Regional holdouts: the unthinned point has the descriptive regional bootstrap interval (Table S7); the thinned estimates have conditional Monte Carlo 2.5–97.5% quantiles across 29 draws. (b) Entirely unseen TDWG Level1 divisions; no interval is available for the unthinned average. (c) Within-South-America and cross-world dyads for the South American holdout, with conditional Monte Carlo quantiles. Positive values indicate improved prediction. Conditional simulation quantiles are not population confidence intervals.
