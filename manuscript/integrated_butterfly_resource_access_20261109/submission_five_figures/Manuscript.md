# Port-associated introduced floras reveal a weaker oceanic isolation gradient in mapped butterfly host resources

**Running title:** Island butterfly host resources

## Structured abstract

**Aim.** Oceanic distance separates islands, but does it still separate butterflies from familiar food plants? We contrasted native and introduced floras as reciprocal comparators of marine dispersal and port-associated supply.

**Location.** Global botanical regions and Macaronesian islands.

**Time period.** Native versus contemporary floras; butterfly records, 2001–2024.

**Major taxa studied.** 239 butterflies; separate 155- and 224-species recording cohorts.

**Methods.** Holding documented host identities fixed, we mapped native and contemporary host opportunities across 369 botanical regions. We compared isolation gradients across 126 islands, port associations, marine geography and dispersal morphology, and evaluated independent butterfly records.

**Results.** Native-host opportunities declined with isolation (β = −0.17), more strongly at species than family resolution; contemporary coverage was nearly flat (β = 0.02), with an uncertain slope contrast. Introduced plants alone supplied 54% of island opportunities, increasingly on remote islands. Among recorded butterfly–region combinations with mapped hosts, introduced-only shares increased with isolation (β = 0.44; nearest/farthest quartiles: 11%/61%, with sparse remote-region records), uncertain under stricter recording thresholds. Introduced floras were ten times as similar among islands and positively associated with ports; the origin contrast was uncertain. Native floras had more seawater-dispersal morphology. A small native baseline amplified island gains; broadly supplied butterflies filled more gaps than narrowly supplied ones (46% versus 9%). Thirty-eight plants supplied half the global additions. Monarchs were recorded in 20 of 42 island regions with only mapped introduced hosts; Canary-endemic *Pieris cheiranthi* larvae fed on introduced *Tropaeolum majus*.

**Main conclusions.** Geographic isolation and resource isolation can diverge when familiar hosts are redistributed. The shared, port-associated introduced layer narrows the mapped island–mainland resource gap, with unequal gains among butterflies. These patterns do not establish transport routes, establishment or demographic benefits.

**Keywords:** alien plants, butterfly–plant interactions, island biogeography, larval host plants, ports, resource geography, spatial prediction

## 1. Introduction

An ocean crossing can bring a butterfly to an island without bringing it closer to its food plants. Island isolation therefore has two meanings for a consumer: separation from populations and separation from familiar resources. Human movement has rearranged plant floras (Daru et al., 2021; van Kleunen et al., 2015), and global establishment histories of non-native moths and butterflies have now been assembled (Couto, Rebelo, Grosso-Silva, Cardoso, & Capinha, 2026). Even without a change in recorded diet, a butterfly's potential food geography can change. We ask whether native and introduced floristic layers uncouple physical isolation from mapped resource isolation.

Host identity matters alongside plant richness: remote islands may retain substantial floras but lack plants in a particular butterfly's diet. Introduced plants may fill some of these gaps. A large proportional gain can arise from a small native baseline or from more efficient gap filling. We distinguish these alternatives across places and across consumers, because geographic compensation need not distribute food opportunities evenly among butterfly species.

The sea can act both as a filter on natural dispersal and as the space through which human transport connects islands. Ports may mark a second geography of plant supply (Essl et al., 2019; Tordoni et al., 2017). The two floras provide reciprocal comparators: stronger seawater-related signatures are expected in native floras, and stronger port associations in introduced floras. Testing both geographical predictors against both origins can challenge pathway specificity, which fitting only the predicted association cannot do. We also ask whether port-associated floras contain familiar butterfly hosts. The comparisons concern contemporary geography, not observed seed transport.

A resource map is biologically meaningful only insofar as it relates to the consumer. Exotic butterfly host use is well documented (Graves & Shapiro, 2003), and host availability can accompany butterfly range changes (Halsch, Shapiro, Thorne, Waetjen, & Forister, 2020; Rashid et al., 2026). We therefore evaluate whether host geography adds information to predictions of independently recorded butterflies and examine named endemic larval interactions.

We test three linked questions. First, how do marine geography and ports relate to the native and introduced floristic layers, and does including introduced hosts weaken the isolation gradient? Second, which plant identities and butterfly resource baselines contribute to the gains, and are those plants represented near ports? Third, do the reconstructed resource maps correspond to independently recorded butterflies? The study holds recorded diet fixed to distinguish redistribution of known resources from changes in host use.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Distinct butterfly and botanical samples

We matched fixed butterfly larval-host identities in the Natural History Museum HOSTS catalogue (Robinson, Ackery, Kitching, Beccaloni, & Hernández, 2023) to accepted native and introduced botanical distributions from WCVP v13 (21 May 2024; Govaerts, 2024). The global potential-resource reconstruction included 239 source-eligible butterflies × 369 WGSRPD3 botanical regions (Brummitt, 2001). The original island–mainland contrast used 126 strictly insular and 237 mainland botanical regions, with mixed or other classifications kept separate; a botanical region is not necessarily one physical island. Whole-flora and port contrasts used identical sets of 126 botanical islands, with a source-matched coastal subset of 104 regions for marine geometry. Independent observational tests had different populations: 155 butterflies in a later-record indexing forecast and 224 butterflies × 169 WGSRPD3 regions (14,196 pairwise combinations) in a separate original GBIF-assemblage model. A 225-candidate × 51-landmass island-index sample and source-defined endemic checklists provided contextual evidence only. Cohorts, source taxonomic crosswalks, original geographic units and licences are detailed in Appendix S1 and the reproducibility information in Appendix S8.

### 2.2. Potential butterfly resource coverage and island baseline accounting

For each butterfly × botanical-region combination, native resource coverage required at least one of its documented larval hosts to occur as a WCVP-native plant in that region. Contemporary coverage used the **same host identities** and additionally counted introduced ranges of those hosts. We defined newly supplied region combinations as *potential* additional larval food opportunities, not newly evolved host preferences, observed feeding, insect occupancy or increased abundance. We recorded total additions and their concentration among butterfly species, host plants and host-family breadth classes; crop-host and other original source sensitivities were retained.

For each geography class, let T be the number of evaluated butterfly–region combinations, N the number supported by a native known host and A the number newly supported by an introduced known host. Native coverage is B=N/T, gap-filling fraction is F=A/(T−N), and proportional expansion is A/N=F(1−B)/B. This identity separates *low initial native-host coverage* from *efficient filling of missing opportunities*. We compared the original 126 island and 237 mainland region populations within 15 shared broad geography × area-overlap strata and retained species-equal and fixed whole-region permutation checks. This comparison describes reconstructed resource geography, not a causal introduced-plant intervention.

### 2.3. Port associations, mainland isolation and island-flora layers

For 126 botanical islands, original WPI port counts (National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, 2019) were related to log1p native and introduced whole-flora richness, controlling for log area and broad botanical divisions (TDWG Level1). We estimated heteroskedasticity-robust (HC3) uncertainty and paired origin contrasts, with botanical-record and port-positive sensitivities. Native and introduced composition was compared using the Jaccard index, the proportion of species shared out of the combined species pool, across 7,875 island pairs (7,874 defined introduced-flora comparisons; one empty-union pair was excluded).

In a separate fixed-239-butterfly analysis of the same 126 islands, approximate isolation was the shortest WGS84 endpoint distance from an island WGSRPD3 polygon to the union of 237 mainland botanical-region polygons, with nearest points selected in longitude–latitude space. Standardized log1p plant richness or butterfly species counts with at least one mapped native or contemporary known host were regressed on standardized log2(1+ports), log1p(isolation distance), log area, absolute latitude and Level1 effects. Paired standardized slopes were contrasted; introduced-only host coverage used log((newly supplied+0.5)/(native-covered+0.5)) for 123 active islands. HC3, nine-Level1 clustered t8 intervals, within-Level1 bootstrap and geographical exclusions assessed robustness. Residual spatial autocorrelation was evaluated using a 2,000-km neighbour graph and Moran's I, followed by ten geography-selected positive Moran eigenvectors (MEM10) as an additional sensitivity. We also contrasted coverage at host species, genus and family ranks and tested native floristic composition (Appendix S3). Higher-rank presence is not demonstrated host suitability.

Separate surveys covered 1,596 port buffers and 38 early-record buffers; 104-island undirected ocean circuits and shortest-water paths, using GSHHG coastlines (Wessel & Smith, 2017), modelled plant similarity. On 5,355 common island pairs, crossed models added shortest-water geometry and port-count products in both orders to the same geographic/richness baseline for each origin, including strict both-endpoints-unseen validation. EuDiS morphology (22 islands; Vargas, Heleno, & Costa, 2023), Tonga dispersal categories (Fall & Drezner, 2011), AIS and OSCAR (ESR, 2009) sources remained distinct transport proxies, not historical ship or current observations. On the 98 islands with botanical indexing-volume data, we also fitted separate introduced-flora and documented introduced-host richness models with area, native baseline, recording volume and Level1 controls, using 4,999 stratified port-label permutations. A host model further conditioned on total introduced richness. Shared-source port–resource comparisons are reported separately as exploratory diagnostics (Appendices S3 and S7).

### 2.4. Plant identities and potential resource geography

We ranked plant species by their contributions to the additional fixed-HOSTS butterfly–region resource units, quantified concentration and related the *geographic breadth of native known-host resources* to absolute island additions by butterfly species. This geographical extent is not necessarily dietary family breadth or butterfly movement ability. Additional exploratory predictions of *potential consumer-resource assemblage* similarity used WCVP introduced whole flora, H plants registered as hosts for the fixed butterfly panel and O introduced plants not registered as hosts for those butterflies. Because **both the H predictor and potential consumer response use the same HOSTS×WCVP records**, these source-coupled results, their matched-family/occurrence negative controls and exact resource-margin swapping nulls are supplementary rather than tests of independently realized butterfly ecology.

### 2.5. Separate independent GBIF butterfly observation endpoints

The first original independent recording analysis used **155 butterfly species** from the original GBIF download (GBIF.org, 2026) and **208,482 evaluated butterfly×cell combinations** to compare retrospective GBIF-record indexing predictions using geography, earlier records, climate and land cover against models adding known larval-host geography and host-family composition. Species-equal mean squared error (MSE) was evaluated on later recordings, spatial blocks and a further subset at least 100 km from training data. Plant distributions were contemporary and not independently dated to the start of the evaluated butterfly-record period, so this was not a historically frozen forecast.

The separate *recorded butterfly community* analysis used the same GBIF download, DOI **10.15468/dl.umqmsf** (GBIF.org, 2026) (original source 14,637,152 occurrence rows in 2001–2024). Original counts were aggregated by 224 indexed species, WGSRPD3 region and year, with regional observation-activity proxies. The original effort-supported cohort comprised **169 regions / 14,196 dependent region pairs**. The response was the Jaccard similarity of butterfly species indexed during 2018–2024; controls included prior 2001–2010 recorded similarity, geography, climate, native potential host resources, whole introduced flora, historical exposure and effort/record-count proxies. Model M2 additionally included introduced plants **not catalogued** as hosts of any focal butterfly (O); M3 added the *documented* larval-host alien plant similarity (H). O is not experimentally established non-host flora. The original Ridge used lambda 10, scaling only on source training pairs and Jaccard prediction clipping to [0,1]. The H-after-O measure was the percentage reduction in held-region mean MSE from M2 to M3.

We reproduced all source-original model folds and regional butterfly Jaccards before running post hoc **observation-intensity** sensitivities. Each fixed region's prior and late butterfly records were probabilistically thinned to the smaller total period-wide source activity, once using the datasetKey×calendar-day proxy and separately using recorded butterfly-occurrence counts. For each of the **29** simulation draws per proxy, **both** the original prior butterfly similarity predictor and the later butterfly similarity outcome were recomputed; other retrospective source covariates, including the original late effort and recorded counts, remained fixed. The original 169 region-holdouts and strict transfer tests holding out each **entire TDWG Level1 division** were rerun. The simulations share one GBIF source and cannot be interpreted as independent field populations or a detection-corrected occupancy model. All additional source replay, Monte Carlo and South American cross-world calibration diagnostics are detailed in the corresponding main source reports and the South America failure diagnostic.

An exploratory island-level analysis joined all 224 indexed butterfly species to their native and introduced-only host-opportunity categories in the original 126 island regions. We modeled the log odds of introduced-only versus native-host coverage among recorded butterflies against mainland isolation, controlling for area, ports, latitude, botanical division and recording activity. We examined stricter effort thresholds, recording in multiple years and exclusion of the monarch (Appendix S5).

### 2.6. Independently documented island-endemic larvae and historic-index safeguards

The independent Macaronesian source checklist contained **41 living butterfly taxa, including 18 endemic species** (42 named taxa including one historical extinction). We audited accepted taxonomic synonyms, distinguished these species from the original 239-butterfly panel, and identified eight source-selected endemics with species-level documented larval host plants for a nonrepresentative separate WCVP reconstruction. Independently documented larval observations were checked for accepted plant identity, regional native/introduced status, wild versus garden context and observed use. In particular, original images document Canary-endemic *Pieris cheiranthi* larvae feeding on introduced *Tropaeolum majus* in Tenerife and La Palma. This supports individual interaction use, not additional butterfly populations. Historical butterfly first-index sources for the separate 225×51 island panel were tested against older records; negative first-index controls remain in Appendix S7. Full evidence and limitations appear in the endemic source-audit methods.

Supporting methods, results and reproducibility information are provided in Appendices S1–S8.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. A small native resource base amplifies island gains

Introduced plants substantially changed the potential food geography of island butterflies. Across the 126 insular regions, introduced plants supplied 5,741 newly covered butterfly–region combinations, accounting for 54.3% of all currently covered combinations. Potential resource coverage more than doubled on islands (+119%), compared with a 41% increase on mainlands (Figure 1). Across all 369 botanical regions, 206 of 239 butterflies gained at least one potential host region, adding 14,553 opportunities in total (+55%).

The larger relative gain on islands arose from a smaller native resource base. Native hosts covered only 16% of possible butterfly–region combinations on islands, compared with 37% on mainlands. Introduced plants filled similar fractions of the initial gaps in both settings (23% and 25%, respectively). The island–mainland coverage gap therefore narrowed, but islands did not fill their resource gaps more efficiently. The matched geographic and area comparisons supported this baseline interpretation (Appendix S2).

### 3.2. A port-associated introduced layer accompanies the geographically filtered native flora

Introduced floras were about ten times as similar among islands as native floras (Figure 2b). Islands with more ports tended to have more introduced plant species (β = 0.29), while the estimate for native richness was smaller and uncertain (β = 0.10; Figure 2a). The directly paired origin contrast was uncertain. In the separate standardized model controlling also for mainland isolation and latitude, the introduced-flora port coefficient was +0.35 (nine-division p = 0.049) and remained positive under MEM10 (+0.39; HC3 p = 0.006). On the 98 islands with plant-indexing-volume data, the port association remained positive for total introduced flora (β = 0.24, permutation p = 0.0012) and distinct documented introduced host taxa (β = 0.16, p = 0.011). The introduced-to-native richness log ratio also increased with ports (β = 0.24, permutation p = 0.0036), although nine-division clustered inference remained uncertain with effort and geographic controls (β = 0.25, p = 0.090; Figure S3.1). The host response did not retain an additional positive port association after conditioning on whole introduced flora (β = −0.04, p = 0.093; Appendix S3). This supports a broader port-associated plant supply rather than selective enrichment of butterfly hosts.

Marine geography and dispersal morphology provided additional context for the two layers (Figure S3.2; Appendix S3). Native floras contained a three-percentage-point higher share of plants with seawater-dispersal morphology across 22 islands. Undirected marine resistance improved introduced-flora similarity predictions by 4.2%, whereas the native-flora improvement was near zero. The contrast was less certain for two unseen island endpoints. Crossed models instead showed shared shortest-water-path information: added after ports, predictive gains were 2.23% for native and 1.89% for introduced flora. Ports added after water geometry yielded −0.08% and +0.16%, respectively, without resolved positive introduced-flora improvement. Paired resampling intervals for both origin contrasts crossed zero; strict unseen-endpoint validation also left the geometry gains uncertain. NOAA and HYCOM directional-current diagnostics added no direction-specific support (Appendix S3).

Native-host opportunities declined with mainland isolation, whereas the contemporary host map showed a nearly flat gradient (Figure 3a; standardized β = −0.17 and 0.02, respectively). Native plant richness showed an uncertain relationship with distance. Documented host identities declined more strongly than host-family presence (Figure 3c; paired standardized slope difference −0.15; nine-region p = 0.008; with MEM10 p = 0.059). Native species belonging to documented host families also declined proportionally (Appendix S3). The paired change in host-resource slopes (Figure 3d) was positive but uncertain when estimated across nine broad botanical divisions (Appendix S3).

Introduced hosts accounted for a greater share of mapped resources on more remote islands (Figure 3b). The fitted introduced-only share rose from 55% at 100 km from the mainland to 72% at 4,000 km. The negative native-host slope and the positive introduced-only share gradient persisted in spatial-filtered models and in a subset controlling for botanical recording volume (Appendix S3). These complementary results support a contrast between the native and contemporary maps without establishing complete removal of the isolation effect.

### 3.3. Geographic compensation does not distribute gains evenly among butterflies

A small fraction of introduced plants accounted for much of the change in consumer resources. Just 38 of the 670 contributing host species supplied half the additional global opportunities (Figure 4a). Concentration persisted when contributors were grouped by genus, and excluding crop hosts retained a substantial global resource increase (46–47%). Eight of the ten largest contributors were recorded near ports in the historical botanical sample (Appendix S7). Those eight taxa accounted for 20.7% of global additions; this is their global contribution, not the fraction transported through sampled ports.

Unlike the island–mainland contrast, butterflies with broader global native-resource geography filled more of their initially empty island opportunities: 46.4% in the broadest group versus 8.9% in the narrowest (Figure 4c). They accounted for 40% versus 11% of island additions (Figure 4b), even though proportional gains relative to native coverage were larger in the narrowest group. The association with absolute additions (Spearman ρ = 0.58) persisted after adjusting documented host species, families and HOSTS row counts (partial ρ = 0.36; Appendix S4). Gains nevertheless reached 82 of 97 one-host-family butterflies; dietary family breadth scarcely predicted proportional expansion (Appendix S2).

Port inventories linked these gains to recorded candidate plants. Across 1,596 completed port queries in 142 country codes, 1,150 contained at least one regionally introduced known-host candidate, comprising 687 plant species and 25,742 plant–port combinations. This inventory documents the presence of familiar host candidates near ports, not their selective enrichment or transport. Shared-source cross-butterfly correlations are exploratory (Appendix S7).

The change in potential resource composition was more pronounced among islands than among mainlands, but nearly all of that difference was accounted for by the fixed resource margins in the null model. Comparisons with nonlisted host plants likewise showed that part of the predicted resource similarity follows from the shared plant and host records used to construct the maps (Appendices S2 and S4).

### 3.4. Independently recorded butterflies also reflect resource isolation

Among 1,171 recorded butterfly–island combinations for the 224 shared species, 193 (16.5%) across 69 island regions had mapped introduced hosts but no mapped native host; 143 were recorded in multiple years. Another 62 lacked any mapped host. Among recorded butterflies with mapped hosts, the introduced-only share increased with mainland isolation (β = 0.44; eight-division clustered p = 0.040; 84 regions), but was uncertain under stricter recording-activity thresholds (Appendix S5). The unadjusted introduced-only shares among records with mapped hosts were 11.4% (70/612) and 61.4% (27/44) in the nearest and farthest distance quartiles; only 12 of 32 far regions contributed records, versus all 32 near regions (Figure 5). These are contemporary host-map associations, not observations of feeding or establishment.

The host maps also contained information about where butterflies were later recorded. For the independent 155-species sample, adding host geography and host-family information to models of climate, land cover, geography and earlier records reduced prediction error by 4.1% in the temporal test and 3.0% in the spatial-block test. The gain remained 2.74% in cells at least 100 km from training data (Appendix S5), but was not supported for butterfly species absent from model training.

The host signal was much smaller in the separate 224-species assemblage analysis. After accounting for the wider flora and earlier butterfly composition, documented introduced-host similarity improved regional prediction by less than 1%; the positive increment persisted under two recording-intensity matching procedures (Figure S5.1a). Average gains in tests holding out entire botanical divisions were also less than 1%, and improvement varied among divisions (Figure S5.1b).

South America revealed a limit to transferring the host signal across regions. Adding host information worsened predictions for comparisons between South America and the rest of the world by about 3–4% under the two recording-intensity procedures (Figure S5.1c). The baseline model already overpredicted similarity for these pairs, and host information increased that overprediction. The overall recorded-assemblage similarity increase was also sensitive to recording intensity (Appendix S5).

Locality-linked HOSTS reports more often involved introduced hosts on islands than mainlands (median 42.4% versus 9.1%), but no additional island excess was detected after supply adjustment (Appendix S7). The monarch (Danaus plexippus) was recorded in 20 of 42 regions with only mapped introduced hosts, including 18 regions with records in multiple years (Appendix S5). Introduced hosts can enter actual endemic feeding relationships. Independent photographs documented *Pieris cheiranthi* larvae feeding on introduced *Tropaeolum majus* on Tenerife and La Palma. The broader resource panel did not represent these endemics: of 41 living Macaronesian butterfly taxa, 18 are endemic, and none of those 18 occurred in the global 239-species panel. A separate reconstruction for eight endemics with documented named hosts found gains in global host geography for some species, but no additional home-archipelago opportunity under the available host lists (Appendix S6).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Geography and consumer identity shape different dimensions of resource gain

The same redistribution produces two different asymmetries. Islands gain proportionately more than mainlands because their native resource baseline is small, not because a greater fraction of gaps is filled. Among butterflies, however, species with wider native-resource geography fill a larger fraction of their island gaps. Geographic compensation can therefore coexist with unequal absolute gains among consumers.

Classical island biogeography emphasizes area, isolation and colonization–extinction dynamics (MacArthur & Wilson, 1967); introduced plants can accumulate alongside native floras (Sax & Gaines, 2008). Our consumer perspective asks how much mapped food access is supplied by introduced plants given the initial distribution of interaction partners.

### 4.2. Two floristic layers connect oceanic isolation and human connectivity

The negative isolation gradient evident in native-host resources was nearly flat when introduced hosts were included. The increasing introduced-only share with isolation provides complementary evidence that distant islands draw more of their mapped food opportunities from redistributed plants. Thus geographical separation from the mainland and separation from potential resources can diverge. The exact magnitude of the slope change remains uncertain under the conservative broad-region comparison.

Taxonomic resolution matters: remote floras retained many host families but fewer documented host identities, alongside reduced representation of their constituent plant species. Adjustment for restricted native ranges attenuated the species-to-genus contrast. This pattern extends floristic disharmony to documented interaction partners without establishing that unlisted native plants are unsuitable. For example, the Canary endemic *Pieris cheiranthi* has a literature-reported native host, *Crambe strigosa*, in its primary laurel-forest habitat (Acosta-Fernández, 2009). Narrowly distributed native hosts may be overlooked when geographically broad host catalogues dominate resource reconstruction. Identifying such interactions and testing larval performance on local native alternatives would help distinguish genuinely missing resources from missing documentation and refine island-specific conservation assessments.

The sea has a dual geographical role. Native floras retain a dispersal-morphology signal, whereas introduced floras are more widely shared and associated with ports. Together, these patterns support a geographically filtered native resource base and a human-associated supply layer (Daru et al., 2021; van Kleunen et al., 2015). Reciprocal comparisons also challenge an exclusive two-pathway explanation: shortest-water geometry informs both floras, port counts do not independently resolve pairwise similarity, and directional-current diagnostics lack robust support. Neither origin is a strict negative control, because natural and human transport can overlap. The port-richness association remains ecologically relevant: the introduced flora contains familiar larval hosts, without evidence of additional host-specific enrichment after conditioning on whole introduced richness. Increasing introduced-only resource share with mainland isolation connects this second layer to potential consumer resources; it does not establish that ports mediate the gradient.

### 4.3. General plant supply is translated into consumer-specific opportunities

Broad plant redistribution need not select butterfly hosts specifically to have selective consequences for consumers. Only 38 host plants account for half of added opportunities, several were recorded at island ports before 2011, and butterflies with broader native-resource geography filled more island gaps. Their advantage is consistent with the widespread geography of their documented hosts, including those found near ports, rather than simply recorded diet breadth. These shared-source patterns are not independent transport evidence. Relative and absolute gains differ: the narrowest resource-geography group gains proportionately more, despite filling fewer gaps. Thus narrowing a geographic resource gap does not imply equalization across consumers.

This is a potential-resource counterpart to nonrandom winners and losers under global change (McKinney & Lockwood, 1999). A recent cross-taxon analysis found that initially localized species can drive observed homogenization or differentiation (Hatfield, Gordon, Beale, & Thomas, 2026). Our measure instead concerns newly mapped food opportunities. The distinction matters because the identities of species with the greatest resource gains need not coincide with the identities of species driving changes in community composition. Testing this requires observations of host abundance, larval survival and consumer population dynamics.

### 4.4. Host resources are informative, but consumer responses remain conditional

Host geography improved predictions beyond environmental predictors and earlier records, although weaker assemblage gains and adverse South American transfer show that its relevance varies geographically.

The monarch, *Danaus plexippus*, illustrates a historical expansion of a consumer represented in our fixed panel. Its mapped island opportunities comprise 22 regions with native hosts and 42 additional regions with introduced hosts only; it was recorded in 20 of the latter, including 18 with records in multiple years (Appendix S5). Nineteenth-century accounts reported monarch appearance following milkweed introduction in Hawaii (Scudder, 1875), and genetic evidence supports stepwise Pacific dispersal, including expansion from Hawaii to the Marianas (Hemstrom, Freedman, Zalucki, Ramírez, & Miller, 2022). These observations are consistent with a resource constraint on expansion, but do not isolate the contribution of host introduction from butterfly dispersal or transport. By contrast, *Pieris cheiranthi* larvae feeding on introduced *Tropaeolum majus* illustrate an additional realized feeding interaction in a native endemic rather than colonization of a new island. Whether additional hosts translate into recruitment depends on plant quality, phenology, local habitat and natural enemies (Braga, 2023; Yoon & Read, 2016). Such filters may help explain why potential resource opportunity is much broader than the demonstrated changes in recorded butterfly distributions.

### 4.5. Conservation: manage introduced host plants with consumer interactions in view

Ports and their surroundings offer practical locations to screen for redistributed host candidates, alongside surveys of native habitats. Removing an introduced plant can remove a candidate larval food source from an island. This possibility is especially relevant on islands where native-host coverage is sparse and introduced plants supply much of the current mapped food opportunity. The named endemic larval interactions illustrate why evaluating plants in isolation can miss consequences for native consumers.

The idea of novel ecosystems emphasizes that human introductions create persistent ecological combinations that complicate management decisions (Hobbs et al., 2006). Our resource mapping offers a screening tool: identify consumers whose documented hosts occur only as introduced plants, then establish whether native alternatives, local host use, larval survival and seasonal food availability support their populations. Such evidence should guide assessment of possible resource loss alongside invasion impacts, including the risk that an introduced host acts as an ecological trap.

## 5. Limitations

**Potential resources and catalogue coverage.** A mapped host establishes regional opportunity, not local abundance, accessibility, host quality, successful development or demographic dependence. Unrecorded island-specific host interactions may be missed, particularly for narrowly distributed native plants. Higher-rank co-occurrence cannot distinguish genuine incompatibility from incomplete documentation. Both axes in Figure 4b share HOSTS/WCVP inputs; row-depth controls are not independent sampling-effort measures. Phylogenetic dependence and species selection remain. The separate 155- and 224-butterfly samples and independently enumerated endemics cannot be pooled with the 239-species resource panel to infer a causal process.

**Isolation and transport.** Nine broad geographic clusters limit precision in the paired native–contemporary slope difference. A near-zero contemporary slope is not proof of exact absence of isolation, and an uncertain native plant-richness slope does not establish a difference in slope relative to host opportunity. Similarly, the native–introduced port slope difference has an interval spanning zero. Modern port counts covary with island area and do not measure historical cargo or seed arrivals. Port buffers lack matched nonport controls, can overlap and have uneven recording; the cross-butterfly port association shares the original host catalogue and contemporary origin assignments. The sea resistance model represents undirected geometry, not currents; stricter geographic transfer is uncertain. Dispersal morphology is recorded for a limited, European-biased plant sample, and the exploratory directional-current diagnostic offers no direction-specific support. Botanical recording proxies omit some islands. The present-day native and introduced maps are not a historical before–after comparison.

**Consumer observations.** GBIF supplies opportunistic presence records rather than standardized monitoring or validated absences. The models use contemporary plant maps, which were not independently dated before the butterfly evaluation period. Recording-intensity matching assumes activity proxies rather than known detection probabilities; repeated draws reuse one archive and their intervals describe conditional simulation variation. The added-host predictor is not an isolated estimate of introduced-host causation. Historical first-index controls found older butterfly records for many apparently new island records (Appendix S7). The endemic feeding evidence verifies particular interactions, not dependence on introduced plants or population benefit.

## 6. Conclusions

Physical island isolation and mapped consumer-resource isolation can diverge. Native-host resources decline with mainland distance, whereas a widely shared, port-associated introduced plant pool supplies a larger fraction of opportunities on remote islands. Reciprocal origin comparisons constrain, rather than establish, separate transport pathways. The resulting resource gains compensate geographically for a small island baseline but differ markedly among butterfly species. This connects island biogeography to the geography of interaction partners, not just counts of species. Independent butterfly records and documented larval use support the relevance of these maps; field evidence is needed to establish recruitment and population consequences. Invasion management should consider which consumers use influential host plants and whether native alternatives exist.

## Data and Code Availability Statement

Butterfly–host identities derive from the Natural History Museum HOSTS catalogue (DOI 10.5519/havt50xw; source mirror commit 808e0b869f9ec1adf8efff87cf6a395adda103e0), and plant distributions from WCVP v13, 21 May 2024 (DOI 10.34885/0yex-xv26) through the source-locked rWCVPdata commit 65bed76bae9d644ccb6ad200c05f9f5071d89e05. WGSRPD3 geometry follows the published scheme (Brummitt, 2001), with the polygon source pinned to tdwg/wgsrpd commit 52da7828aba9d461dd133c27b3bd7a4407161f54. The original NHM HOSTS dataset is licensed CC0, and the archived Kew WCVP v13 dataset CC BY 3.0; redistribution of any separate third-party material remains subject to its own terms. The 155-species forecast and 224-species community analysis share the original GBIF occurrence download, DOI 10.15468/dl.umqmsf (14,637,152 original records), while evaluating different cohorts and outcomes. The 224-species annual regional observations and effort counts are retained with version identifiers and verified checksums. The privately recovered 155-species forecast panel supported verified temporal and spatial-block refits and remains outside the public archive. The 225-butterfly×51-landmass reindex and 1,596-port query sample retain separate acquisition receipts but still require completed datasetKey-level attribution or their own suitable archived GBIF/derived dataset DOI. The older 10.15468/dl.pp5nc9 applies to a separate 32-butterfly pilot, not the above analyses. The original historical 83,573-row butterfly–island–year author Parquet and some occurrence-level inputs are not included in the present source archive. The National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency published the 27th edition of the World Port Index (Publication 150) in 2019. Study GeoJSON coordinates match the 2019 comparison mirror to six decimal places. The GeoJSON lacks edition metadata, and its original download and conversion remain unverified.

The underlying derived tables, model outputs and figure inputs are documented in Appendix S8 and the accompanying reproducibility records. The historical GBIF acquisition sources and port inventories have unresolved occurrence-level attribution and access requirements; these are distinct from the GBIF download cited above. A complete publicly accessible analysis archive has not yet been deposited.
