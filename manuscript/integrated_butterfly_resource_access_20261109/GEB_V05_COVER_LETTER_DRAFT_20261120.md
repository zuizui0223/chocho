**Why this paper matters to GEB readers (editorial significance paragraph; fewer than 250 words):**

Human-mediated plant introductions are changing not only regional floras but also the geography of food resources available to consumers whose documented diets have not changed. We reconstruct the potential locations of known larval-host plants for 239 butterflies across the world's botanical regions and show that introduced hosts add 14,553 butterfly–region opportunities (+54.9%). Relative gains are particularly large on sampled botanical islands (+118.9% versus +40.9% on mainlands), but our analysis reveals a less obvious reason: islands begin with a smaller mapped native-host baseline, rather than filling a larger fraction of initially empty resource opportunities. Just 38 of 670 introduced host taxa account for half of the global additions, and butterfly species differ markedly in how many island opportunities they receive. The introduced botanical layer is more similar among islands and associated with port infrastructure; sea-related geographic descriptors also provide information about its distribution. Independent butterfly records and a documented island-endemic larval interaction connect this plant-based opportunity to separately observed consumers, while revealing important geographic limits. Our study offers a general, quantitatively defined consumer-resource perspective on island biogeography and human plant redistribution without confusing mapped food opportunity with demonstrated colonization or population benefits.

The manuscript's primary advance is **resource geography with fixed documented host identities**. It is distinct from analyses of established non-native Lepidoptera and observed community homogenization, and is relevant to general ecological consequences of species redistribution. The four figures bring together baseline dependence, contrasting native/introduced floras, unequal plant–butterfly partner contributions and independent consumer observations.
