# Human-redistributed larval hosts expand butterfly resource geography across diets and islands

## Structured abstract

**Aim.** Human activities redistribute the plants on which butterflies depend, potentially changing the geography of larval resources even when butterflies retain the same host lists. We asked which butterflies gain new resource opportunities, whether gains differ between islands and mainland regions, and how potential resources relate to the geography of recorded butterflies and marine access.

**Location.** Worldwide botanical regions, complemented by a separately sourced 51-landmass island and port panel.

**Time period.** Native and contemporary plant distributions; retrospective butterfly-record analyses spanning 2001–2022, with a separate recent occurrence-prediction evaluation.

**Major taxa studied.** Butterflies and their larval host plants.

**Methods.** We reconstructed native-only and contemporary potential larval-host resource geography for 239 butterflies, holding recorded host identities fixed. We assessed diet breadth, insularity, and the contributions of redistributed hosts, then examined port-associated floras and butterfly occurrences. Separate 155-species predictions and a fixed 225-butterfly by 51-landmass recording panel evaluated the observational correspondence of resources and access; species-, island- and region-constrained tests assessed spatial structure.

**Results.** Introduced host distributions expanded potential resource geography for 206/239 butterflies, increasing butterfly–region combinations by 54.9%. Expansion was widespread among narrow- and broad-diet butterflies and was concentrated in a minority of host plants. Potential opportunity increased by 118.9% in insular versus 40.9% in mainland regions, with a 10.7-percentage-point matched insular excess in added-resource share. Port-rich regions contained more introduced flora, including larval hosts, without a further positive host-specific enrichment. Host information improved retrospective butterfly-record prediction, and later island first-index records were spatially associated with previously indexed conspecific islands. Ocean-resistance and wing-size effects were less transferable across regions.

**Main conclusions.** Global plant redistribution reorganizes the potential resource geography of butterflies across diet breadths, especially on islands. The geography of recorded butterflies retains additional spatial structure that cannot yet be interpreted as verified host-facilitated colonisation or shipborne transport. Butterfly conservation and biogeography therefore require distinguishing taxonomic diet breadth, partner geography and access.

**Keywords:** anthropogenic biotic redistribution; butterfly biogeography; ecological interactions; host plants; insularity; macroecology; marine barriers; naturalised plants; specialization

## 1. Introduction

How specialized a butterfly is and where it can find its larval hosts are related, but they are not equivalent, ecological properties. Dietary specialization is commonly described by the number or phylogenetic diversity of host plants used by a butterfly. Such measures help explain resource dependence and distribution (Slove & Janz, 2011; Lancaster, 2020). They do not directly indicate whether those same host species occur only in a small native range or have been redistributed across distant regions. Anthropogenic movements of plants can alter the geography of potential resources without any change in a butterfly's known larval-host list.

This difference has become consequential because humans have redistributed thousands of plant species beyond their native ranges, with widespread effects on plant community similarity (van Kleunen et al., 2015; Daru et al., 2021). Naturalised forage plants, turf grasses, crops and ruderal species can become geographically widespread. The butterfly associated with such a plant may remain a taxonomic specialist, yet encounter its familiar host in more regions than expected from native-range maps. Conversely, a generalist may depend on several hosts that have undergone relatively little anthropogenic redistribution. The geographical consequences of a fixed diet are therefore contingent on the changing distributions of the interaction partners.

Islands offer a particularly informative contrast because resource availability and accessibility need not change together. Introduced plants can increase potential larval-host opportunities on insular botanical regions, yet butterflies still face geographic separation from other recorded populations. Ports are possible indicators of broad human-linked plant redistribution, but their contemporary numbers also covary with island size, history, human population, habitat disturbance and survey intensity. Likewise, a current flowing through a modelled marine-resistance network is not an observed butterfly movement, and newly indexed butterfly records do not document the date or route of biological arrival. These distinctions demand a comparison that keeps resource, observed butterfly geography, and candidate access mechanisms analytically separate.

Existing studies have examined butterfly range shifts, host switching and the constraints imposed by plant geography (Braschler & Hill, 2007; Neu et al., 2021). We instead begin on the resource side: holding butterfly–host associations fixed, what did plant redistribution change for butterflies? We then ask whether islands have experienced a greater change than mainland regions, and whether butterfly-record geography retains information consistent with constraints beyond resource opportunity. Finally, we use butterfly species' own record histories and traits to evaluate how far these observations can support interpretations of access, rather than letting island similarity become the biological response by default.

We analyse three explicitly distinct layers rather than treat them as one causally identified pathway. First, a native-to-contemporary reconstruction tests the magnitude and concentration of potential resource gains for 239 butterflies across botanical regions. Second, insular versus mainland resource comparisons and port-associated introduced floras test where those opportunities accumulate. Third, separate butterfly occurrence-evaluation panels test whether host geography and source accessibility contain information about recorded butterfly distributions. We predict broad resource gains across host-breadth classes, disproportionate insular opportunity, a broad-flora rather than exclusively host-specific association with ports, and a geographically structured—but not necessarily trait-independent—recording pattern among island butterflies. Evidence for historical plant arrival, larval feeding and actual colonisation is outside the scope of these datasets.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1. Three data grains, one butterfly-centred question

Analyses were assembled from source-versioned, non-interchangeable populations. The global resource reconstruction uses **239 resource-eligible butterflies** and botanical regions of the World Geographical Scheme for Recording Plant Distributions at Level 3 (WGSRPD3). A separate climate/land-cover-plus-host recording evaluation contains **155 butterflies** with sufficient indexed records; a small supplementary host-envelope overlap comparison includes **32 selected butterflies**. The island record and access reconstruction uses a **fixed 225-butterfly candidate list across 51 WPI-associated GSHHG landmass groups**, of which **199 butterflies** have complete frozen LepTraits wing-size and disturbance descriptors. The 51 landmass groups, 98 effort-covered insular botanical regions, and WGSRPD3 insular regions are different geographic supports; their counts and coefficients are never pooled as though they share one denominator.

The global ecological response is *potential butterfly × botanical-region host opportunity*. The butterfly-record responses are *indexed butterfly records, retrospective recording probabilities, or first-indexed species × landmass cells*. They are not occupancy estimates, observations of larval host use, individual movements, or successful establishment. We report these definitions throughout because an increase in one outcome does not mechanistically imply an increase in another.

### 2.2. Reconstructing butterfly resource geography

The frozen HOSTS interaction dataset links butterflies to accepted larval hosts in the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP). We held accepted butterfly–host identities fixed and used each host plant's native and contemporary (native plus introduced) WGSRPD3 distributions to construct a binary potential resource envelope for each butterfly. For butterfly s and region r, the envelope was coded as a candidate opportunity if at least one accepted host of s was recorded in r. The native-only and contemporary envelopes used the same interaction list. The difference therefore measures the geography added by plant distributions and not host-switching or expanded taxonomic diet.

We calculated the added number of butterfly–region combinations, the contemporary/native resource ratio and the occurrence of gains across classes of host-family breadth. We decomposed added butterfly–region credit among contributing host species and genera using the original fractional attribution, preserving the finite number of possible regions. We retained published analyses excluding exact FAO crop-host identities and a more conservative plant-genus crop exclusion, Poaceae-feeder exclusion, native-resource breadth controls, family mixed effects, geographically stratified comparisons, and a taxon-matched butterfly phylogenetic sensitivity. A post hoc narrow-equivalence test for the host-breadth association was reported as a bound, not proof of zero association.

### 2.3. Island amplification of resource opportunity

WGSRPD3 regions were classified using the archived strict island coding rule rather than assuming that every geographically named island belongs to an island region. We contrasted native and contemporary butterfly–region resource combinations across **126 insular and 237 mainland botanical regions** in the original comparison, with area- and broad-biogeographic matching based on **15 supported Level-1 × region-area overlap strata**. Relative gains are computed within each geography class. Because alternative classifications and exclusions affect denominators, class-specific butterfly–region counts are not added to reconstruct the different overall 239-species total.

The matched comparison estimates a difference in the *share of contemporary opportunities attributable to introduced host geography* for comparable strata. It is not a controlled field experiment, an increase in butterfly abundance or an established proportion of opportunities actually used.

### 2.4. Ports and the whole introduced flora

For **98 insular botanical regions** with source-defined plant indexing effort, we examined associations between modern World Port Index (WPI) port counts and (a) total introduced vascular-plant richness, (b) distinct introduced taxa known as butterfly larval hosts, and (c) the host-taxon response conditional on the whole introduced flora. Geographic, native-baseline, area and botanical-indexing controls followed the source analysis. We retain each model's own coefficient rather than interpreting different response-model coefficients as directly comparable causal effects. We did not infer ships, botanical propagule routes or port-specific plant arrival dates from current WPI port counts.

### 2.5. Does host geography matter for butterfly records?

Two separate observation checks guard against a purely arithmetic interpretation of larger plant-defined envelopes. In a stratified 32-butterfly host-envelope comparison, we examined whether contemporary butterfly records outside native-only host envelopes fell inside the expanded envelopes, testing geometric and region-matched null expectations. In a larger **155-butterfly** panel, we compared climate-plus-land-cover recording predictions against models also including host-resource and host-composition information under a subsequent-period evaluation, held spatial blocks, and documented observation/source sensitivities. These analyses assess correspondence and prediction of GBIF records; static contemporary host geography is **not** a dated pre-colonisation exposure. Temporal holdout refers to butterfly records, not historically frozen host maps.

### 2.6. Butterfly species and island-record histories

The 225 candidate butterflies were fixed from the original island host-network source before examining subsequent recording outcomes. To make source availability auditable, we independently re-queried public GBIF species-key facets on original GSHHG 2.3.7 landmass polygons in three windows: **2001–2010**, **2011–2017**, and **2018–2022**. The source uses the documented six butterfly-family taxon keys, valid-geographic-coordinate filters and PRESENT occurrence status; it is **not restricted to human observations**. Six independent source shards were combined only after polygon-area, query-receipt, taxonomic-key and record-rule checks, producing **11,475 candidate species × landmass cells**. Differences from the older 17-landmass GBIF indexing snapshot were audited. This newly queried source is distinct from the previously unavailable, private, 83,573-row annual Parquet.

For each butterfly species and recording period, a target landmass was eligible for a first index only when that species had no indexed record there in an earlier window. To calculate source distance, we additionally required at least one previously indexed conspecific *elsewhere among the sampled 51 landmass groups*. That earlier indexed landmass is a **candidate recording source**, never a demonstrated exporting population. Nearest source distance is calculated among those candidates only; it may greatly overestimate the distance to the closest true population when nearer mainland or island populations are outside the panel. We report species- and island-specific observed record counts as descriptive outcomes without treating multiple island records from a species as independent migration events.

### 2.7. Marine resistance and butterfly trait sensitivity

The source-locked marine network uses original island-port representative locations and a modelled undirected ocean-resistance surface. The 51-landmass uniform-ocean pair matrix is used to construct the minimum resistance to earlier indexed conspecific landmasses. We separately model nearest geographic distance and the residual of log marine resistance after a source-only quadratic regression on log geographic distance. In analyses of retrospective initial indexing, geographic access, island area, latitude/longitude, prior species indexing, target butterfly indexing effort, modern port count and early general botanical indexing are included as controls where supported. The ocean residual is a statistical quantity, not an observed directed ship route.

The 199 matched LepTraits butterflies provided a composite mean of recorded wing-span and forewing numerical bounds and disturbance-affinity categories. Wing-size proxy is not independently measured migratory range or aerodynamic performance. A positive-only set of **111 candidate migratory butterflies** was also matched exactly to a later researcher's GBIF request based indirectly on the 2021 migration review; the review's original supplementary species table was not accessible. **All 114 unlisted candidate species have UNKNOWN migratory status and are not non-migrant controls.**

We evaluated L2-regularised logistic retrospective indexing models with training-only standardization, holding out individual landmasses, seven source-only geographic groups, or fixed species groups. We reported Brier score and log loss, including worse-performing comparisons. A separate 2×2 constrained null exchanged initial-index events across eligible butterfly × landmass cells while preserving the number of events for each butterfly and target landmass. A stricter null additionally restricted exchanges within the original seven geographic groups, preserving butterfly × region event counts. Trait interactions were centred by the event-weighted trait mean to avoid mistaking the main spatial-access association for a trait effect. These are exploratory conditional randomisation diagnostics, not a random experiment on biological colonisation.

## 3. Results

### 3.1. Human-redistributed hosts broaden the geography of most butterflies

The contemporary host envelope included **41,083 butterfly × WGSRPD3 resource combinations**, compared with **26,530** under native-only host distributions. The **14,553** added combinations represent a **54.9%** aggregate expansion for the resource-eligible 239 butterflies. Gains occurred for **206/239 (86.2%)** butterflies. They were widespread for one-family feeders (**82/97, 84.5%**) as well as two-family (**47/59**), three-to-five-family (**44/50**) and six-or-more-family (**33/33**) feeders. The increases persisted after excluding exact FAO crop hosts (**47.0%**) or applying the stricter genus-level exclusion (**46.0%**). These are added *potential* host co-occurrence opportunities at a coarse geographic scale, not measured increases in butterfly distributions or fitness.

Added opportunity was concentrated in a relatively small set of host plants. Of **670** contributing host species, the leading **38** supplied half the added butterfly–region credit; the top 10 supplied **25.1%**. The principal plants include *Medicago sativa*, *Poa pratensis*, *Oxalis corniculata*, *Cynodon dactylon* and *Dactylis glomerata*. Concentration persisted after genus aggregation. This identifies the botanical identities most associated with the arithmetic expansion of butterfly opportunity without establishing their historical transport mechanisms or superior larval performance.

### 3.2. Conventional host breadth does not capture geographic opportunity gains

Host-family breadth was only weakly associated with proportional resource gain (full-panel Spearman **rho = 0.008**, species bootstrap **95% CI −0.111 to 0.128**). A strict post hoc equivalence test for an absolute association smaller than 0.10 was narrowly inconclusive (**p = 0.078**). Thus our finding is a small estimated correlation with bounded but nonzero uncertainty, **not** proven exact independence. The association remained near zero after adjusting native resource breadth, excluding Poaceae feeders (**rho = 0.026**, n = 181) and using family and geographically stratified sensitivities. A partial species-level phylogenetic comparison also failed to identify a consistent large effect, though coverage and the fixed-Brownian sensitivity leave uncertainty.

The ecological result is positive rather than simply a null slope: host redistribution expanded the geography of most *taxonomically specialized* butterflies. A butterfly's number of host families and the anthropogenic geographic distribution of its hosts capture different properties of resource dependence.

### 3.3. Islands gain disproportionately, while ports index broad introduced floras

Within strict insular botanical regions, potential butterfly–region resource combinations increased from **4,829 to 10,570 (+118.9%)**, compared with **21,215 to 29,901 (+40.9%)** on the mainland. An area- and Level-1-biogeography-matched comparison across **15 supported strata** retained an insular excess of **+10.7 percentage points** in introduced-added contemporary resource share (descriptive permutation **p = 0.0002**).

Across **98** effort-covered insular regions, modern WPI port counts positively covaried with total introduced flora (model coefficient **beta = +0.2445**, descriptive p = 0.0012) and with distinct introduced larval-host taxa (**beta = +0.1639**, p = 0.011). Conditioning the latter on the richness of the entire introduced flora yielded no positive additional host-selective coefficient (**beta = −0.0415**, p = 0.093). Ports therefore provide a spatial indicator of broader alien-plant assemblages that contain butterfly hosts, not demonstrated preferential transport of butterfly food plants.

### 3.4. Butterfly-record geography retains information beyond native-only resources

In the separate 32-species envelope check, contemporary butterfly records generated **115** butterfly × region observations outside native-only resource envelopes; introduced-host geography recovered **66/115 (57.4%)**. The region-matched null and high-leverage species exclusions retained a positive overlap, although an accepted host in a region does not demonstrate feeding there. A broader **155-butterfly** recent-record evaluation compared the fixed climate-and-land-cover model with added host information across **208,482** evaluation combinations. Species-equal MSE improved by **4.12%** for the subsequent-period check and **3.00%** for held spatial blocks; the more distant evaluation subset also improved, with the caveat that it was a post hoc subset and not a third independent forecast. These are retrospective improvements in indexed recording prediction for known species, not out-of-species validation or causal proof of historically prior host arrival.

On the separate **51-landmass** host/butterfly assembly dataset, candidate port-associated host assemblage similarity corresponded to later indexed butterfly assemblage similarity (Spearman **rho = +0.300** across **1,275 dependent pairs**; distance-and-earlier-butterfly adjusted descriptive partial rank **+0.215**). Host assemblages were associated at least as strongly with **earlier** recorded butterflies (**rho = +0.418**), a crucial counterweight to the tempting inference that host redistribution uniquely preceded newly arriving butterflies. A different source-locked joint prediction of later butterfly Jaccard had conditional leave-one-island-out MSE gains of **1.73%** from host predictors beyond marine terms and **6.49%** from marine predictors beyond hosts. Both were geographically and model-specification dependent and should not be conflated with the species × island first-index outcome.

### 3.5. Recorded butterfly species have spatially structured first indexes, not verified routes

The independent public 225-butterfly by 51-landmass GBIF reindex identified **221** first-indexed species × landmass cells in **2011–2017**, and **96** in **2018–2022**; there were **11,475** candidate cells altogether. After restricting to complete LepTraits information and at least one earlier indexed conspecific elsewhere within the 51 groups, the respective analytical risk sets were **4,374/187** and **4,933/83** cells/first indexes. A source-locked species-first event table retains **270** index events and species-level profiles rather than substituting island similarity for butterfly identity. Illustrative later records include *Zizula hylax* on five sampled landmasses, *Nymphalis antiopa* on four, and *Vanessa cardui* on three; the candidate prior-index source does not document their actual biological origins.

Under the species-, target-island- and within-region-event-count constrained null, indexed conspecific source proximity showed positive spatial alignment with first-index outcomes in both periods (Monte Carlo **p = 0.00033** for the 2018–2022 geographic proximity statistic). A quadratic-distance-residual uniform-ocean-access statistic also aligned with later first indexes (**p = 0.00233** in the full trait-complete 51-landmass panel). However, in the later geographical-region-held-out first-index prediction, adding uniform-ocean resistance beyond the distance-based model **worsened** Brier prediction by **6.48%**. A source-aligned association and an independently transferable predictive gain are distinct quantities; the first does not imply the second.

### 3.6. Traits and maritime proxies do not identify how butterflies reached islands

In the 199-trait-butterfly later window, adding wing-size proxy × nearest conspecific source distance modestly reduced Brier loss by **0.349%** for island-held-out, **1.861%** for geographic-group-held-out and **0.134%** for species-block-held-out predictions. Yet a stricter within-region species-and-island case-count null did not support the wing-size association (**p = 0.227**), nor did it support wing-size moderation of the distance-residual ocean resistance (**p = 0.645**). Restricting to butterfly species in the **111-species author-selected migration candidate list** did not produce a consistent wing-size × distance prediction gain across the same three holdouts; the unlisted remainder cannot be used as a sedentary control.

The candidate-migrant subset retained an association between later first-index geography and proximity to earlier indexed conspecifics under within-region case-count constraints (later Monte Carlo **p = 0.00067**). Its ocean-residual association was weaker (later **p = 0.0473** before multiplicity adjustment). Removing the two most port-rich landmass groups (IDs 15 and 9), **as both targets and candidate prior sources**, retained the later proximity association (**p = 0.00267**) but weakened the ocean-residual association (**p = 0.229**). Thus observed butterfly-record geography is not explained uniformly by wing size, the indirect migration roster or a stable independent maritime term. Additional vessel-density placebos and a separate historical liner-connectivity test also fail to identify ship-specific transport from the current data.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1. Resource globalization is not equivalent to generalization in butterflies

The principal insight is that a fixed interaction repertoire can have a changing geography. Nearly nine in ten focal butterflies gained potential larval-host regions when already-known host plant species were allowed to occupy their introduced distributions. The gain crossed one-family and multi-family host breadths, with a negligible estimated host-breadth gradient. This does not make a one-family species biologically generalist. Rather, it separates *interaction breadth* from *the distributional geography of those interactions*. A specialized butterfly whose hosts have been moved broadly by humans may inhabit a more geographically extensive potential resource landscape without having evolved broader feeding behavior.

The strong concentration of added geographic opportunity among a small set of hosts adds a second useful distinction. Host–plant identity matters to the geographic reorganization, but the concentration does not prove those species were selectively imported for butterflies, used by larvae outside their native ranges, or intrinsically high-quality resources. Host naturalisation, pasture and turf management, other disturbances and recording intensity may all contribute.

### 4.2. Islands amplify the resource-side contrast, not verified colonisation

Islands emerged as disproportionate beneficiaries of introduced *potential host geography*, including after matching the supported botanical regions on area and broad geography. Resource reconstructions and butterfly records should nevertheless not be treated as if they were observations of a completed plant-to-butterfly invasion chain. In particular, the botanical regions used to estimate resource gain and the 51 port-indexed landmass source groups used for first-indexing are different, non-random samples. Directly multiplying their effects or reading the ratio of first indexes to added host opportunities as colonisation success would violate these study designs.

Port association sharpens that interpretation. Ports covary with the broader introduced plant flora, which includes larval-host taxa, but a host-specific surplus after accounting for all introduced plants was not observed. This supports a picture of generalized plant redistribution, not an identified mechanism of preferentially imported butterfly food plants. It is compatible with, but cannot distinguish, shipping, horticulture, agriculture, settlement history and local observation.

### 4.3. Butterfly records reflect both resource geography and imperfect access information

Two distinct forms of evidence indicate that resource geography is ecologically informative rather than merely a reconstructed map: introduced-host envelopes recover contemporary butterfly records outside native-only resource envelopes, and host covariates improve subsequent-record prediction for 155 butterflies beyond climate and land cover. Neither is direct proof of larval feeding or the causal effect of a dated plant introduction, but they suggest that ignoring redistributed hosts can mischaracterize the present geography of known butterfly resources.

The island butterfly panel adds a complementary spatial dimension. The strongest butterfly-centred observation is that later indexed conspecific records are structured relative to *previously indexed* landmasses, after holding several record-count margins fixed. The residual-ocean statistic gives further observational alignment, but its weak geographic transfer and sensitivity to two port-rich landmasses restrict mechanistic interpretation. A minimum distance to a conspecific among only 51 sampled landmass groups can easily miss intervening continental or island populations. Uniform over-water circuits do not contain island stopover persistence or actual butterfly trajectories, and current port indices are not the historical plant or larva shipping records required by a causal relay hypothesis.

The weak transfer of wing and migration proxies is an instructive boundary rather than an unwanted result. Species with larger wings need not have greater ability to survive a given ocean crossing; wing loading, flight behaviour, wind, anthropogenic transport and habitat suitability matter. Here the migration list is a literature-derived *positive-only* proxy, while 114 unlisted candidate species are genuinely unknown. Neither can test migrant-versus-sedentary colonisation at the studied islands. A butterfly-first manuscript should not replace species ecology with an unvalidated binary label merely because that would produce a more striking island figure.

### 4.4. General implications and the missing causal test

The results suggest a conceptual **resource–access distinction** for consumer biogeography under globalization. Human redistribution can expand the area containing familiar interaction partners, while the spatial arrangement of populations and barriers can shape where consumers are subsequently recorded. The two layers are measurable on different data sources and can both contain predictive information; their interaction as a biological mechanism has not been identified by these retrospective data.

This distinction matters for conservation and invasion management. Introduced host plants may be candidate resources for native butterflies but can also be unsuitable or produce ecological traps (Yoon & Read, 2016; Braga, 2023). Potential resource-cell counts are neither demographic benefits nor evidence that planting an exotic host is desirable. Assessing the consequences for a given butterfly requires confirming its local oviposition, larval development, host phenology and persistence and weighing the ecological costs of the introduced plant.

A decisive mechanistic next test would establish whether species-specific compatible introduced hosts were **actually indexed or observed on an island before butterfly arrival**, control the effort and habitat available at port and non-port sites, and compare documented butterfly reproduction or independent population histories under geographically and temporally held-out designs. Historical vessel movements and a directed movement model allowing stopover populations would be necessary to distinguish direct flight, transport, and stepwise colonisation. These requirements define the limits of our present inference rather than becoming unobserved Results.

## 5. Limitations

The plant and butterfly layers have different units, species filters, geography and temporal resolution. HOSTS is incomplete; WCVP introduction status is a region-level contemporary record without a date or vector of actual establishment. WGSRPD3 opportunity does not ensure that a host and larva coexist at a field site. The host-family equivalence margin was post hoc and was not passed in a strict test. Butterfly occurrence prediction uses static contemporary host layers and opportunistic recording; time holdout on butterfly records is not a historical prospective test of host movement.

The 51-landmass GBIF panel comprises selected port-linked groups and uses a new 2026 public index rather than the unavailable 83,573-row author original. Non-indexing is not absence, first indexing is not first arrival, and the independently dated source distances are among sampled landmasses only. Pairwise Jaccard rows reuse the same island identities and do not constitute 1,275 independent biological replicates. Modelled electrical marine resistance, current concentration, present-day port abundance and maritime activity proxies must not be interpreted as realized immigration, ship routes or historically dated propagule delivery. The conditional randomisations are retrospective and multiple comparisons are exploratory. Although results about the geography of *recording* are reproducible, no integrated historical island host × access × butterfly establishment likelihood is estimated.

## 6. Conclusions

Human-redistributed larval hosts have greatly expanded potential resource geography for butterflies, including dietary specialists, and the expansion is especially large on islands. Host geography contributes information to observed butterfly records, and island first-index records retain spatial relationships with earlier recorded conspecifics. However, the resource expansion, port-flora association and butterfly access patterns are distinct observational layers, not a demonstrated ship-mediated causal sequence. The general advance is to separate the breadth of a consumer's diet from the geography of its partners and from the accessibility of its recorded populations.
