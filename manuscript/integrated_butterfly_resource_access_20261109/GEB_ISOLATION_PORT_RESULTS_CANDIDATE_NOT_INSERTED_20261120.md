Across 126 botanical islands, mainland isolation and modern port counts showed contrasting associations with mapped plant and butterfly host resources after adjusting for island area, absolute latitude and botanical continent. Introduced plant richness was positively associated with ports (standardized β = +0.350, HC3 p = 0.009), whereas native richness was not (β = +0.083, p = 0.279). Among the fixed 239 butterflies, native-host opportunity declined with mainland distance (β = −0.168, p < 0.001), while contemporary native-plus-introduced host coverage lacked a detectable negative distance slope (β = +0.019, p = 0.751). Their paired distance-slope contrast was positive (β = +0.187, HC3 p = 0.001), but less certain when clustered by nine botanical continents (p = 0.068). The introduced-only fraction of mapped potential host coverage increased with isolation (log-odds β = +0.425; nine-cluster p = 0.014), with adjusted shares of 55%, 66% and 72% at 100, 1,000 and 4,000 km, respectively. These associations describe potential resource geography, not established butterflies or demonstrated plant transport.
