# Prior evidence and the contribution of the island–port study

Targeted comparison of primary studies, 17 November 2026. The comparison covers the closest precedents for each inference in the manuscript. It establishes the scope of the contribution without a claim to bibliographic priority.

| Question | What the prior study actually measured | What can be attributed to this study |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How do butterfly diets vary across islands and mainlands? | A global study related larval diet breadth to climate, regional plant richness and island endemism. [Gross, Kawahara & Daru (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-73236-4) | For each of 239 butterflies, hold its documented host list fixed and compare the fraction of island and mainland botanical regions containing any host under native-only and contemporary ranges. This measures a geographic change in potential food availability. |
| Are human activities associated with richer introduced island floras? | Across oceanic islands, alien plant richness covaried with human population size and native richness. [Blackburn et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12339) | Regional port counts describe where the introduced plant pool is richer. Identifying globally important host taxa in dated port-area inventories links these floras to particular butterfly resources. Crops and ornamentals contribute alongside weeds. |
| Can ships carry weed seeds? | Inspection of refrigerated containers at Savannah recovered seeds from air-intake grilles. [Lucardi et al. (2020)](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/60867) | Port-area plant occurrences locate potential host resources. Vessel carriage, horticultural introductions and subsequent escape remain possible for individual taxa at those ports. |
| Can introduced hosts support range expansion or precede insect invasions? | Californian records document butterfly use of introduced hosts and range expansion in some taxa. A global analysis found that earlier nonnative plant flows predicted later insect flows between regions. [Graves & Shapiro (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0006-3207(02)00233-1) · [Bonnamour et al. (2023)](https://www.fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/jrnl/2023/nrs_2023_bonnamour_001.pdf) | The island–mainland contrast measures how much of the initial host-coverage gap shrinks. Global contributor identities can be checked against local plants with alternative native hosts, including cases in which the putative host was one of several choices. |
| Can butterflies colonise islands in sequence and use introduced hosts? | Pacific monarch populations carry a genetic signature of serial founding. Experiments also documented population differences in host performance during monarch expansion. [Pierce et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2014.2230) · [Freedman et al. (2020)](https://academic.oup.com/evolut/article/74/2/377/6726937) | Dated host, larval and later butterfly detections nominate island pairs for follow-up. Multiple source islands can lead to the same later detection, and the ancestry of each later individual is unknown. |

**Manuscript wording.** The contribution is the estimate of how introduced plants change the regional coverage of existing larval diets, followed by an identity check of the plants contributing most to that change in port inventories. The island advantage in proportional gain mainly reflects low native coverage. Local histories illustrate an additional possibility: an introduced plant can add another food option where compatible native plants already occur. Separate samples test resource coverage, port vegetation and butterfly records. Confirming a port-mediated breeding relay would require independent evidence for plant introduction, local host use and the origin of onward dispersers.
