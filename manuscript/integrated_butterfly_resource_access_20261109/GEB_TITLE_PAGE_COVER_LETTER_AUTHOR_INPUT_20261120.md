## GEB cover-letter significance paragraph (English; 175 words, source-safe)

Plant introductions are widely known to reshape floras, but they also redistribute familiar ecological partners: a plant already used by a butterfly elsewhere may become its first documented larval food resource in a new region, without any evolution of diet. Across 239 butterflies and global botanical regions, introduced hosts increased mapped butterfly–region resource opportunities by 54.9%. The proportionate increase was particularly large on islands (118.9%), not because introductions filled a larger fraction of remaining host-resource gaps, but because native hosts initially covered fewer opportunities. The new resources were unevenly supplied by plant identities and unequally distributed among butterflies with different existing host geographies. Island ports and marine-geographic predictors helped characterize distinct contemporary botanical layers. Independent butterfly records also retained additional, but geographically conditional, information from known-host geography. This study connects island biogeography, anthropogenic plant redistribution and consumer-resource geography through a clearly defined, transferable source-opportunity estimand. Its evidence distinguishes potential food geography from confirmed butterfly establishment, a crucial distinction for understanding ecological consequences of human plant exchange.
