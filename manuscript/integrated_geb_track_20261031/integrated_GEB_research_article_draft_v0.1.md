# Host-plant globalization expands butterfly resource geography and improves occurrence predictions

**Integrated alternative research manuscript v0.1 — 2026-10-31. NOT submission-ready; new figures and sensitivity sections require validation. Original GEB v0.2 remains unchanged.**

## Abstract

**Aim:** We tested whether human redistribution of known larval hosts expands butterfly resource geography, improves later butterfly-record predictions, and corresponds with island assemblage patterns.

**Location:** Global, with an exploratory comparison among 51 islands.

**Time period:** Contemporary native and introduced plant distributions; butterfly records from 2010–2026; island-port botanical records pooled across 2001–2024.

**Major taxa studied:** Butterflies and their larval host plants.

**Methods:** We reconstructed native-only and contemporary resource envelopes for 239 butterflies, holding host identities fixed. We assessed host predictors beyond climate and land cover in 208,482 temporally held-out recording-probability evaluations for 155 species, with spatial sensitivity tests. Across 51 islands, we related port-indexed candidate introduced-host composition to new butterfly assemblages.

**Results:** Introduced-host ranges added 14,553 butterfly × region resource units (+54.9%) and expanded potential resources for 206/239 butterflies; removing crop hosts retained 46–47% growth. Thirty-eight plant species contributed half of the additional opportunity, whereas diet-family breadth was weakly associated with proportional gain (Spearman ρ = 0.008). Host information reduced temporal test mean squared error by 4.12%, spatially held-out error by 3.00%, and error at distances ≥100 km from training locations by 2.74%. Among 51 islands (1,275 non-independent pairs), candidate introduced-host similarity covaried with the similarity of newly recorded butterfly assemblages (ρ = 0.300; partial rank correlation after distance and prior butterfly composition = 0.215). Association with previously recorded butterfly assemblages was stronger (ρ = 0.418).

**Main conclusions:** Anthropogenic redistribution of known hosts enlarges potential butterfly resource geography, and host-resource information improves predictions of later and spatially withheld butterfly records. Island-port host composition shows additional spatial concordance with butterfly records, but these associations do not establish plant transport routes, local larval feeding or host-mediated colonization.

**Keywords:** biotic redistribution; larval host plants; butterflies; macroecology; occurrence prediction; island biogeography; introduced plants

## 1. Introduction

In butterflies, larval host specialization is a defining ecological axis. Comparative studies usually express it as a property of the butterfly: how many host taxa it uses, how phylogenetically diverse those hosts are, or how host breadth covaries with the butterfly's own geographic range. Broader diets have been linked to larger ranges and to range-shift dynamics, while recent global work has identified climate and regional plant richness as drivers of larval diet specialization (Slove & Janz 2011; Lancaster 2020; Gross et al. 2026). These approaches establish that **what a butterfly can eat** matters for biogeography. They do not, however, specify **where those larval resources occur**.

That distinction becomes important under global change because interaction partners are redistributed independently. Humans have naturalized more than 13,000 plant species outside their native ranges, and these movements have measurably homogenized regional floras worldwide (van Kleunen et al. 2015; Daru et al. 2021). For butterflies, this plant globalization changes not only floral composition but the geography of potential larval resources. A butterfly restricted to one plant family may use a host that humans have spread across continents, whereas a taxonomic generalist may depend on many hosts whose distributions remain geographically limited. Human transport can therefore change the geographic amount of resource opportunity without changing the butterfly's taxonomic diet breadth at all. Regional studies already show that introduced plants can become larval hosts and sometimes accompany butterfly range expansion or longer flight seasons (Graves & Shapiro 2003). Species-level range-shift studies also show the complementary route in which the butterfly changes: exploiting more widespread hosts or reduced host specialization can accompany butterfly expansion (Braschler & Hill 2007; Neu et al. 2021). Our question is the mirror image. We hold the butterfly's known host list fixed and ask what happens when the **hosts themselves are redistributed**. Host-range studies further show that plant availability can constrain butterfly distributions, particularly for specialists, but typically treat host geography as a current or future constraint rather than ask how anthropogenic plant redistribution has already altered that geography (Rashid et al. 2026).

A second literature documents redistribution of butterflies and other Lepidoptera themselves. Global syntheses show widespread contemporary butterfly range shifts, while non-native Lepidoptera are now established worldwide and global trade produces strongly asymmetric flows among regions (Chowdhury et al. 2026; Couto et al. 2026). More broadly, species introductions can homogenize the composition of ecological interaction networks across regions (Fricke & Svenning 2020). These results establish that globalization changes both species distributions and interaction structure. They do not answer the butterfly-specific geographic question posed here: **how much does moving known larval host plants change where a butterfly has access to resources?** What remains unresolved is this **resource-side biogeography of butterfly specialization under globalization**—the magnitude of anthropogenic change in butterfly resource geography, whether conventional specialization metrics capture that change, and whether the added opportunity is diffusely distributed across hosts or carried by a small set of highly redistributed plants.

Butterflies provide an unusually tractable system for addressing this gap because global host-use data can be linked to plant native and introduced distributions. Our unit of inference is therefore not the composition of a local interaction network but the **butterfly-specific resource envelope**: for each butterfly, we project the distributions of its known hosts into geographic opportunity and contrast the envelope implied by native host ranges with the envelope available after introduced ranges are retained. This paired reconstruction separates two quantities that are often implicitly conflated: **taxonomic specialization** (how broadly a butterfly feeds) and **resource geography** (where its known hosts occur). If human redistribution substantially expands resource geography even for narrow-diet butterflies, then specialist–generalist labels alone will not predict how ecological opportunity has been reorganized by globalization.

Here we distinguish three empirical levels that should not be treated as identical outcomes: potential host-resource distributions, prediction of subsequent butterfly recording probabilities, and spatial concordance of island butterfly and candidate-host plant assemblages. The first tests the geography implied by known butterfly–plant associations and contemporary plant distributions. The second asks whether knowledge of those resources is informative when predicting held-out butterfly recording patterns in addition to climate and land cover. The third evaluates where patterns in port-indexed plants and newly recorded butterflies covary across islands, while explicitly confronting prior butterfly composition and uneven geographic sampling. These components are linked by a general question, not by an assumed shipping-mediated causal chain.

Our hypotheses were that (i) introduced host distributions would add substantial resource geography for many butterflies without a proportional dependence on diet-family breadth; (ii) host-resource predictors would improve later and spatially withheld recording-probability predictions beyond climate and land cover; and (iii) similarities in host plants indexed near island ports would covary with new butterfly recording assemblages. The last hypothesis is exploratory: pooled botanical records may postdate butterflies, port/nonport botanical control is incomplete, and historical first indexing is not arrival or establishment. The smaller climate-stratified occurrence-recovery panel remains a separate observational comparison.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Conceptual design: larval host breadth, resource geography and butterfly occurrence

We organized the analysis around three related but non-equivalent quantities. **Taxonomic interaction breadth** describes which kinds of larval hosts a butterfly uses, measured here as host-family richness. **Resource geography** describes where those known hosts occur, represented by the union of their geographic distributions. **Realized butterfly geography** describes where the butterfly is actually observed. Human redistribution enters between the first two quantities: moving a known host can alter a butterfly's resource geography without changing its taxonomic interaction breadth.

We therefore first reconstructed each butterfly's resource geography under native-only and contemporary host distributions and measured the difference as anthropogenic resource gain. We then asked whether proportional gain scales with taxonomic diet breadth, which host plants account for the added geography, and whether the contemporary envelope recovers butterfly occurrences missed by the native-only envelope. The climate analysis provides a secondary test of filtering between geographic opportunity and realization.

The LepTraits-derived descriptor panel contained 339 species (Shirey et al. 2022). Resource analyses used 239 species with positive host-family breadth and at least one reconstructed native host-resource WGSRPD3 unit. A conservative subset of 215 species also required the number of resolved host species to equal or exceed LepTraits host-family count. A separate 32-species panel was used for occurrence validation and climate analysis; it had been selected to balance the subsequent climate test, not optimized for validation. Twenty-four species passed the climate quality criteria.

The resource reconstruction is descriptive and uses fixed LepTraits, HOSTS and WCVP inputs. The 32-species panel was assembled separately for the climate test, using eligibility and analysis rules fixed before its ecological responses were evaluated; the occurrence analysis is a secondary use of that panel. The host-contribution decomposition, equivalence diagnostic and later null-model, ceiling, phylogenetic and geographic sensitivities are identified as post-hoc analyses.

### 2.2 Reconstructing native and contemporary resource geography

Larval host records came from HOSTS and plant taxonomy and distributions from WCVP (Robinson et al. 2023; Govaerts et al. 2021). Species-level host names were resolved to accepted WCVP taxa. Under WCVP terminology, introduced distributions are wild non-native records (including casual, naturalised and invasive occurrences), not cultivation records; plants that are only cultivated or merely planted without self-reproduction are not classified as introduced.

For each butterfly, the **native resource envelope** was the union of WGSRPD level-3 units in which any resolved host was native, extant and non-doubtful. The **contemporary resource envelope** additionally retained introduced host distributions. Host identities were held fixed between the two envelopes, so their difference isolates redistribution of already-known resources rather than host switching or expansion of the butterfly's recorded diet. WGSRPD3 is a coarse regional framework (Brummitt et al. 2001), so these envelopes represent geographic resource opportunity rather than local host occupancy.

As a crop-host sensitivity, we repeated the reconstruction after excluding host taxa listed in the FAO Indicative Crop Classification, first by exact botanical binomial and then with an intentionally over-conservative rule that also excluded all congeners when the FAO list specified a crop as spp. Host-family breadth was kept fixed as the specialization predictor; only the reconstructed resource envelopes and host contributions were recalculated (Supplementary Table S7).

We calculated native and contemporary resource breadth, introduced-added units, the contemporary/native ratio, log resource expansion, and the introduced share of contemporary geography. In a post-hoc descriptive decomposition, each butterfly × WGSRPD3 unit newly added by introduced host distributions contributed one unit of credit divided equally among the host species that added that unit. Credits therefore summed exactly to the aggregate number of added butterfly × region units. We summarized the concentration of those credits across host plants using cumulative top-k shares, the Gini coefficient and the inverse-Herfindahl effective contributor number. As a taxonomic sensitivity, contributing plant species were first collapsed to unique genera within each butterfly × region unit and the unit credit was then divided equally among those genera. This decomposition is explicitly contribution-weighted: a plant can accumulate credit by being used by more focal butterflies, by adding more geographic units, or both. We therefore use it to describe which resources generate the reconstructed opportunity, not as a sample of independent plant-level effects.

### 2.3 Robustness analyses for host identity, specialization and taxonomic structure

We examined host-family breadth against proportional resource gain using rank correlation and cluster/bootstrap uncertainty, with sensitivity analyses for native-envelope extent, broad biogeographic region, butterfly Family and phylogeny. The 215-species conservative taxonomic subset and the 124 species with exact Kawahara et al. (2023) tree matches were analyzed separately; tree-matched results were not treated as estimates for all 239 species. Crop exclusion was evaluated using FAO host lists, including a deliberately more conservative genus-based exclusion. Degree-/family-constrained host-network nulls and plant-prominence analyses were retained as post-hoc diagnostics rather than evidence of an identified host-selection mechanism; details and negative controls are in the existing Supplementary Information. A post-hoc equivalence test for the host-breadth association was treated as inconclusive, not proof of zero association.

### 2.4 Secondary occurrence validation

Butterfly occurrences for 2010–2026 were obtained from GBIF (GBIF.org 2026) for the separately assembled 32-species panel and mapped to WGSRPD3 units. We counted occurrence units outside each species' native host-resource envelope and asked how many entered the contemporary envelope after introduced host ranges were retained.

Two post-hoc nulls tested whether recovery exceeded geometric overlap expected from larger envelopes. The first preserved native-envelope size, the number of introduced-added units and the number of outside-native occurrence units, but placed added units uniformly among non-native WGSRPD3 units. The second additionally preserved the number of added units within each WGSRPD level-1 region. Aggregate null distributions used 199,999 hypergeometric draws. We then treated butterfly species as the replication unit: 99,999 region-matched null draws were used for the mean and median species-level recovery fractions, 50,000 species-cluster bootstrap resamples quantified uncertainty, and every informative species was removed in turn to assess leave-one-species-out sensitivity.

### 2.5 Secondary climate analysis

A separate pre-specified analysis tested whether broader host-family diets weaken climatic filtering within contemporary host-resource opportunity. Climate covariates were drawn from CHELSA (Karger et al. 2017, 2021), and twenty-four species passed the fixed quality criteria. Because this analysis addresses realization of opportunity rather than the paper's primary resource-redistribution question, its cross-fit design, geographic-matching sensitivities, effect-size precision and full results are reported in Supplementary Methods S1, Supplementary Table S5 and Fig. S1.

### 2.6 Out-of-time and spatial-block butterfly recording predictions

A separate panel of 225 candidate butterflies was matched to background, climate, land-cover and larval-host-resource information at species × geographic grid-cell resolution. Observation-support rules retained 155 species for the main analysis. Using earlier GBIF records (2011–2014 to 2015–2018) for the retrospective training comparison, models were evaluated against the 2019–2022 period (208,482 species × grid-cell evaluation pairs). The reference model (M3) included recording background, climatic covariates and land-cover variables; M4 added host-resource metrics and M5 additionally included host-family composition. All model comparisons used species-equal mean squared error (MSE), also checking mean absolute error. We compared M3 and M5 on withheld temporal data and in a spatially blocked evaluation, then inspected a post-hoc subset ≥100 km from model training locations (102,728 evaluation pairs). Sensitivities standardized recording opportunity to 20 days, changed the candidate radius to 250 km, excluded records from the largest provider, and restricted positional uncertainty to ≤100 m. The response is standardized opportunistic *recording probability*, not absence-adjusted occupancy or butterfly establishment; some static host-distribution knowledge may be contemporary to or postdate the evaluated butterfly records. The analysis is therefore a retrospective prediction test, not a prospective introduction experiment. Source locks, exact evaluation exclusions, bootstrap resampling and data-source details are reported in the separate archived validation protocol.

### 2.7 Exploratory island-port host and butterfly assemblage correspondence

For 51 original geographic islands with qualifying port-associated host-plant records and earlier butterfly indexing, we compared all 1,275 pairs. Candidate introduced larval-host assemblages were compiled from botanically indexed plants in 2001–2024 near mapped port locations, while new butterfly assemblages comprised species first indexed on each island in 2011–2022. We quantified pairwise Jaccard similarities, Spearman associations and descriptive partial rank correlations after removing island-pair distance and earlier butterfly-assemblage similarity; island leave-one-out analysis assessed leverage. Island pairs share sites and are not independent replicates, and the pooled plant archive is not temporally prior to all butterfly outcomes. To test whether the more specific historical host-indexing association survived geographical controls, we separately analyzed 17 islands with 38 historical port buffers and 2001–2010 plant speciesKey records, holding butterfly species/region and island recording margins or plant geographic prevalence fixed in structural nulls. This small, distinct sample cannot establish ship transport or colonization. Data processing and source hashes are preserved in the separate island-port audit.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Human redistribution broadly expands butterfly resource geography

Introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed resource opportunity for 206 of 239 species (86.2%). Expansion occurred across the full diet-breadth spectrum: 82/97 one-family species (84.5%), 47/59 two-family species (79.7%), 44/50 species using three to five families (88.0%) and all 33 species using six or more families gained at least one resource region. It was also widespread across the five major butterfly families represented by at least 10 species, with 83.5–90.2% of Nymphalidae, Hesperiidae, Pieridae, Lycaenidae and Papilionidae expanding (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, Hesperiidae and Pieridae each had a median host-family breadth of one, yet 90.2% and 86.0% of their focal species expanded. Aggregate species × WGSRPD3 coverage increased from 26,530 under native-only host distributions to 41,083 under contemporary distributions, an addition of 14,553 units (+54.9%; Fig. 1a). Excluding FAO-defined crop hosts still yielded a 47.0% aggregate increase, or 46.0% under the conservative genus-expanded exclusion (Supplementary Table S7).

Those 14,553 added butterfly × region units were generated unevenly across host plants. Fractional attribution identified 670 contributing host species. The top 10 species accounted for 25.1% of total credit, the top 20 for 37.3% and the top 50 for 57.0%; only 38 host species were needed to reach half of all added opportunity. The plant-level Gini coefficient was 0.777 and the inverse-Herfindahl effective contributor number was 92.5 species. The largest contributors were *Medicago sativa*, *Poa pratensis*, *Oxalis corniculata*, *Cynodon dactylon* and *Dactylis glomerata*, and seven of the top ten species belonged to Poaceae. Collapsing contributing species to genera did not remove the concentration: 431 genera contributed, the top 10 genera accounted for 28.7% and the top 50 for 67.0%, 25 genera accounted for half, and the genus-level Gini coefficient was 0.774. Thus expansion had a long tail of contributors but a strongly concentrated upper tier rather than being distributed uniformly across known hosts (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table S6).

Matched-host diagnostics suggested an apparent host-identity excess under a simple same-family null, but that excess was sensitive to how candidate plants were matched on native range and weighted by their use by other Lepidoptera. Because HOSTS network degree may combine ecological prominence, database visibility and reverse causation, we treat network prominence only as a possible explanation for this sensitivity rather than as an identified mechanism; the full null grid and plant-level analysis are reported in Supplementary Information.

### 3.2 Taxonomic diet breadth poorly predicts proportional resource gain

Host-family richness was weakly related to proportional expansion across 239 species (Spearman ρ = 0.008; 49,999-bootstrap 95% CI −0.111 to 0.128), with a similar estimate in the conservative 215-species subset (ρ = 0.015; Fig. 1b). The strict exploratory ±0.10 equivalence diagnostic remained inconclusive (TOST p = 0.078). Family random-intercept and estimated-λ phylogenetic checks retained weak point estimates, although phylogenetic inference covered only 124/239 tree-matched species and a fixed Brownian sensitivity could not exclude a moderately positive association. Excluding all 58 butterflies with a Poaceae host still gave ρ = 0.026 (n = 181), so grasses did not explain the near-zero global slope. Geography-stratified and sensitivity results, including a modest positive European association, are retained in Supplementary Information; no exact null effect or equivalence is claimed.

### 3.3 Added resource geography aligns with contemporary butterfly occurrence

Thirty-one of 32 species in the separately assembled climate-stratified panel yielded contemporary GBIF records. Across the panel, 115 butterfly species × WGSRPD3 observations occurred outside native host-resource envelopes. Adding introduced host distributions recovered 66 of these units (57.4%; Fig. 2a).

Twenty-three species had at least one outside-native occurrence, and 18 recovered at least one unit after introduced hosts were added. The median species-level recovery fraction was 0.60; eight species recovered all outside-native observed units. Seven of those eight complete-recovery cases involved only 1–4 outside-native units; the exception was *Pyrgus communis*, with 22/22 units recovered.

Recovery exceeded geometric expectation at both pooled and species levels (Fig. 2b). A uniform envelope-enlargement null had median recovery of 38 units (95% interval 30–46), while a null preserving each species' broad regional distribution of added host geography had median 49 (43–56); none of 199,999 draws reached the observed 66.

Treating species rather than species × region units as the replication level gave the same conclusion. Mean species-level recovery was 0.583 versus a region-matched null median of 0.385 (95% interval 0.308–0.470), with no exceedance in 99,999 draws. A species-cluster bootstrap placed the pooled recovery fraction between 0.349 and 0.774 (95% percentile interval).

*Pyrgus communis* contributed 22 of the 66 recovered units, but removing it still left 44/93 units recovered (47.3%) versus a region-matched null median of 30 (24–36), again with no exceedance in 99,999 draws. Removing *Pieris brassicae* left 62/111 (55.9%) versus a null median of 46 (40–52). Recovery was heterogeneous rather than universal: *Erynnis tristis* recovered 0/11 outside-native units and *Historis acheronta* recovered 5/13 (Supplementary Table S3).

### 3.4 Adding host-resource information improves held-out butterfly recording predictions

In the separate 155-species validation, adding host-resource and host-family predictors to the climate–land-cover background model reduced species-equal temporal test MSE from 0.032064 to 0.030743 (a **4.12% reduction**; 208,482 evaluation pairs), with improvement in 119/155 species. The gain persisted when entire spatial blocks were withheld (MSE 0.032327 to 0.031359, **3.00% reduction**) and in the post-hoc subset ≥100 km from the fitted training locations (0.033468 to 0.032552, **2.74% reduction**, 102,728 pairs; proposed Fig. 4). Each of four observation/coverage sensitivities showed the same MSE direction (reductions of 2.98%, 3.76%, 5.55% and 5.34%). MAE also decreased relative to M3 in the temporal and spatial evaluations, although M5 did not universally dominate a simple recording-persistence predictor on every error metric. In a different test holding out **butterfly species**, M5 did not improve on M3 (MSE 0.032701 versus 0.032633); the observed gains therefore concern subsequent and spatially withheld recordings of *already characterized species*, not generalized transfer to novel species. All evaluations remain retrospective proxies for recording geography.

### 3.5 Island butterflies covary spatially with port-indexed candidate larval hosts

Across 51 islands, similarity in the pooled (2001–2024) composition of candidate introduced larval hosts indexed near ports was positively correlated with similarity of butterfly species first indexed in 2011–2022 (1,275 nonindependent pairs; Spearman **ρ = 0.300**). Distance and earlier butterfly assemblage similarity-adjusted partial rank correlation remained positive (**0.215**; proposed Fig. 5), and leave-one-island-out estimates remained between 0.270 and 0.327. However, the same host-plant similarity was more strongly correlated with **previously** recorded butterfly assemblages (**ρ = 0.418**), reducing evidence that the relationship is specific to new records. A catalogue-compatible introduced-host candidate was indexed at both ports for 208/1,163 shared-new species × island-pair cases (17.9% versus an 11.9% plant-family/degree-constrained network reference), but these host records are not necessarily prior to the butterflies. In the separate 17-island historical panel, compatible plants indexed in 2001–2010 preceded 34/113 butterfly first-indexed events, yet geography- and host-index-frequency-preserving nulls did **not** support an independent increment beyond regional recording structure. The island correlations are spatial concordance among recorded plants and butterflies, not historical shipping or host-mediated establishment.

## 4. Discussion

This study identifies a resource-side route by which globalization can reorganize butterfly biogeography. Human redistribution of larval host plants increased reconstructed butterfly resource opportunity by 54.9% and expanded the envelope of more than 86% of resource-eligible species, yet proportional gain was only weakly related to family-level diet breadth. The added geography was instead strongly structured by the resources themselves: 38 of 670 contributing host species accounted for half of all added butterfly × region opportunity. A secondary occurrence analysis further showed that introduced hosts recovered 57.4% of contemporary species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes. Together, these results separate **how broadly a butterfly feeds** from **where human activity has made its larval resources available**.

### 4.1 Partner redistribution changes what consumer specialization means geographically

Diet specialization and the geography of accessible host resources are distinct traits of a butterfly's contemporary environment. Under fixed larval-host identities, 84.5% of one-family butterflies still gained resource geography when introduced ranges of their known hosts were included. The 54.9% aggregate increase was not a simple consequence of a few cultivated crop species: removing FAO-listed crop hosts retained 46–47% of the inferred gain. Its contribution was nonetheless concentrated on the plant side, with 38 of 670 hosts accounting for half the newly mapped opportunity. A specialist can therefore remain taxonomically specialized while gaining extensive *potential* geographic opportunity because humans redistribute the few plants that it can use. Regional variation, unresolved phylogenetic coverage and the nearly—but not conclusively—null association between host-family breadth and proportional gain all limit interpretation of specialization as a single global slope. The source-host network prominence diagnostics were sensitive to source visibility and matching assumptions, and did not establish a host-selection mechanism.

### 4.2 Added resource geography is reflected in contemporary occurrence

A resource-envelope analysis can otherwise remain purely potential. This secondary occurrence comparison provides a bridge to realized biogeography.

More than half of species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes were brought inside the envelope by adding introduced host ranges. Crucially, the result persisted when butterfly species were treated as the replication unit and when the largest-contributing species was removed. The validation therefore cannot be reduced to the fact that contemporary envelopes are larger or to a single high-leverage butterfly. This does not prove that the recorded butterfly used the introduced host at that locality, nor that host introduction caused colonization, but it demonstrates that a native-only view of larval resources can miss contemporary geographic opportunities that coincide with butterfly presence.

That distinction matters for butterfly macroecology. When larval host plants have been redistributed, native-only host maps can create apparent butterfly–host mismatches that are partly artifacts of treating present-day resource opportunity as if plant geography were still native.

### 4.3 Predictive value and the island interface

The source-side redistribution result becomes ecologically more informative when host-resource geography helps predict observed consumers. Adding host features improved later recording-probability predictions after climatic and land-cover features were fitted, with smaller but consistent gains under spatial blocking and at ≥100 km from model training locations. This is an empirical information contribution, not a claim that plants indexed at a port specifically caused the forecast gain. Failure to improve predictions for held-out butterfly species limits transfer beyond taxa with characterized larval-host associations. Nor are contemporary WCVP geographic statuses a pre-2019 inventory of actual host abundance.

The island analysis gives a spatially explicit, but weaker, observation of the same resource–consumer interface: islands whose port surroundings share more candidate introduced larval hosts also share more butterfly recording taxa. The positive association remains after descriptive controls for distance and prior butterfly community, but the old-butterfly association is stronger, the pooled plant records may postdate butterfly records, and stricter historical geographic nulls remove apparent added host-specific excess. Thus the island analysis demonstrates an **observed spatial correspondence** between redistributed-resource maps and butterfly recording assemblages; it does not identify transport pathways, establish local host use or measure the fraction of modelled resource gain realized via harbor-mediated colonization.

### 4.4 Implications for invasion ecology, conservation and global-change biogeography

Globalization can alter a consumer's apparent biogeographic constraints by changing its interaction partners rather than its own diet. This makes larval-host distribution a relevant part of range-distribution models, interpretation of range shifts and assessments of changes in ecological opportunity. Yet host geographic availability should not be equated with resource quality: introduced plants may be suitable, poor quality or ecological traps, and our data contain neither standardized feeding trials nor fitness estimates (Yoon & Read 2016; Braga 2023). Moreover, ports are plausible sites of both plant arrival and contrasting land-use conditions, so port proximity need not imply increased larval habitat. Conservation inferences should therefore separate map-based opportunity, actual host use, sustained reproduction and possible ecological costs. Independent historical invasion dates and matched port/nonport observations are needed before distinguishing transport-mediated host provisioning from older butterfly biogeography, climate and observation effort.

## 5. Limitations

Our measured outcomes differ in both geography and meaning: the 239-species native–contemporary envelope estimates *potential* regional host-resource opportunity; the 32-species observation-recovery panel measures presence-side overlap; the 155-species prediction test estimates held-out standardized GBIF recording probabilities; and the 51-island analysis compares nonindependent assemblage similarity from port-area plant records and opportunistic butterfly indexing. The denominators must not be pooled or used to estimate an island-specific fraction of the globally added 54.9% opportunity.

HOSTS interaction data are incomplete and uneven across regions. WCVP statuses identify contemporary introduced ranges at WGSRPD3 rather than the time and precise location of introduction, feeding or local abundance. The main butterfly source samples differ in taxonomic and geographic coverage, including 124 phylogeny-matched species. The temporal forecast uses static host geography which might include distribution knowledge acquired after some test outcomes; evaluation is retrospective rather than genuinely prospective. A new-species transfer test did not show improvement, and GBIF non-detection is not ecological absence. Bootstrap intervals fix fitted predictions and do not include refitting uncertainty.

At island ports, mapped 2019 port coordinates are retrospective spatial proxies and botanically indexed records pooled over 2001–2024 can postdate butterflies. The 51 islands were selected partly by botanical recording and earlier butterflies; the 1,275 pairs share islands and should not be treated as independent replicates. Previous butterfly communities covary more strongly with hosts than new communities do. In a separate smaller 17-island 2001–2010 botanical analysis, controlling plant geographic prevalence and butterfly/island recording margins removed an apparent extra host matching signal. We therefore present positive island similarity as **spatial association**, not a demonstrated historical port effect or stepping-stone process. Plant abundance, historical non-native status, shipping routes, local larval feeding, persistence and breeding remain unmeasured.

## 6. Conclusions

Holding butterfly larval-host identities fixed, contemporary introductions of their known plants add **14,553 butterfly × region resource units**, expanding reconstructed resource opportunity by **54.9%** across 239 butterflies. Resource expansion crosses taxonomic diet-breadth classes and is concentrated on a subset of redistributed plants rather than consistently scaling with host-family richness. A separate occurrence-envelope analysis recovers 66/115 butterfly observations outside native host resources when introduced host geography is included.

Crucially, the geography of those resources also contains predictive information: models including host resources and family composition reduce retrospective held-out butterfly recording errors beyond climate and land cover (4.12% temporal; 3.00% spatial). At island ports, candidate introduced-host composition additionally covaries with butterfly assemblage similarity (51 islands, ρ = 0.300), even after descriptive distance and prior-community adjustment. Those associations do not demonstrate that hosts were shipped to the islands or drove colonization; geographically matched historical analyses give no independent port-index effect.

More broadly, **the redistribution of consumers' existing interaction partners changes their mapped resource opportunity and adds information about their recorded biogeography**. Distinguishing the availability of resources from their utilization and demographic consequences is essential before interpreting the geographic reorganization of biotic interactions as a change in consumer population range.

## Data and Code Availability

Analysis code and the inputs required to reproduce the reported results and figures are versioned in the study repository. Public source datasets include LepTraits, HOSTS, WCVP, GBIF, CHELSA and WGSRPD. The secondary occurrence validation used 53,434 GBIF occurrence records. An exact-ID archival download requested those same records (https://doi.org/10.15468/dl.pp5nc9); GBIF returned 53,144 records, indicating that 290 records used in the frozen analysis were no longer returned by GBIF at archival download time. Exact source identities, retrieval rules, robustness-analysis specifications and execution records are documented in the supplementary reproducibility materials rather than the main text. A stable public archival snapshot of the analysis package will be deposited before publication.
