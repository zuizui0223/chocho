# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography

**Working draft v0.2**

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Research Article, **“Human redistribution of host plants expands butterfly resource geography largely independently of diet breadth”** for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

Global change redistributes interacting species as well as climate, yet consumer biogeography is often interpreted against static or native-only resource templates. We ask how human redistribution of larval host plants changes butterfly resource opportunity, whether broad taxonomic generalists benefit disproportionately, and whether contemporary introduced-host geography aligns with butterfly occurrence.

Across 239 butterflies, introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed larval-resource opportunity for 206 species and increased aggregate species × WGSRPD3 coverage from 26,530 to 41,083 units (+54.9%). A unit-preserving decomposition showed that this added geography was strongly uneven across 670 contributing host species: the top 10 accounted for 25.1% of added butterfly × region opportunity, the top 50 for 57.0%, and 38 species together accounted for half. The same pattern survived taxonomic aggregation: 25 of 431 host genera accounted for half of all added opportunity. Proportional expansion was nevertheless nearly unrelated to host-family breadth (Spearman rho = 0.008; bootstrap 95% CI -0.111 to 0.128). A strict post-hoc ±0.10 equivalence test was narrowly inconclusive (TOST p = 0.078), so the manuscript explicitly avoids an exact-null claim.

As a secondary analysis, we reused the 32-species panel originally stratified for the climate test. Introduced host ranges recovered 66 of 115 butterfly species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes. Species-level recovery exceeded a region-matched null, and the conclusion remained after excluding the largest contributor, *Pyrgus communis*. We interpret this as occurrence-side evidence that contemporary host redistribution matters for realized butterfly geography, not as proof of local larval use or causal range expansion.

Matched-host analyses showed that a simple host-identity excess is sensitive to how plant network prominence is represented. We therefore do not make network prominence or butterfly-specific host choice a headline mechanism. Instead, those post-hoc diagnostics are reported in Supplementary Information and discussed as possible explanations whose causal direction and recording biases remain unresolved.

We believe the manuscript fits *Global Ecology and Biogeography* because it shows that anthropogenic redistribution of interacting species changes the geography of ecological opportunity itself. The central result is simple and concrete: human movement of host plants has greatly expanded butterfly resource geography across the specialization spectrum, while a relatively concentrated subset of redistributed hosts supplies much of the added opportunity and broad diet breadth provides little information about proportional gain. A secondary occurrence analysis then links that reconstructed opportunity to contemporary butterfly distributions.

The analyses use public trait, interaction, plant-distribution, occurrence, phylogenetic and climate data. Analysis code, fixed source identities, robustness analyses and result records are versioned for reproducibility; archival public metadata and the anonymized reviewer-access package will be finalized for submission.

This manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors will approve the submitted version and agree to be accountable for the work.
