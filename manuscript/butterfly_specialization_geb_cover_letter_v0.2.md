Please consider our Research Article, **“Plant globalization expands and homogenizes butterfly larval-resource geography”**, for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

This manuscript addresses a broad ecological consequence of plant globalization that is not captured by changes in species richness alone: human redistribution of larval hosts can change both the amount and the structure of consumer resource space. Holding known butterfly–host identities fixed for 239 butterflies, introduced host ranges increased aggregate butterfly × region opportunity by 54.9%. More importantly, mean similarity among regional butterfly resource assemblages increased from 0.277 to 0.462 (+66.8%) and pairwise overlap among butterfly resource envelopes from 0.222 to 0.328 (+47.4%); both exceeded fixed-margin null expectations that preserved the amount of opportunity added per butterfly and region (p = 0.002). Exact shared-host butterfly-pair × region units increased by 127.1%, with 920 of 986 host-sharing pairs gaining new shared-resource regions. A secondary occurrence analysis further recovered 66/115 outside-native butterfly × region observations. Together, the results identify a dual ecological consequence of partner redistribution: resource release for individual consumers and resource homogenization among consumers and regions. The manuscript explicitly distinguishes this potential shared-resource exposure from realized competition and uses host-removal simulations only as a coarse network-dependency stress test.

The manuscript uses public trait, interaction, plant-distribution, occurrence, phylogenetic and climate data, with fixed source identities and reproducible analysis receipts. An anonymized data-and-code reproducibility archive is supplied with the submission as supplementary review material. We distinguish reconstructed resource opportunity from realized occupancy and do not infer that host introduction caused butterfly range expansion.
