Please consider our Research Article, **“Anthropogenic host redistribution expands butterfly resource geography across the specialization spectrum”**, for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

This manuscript addresses a broad-scale biogeographic consequence of global change that is largely missed when specialization is treated only as a property of the consumer: human activity also redistributes the interaction partners that define ecological opportunity. We hold known butterfly–host identities fixed and compare native-only with contemporary host-resource geography for 239 butterflies. Introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed resource geography for 206 species (86.2%) and increased aggregate butterfly × region coverage by 54.9%; the increase remained 47.0% after exact FAO crop-host exclusion and 46.0% under a deliberately conservative genus-expanded exclusion. Yet family-level diet breadth was only weakly related to proportional gain (Spearman rho = 0.008; bootstrap 95% CI -0.111 to 0.128). Instead, added opportunity was strongly concentrated among redistributed hosts: 38 of 670 host species accounted for half of all added units. A secondary occurrence analysis further showed that introduced-host geography recovered 66 of 115 butterfly × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes, beyond region-matched structural expectations. Together, these results identify **partner redistribution as a distinct pathway by which globalization can reorganize consumer biogeography without requiring host switching or broader taxonomic diets**. The pattern spans the major butterfly families in our dataset and motivates a general distinction between interaction breadth, interaction-partner geography and realized consumer geography.

The manuscript uses public trait, interaction, plant-distribution, occurrence, phylogenetic and climate data, with fixed source identities and reproducible analysis receipts. We distinguish reconstructed resource opportunity from realized occupancy and do not infer that host introduction caused butterfly range expansion.
