# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography

**Working draft v0.2**

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Research Article, **“Anthropogenic expansion of butterfly resource geography is concentrated in network-prominent host plants”** for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

Global change redistributes interacting species as well as climate, yet consumer biogeography is often interpreted against static or native-only resource templates. We ask how human redistribution of larval host plants changes butterfly resource opportunity, whether broad taxonomic generalists benefit disproportionately, which plant-level properties account for the apparent expansion signal, and whether contemporary introduced-host geography aligns with butterfly occurrence.

Across 239 butterflies, introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed larval-resource opportunity for 206 species and increased aggregate species × WGSRPD3 coverage from 26,530 to 41,083 units (+54.9%). This proportional gain was nearly unrelated to host-family breadth and remained so after finite-area, regional, random-effect and species-level phylogenetic sensitivities.

A sequence of matched null models sharpened the biological interpretation. A simple same-family null suggested that actual host identities generated unusually large anthropogenic expansion. That signal persisted after matching plant native-range breadth alone. However, it disappeared when alternative hosts were simultaneously matched on native-range breadth and weighted by their use by other Lepidoptera in HOSTS: observed total added units were 12,853 versus a null median of 12,971 (p = 0.584), while mean and median log expansion likewise fell within the combined null (p = 0.073 and 0.079). We therefore do not claim that butterfly host choice independently targets unusually globalized plants. Instead, a complementary analysis of 8,909 host plants showed that network degree remained positively associated with geographic expansion after controlling native breadth and plant-family structure (within-stratum rank r = 0.295; 0/4,999 permuted correlations as extreme; Monte Carlo p < 0.001). Anthropogenic resource expansion is thus concentrated in plants already prominent across the broader Lepidoptera–host network, with the important caveat that network prominence also reflects recording intensity.

As a secondary analysis, we reused the 32-species panel originally stratified for the climate test to ask whether contemporary host geography is relevant to butterfly distributions. Introduced host ranges recovered 66 of 115 butterfly species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes. Species-level recovery remained above region-matched null expectation after species resampling and after excluding the largest contributor, *Pyrgus communis*. We interpret this as occurrence-side validation of contemporary resource opportunity, not as proof of local larval use or causal host-facilitated range expansion.

A smaller independently specified climate analysis is retained as a secondary result. Climate-associated filtering remained evident after geographic matching, whereas the pre-specified prediction that broad host-family diets weaken filtering was not supported and was imprecisely estimated.

We believe the manuscript fits *Global Ecology and Biogeography* because it links two macroecological structures that are usually studied separately: the topology of consumer–resource networks and the anthropogenic redistribution of resource species. Previous work has identified plant lineages that support disproportionate Lepidoptera diversity; here we show that plant-level network prominence also tracks the geographic expansion of host resources under human redistribution. The results show that human movement of host plants has greatly expanded potential butterfly resource geography, but that the magnitude of this change is better explained by plant-level host prominence and biogeography than by broad butterfly diets. Independent occurrence validation further shows that contemporary host redistribution matters for where butterflies are observed, while additional filters limit realization.

The manuscript also reports an important robustness correction. An initially striking specialist–generalist contrast in host-contribution concentration largely disappears after exact host-species richness is held constant, and the initial host-identity excess disappears under the stricter host-prominence null. We retain both corrections explicitly because they define the defensible ecological interpretation.

The analyses use public trait, interaction, plant-distribution, occurrence, phylogenetic and climate data. Analysis code, fixed source identities, robustness analyses and result records are versioned for reproducibility; archival public metadata and the anonymized reviewer-access package will be finalized for submission.

This manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors will approve the submitted version and agree to be accountable for the work.
