# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography

**Working draft v0.2**

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Research Article, **“Anthropogenic host redistribution expands butterfly resource geography across the specialization spectrum”** for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

Global-change biogeography usually asks how focal species respond to climate, dispersal barriers or changing interactions. Yet human activity also redistributes the interaction partners themselves. Our manuscript addresses this resource-side problem by separating **taxonomic interaction breadth**—what a butterfly can eat—from **consumer-specific resource geography**—where those known hosts occur. Earlier studies have shown that broader host use can accompany butterfly range expansion, that host availability can constrain distributions, and that species introductions homogenize biotas and interaction networks. We ask the mirror-image question: what happens when known butterfly–host links are held fixed analytically, but the hosts' geography changes?

We reconstruct paired native-only and contemporary host-resource envelopes for 239 butterflies. Introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed resource geography for 206 species (86.2%) and increased aggregate butterfly × WGSRPD3 coverage by 54.9%. Expansion occurred in every diet-breadth class and in 83.5–90.2% of species within each of the five major butterfly families represented by at least 10 species. This added opportunity was strongly concentrated: among 670 contributing host species, the top 10 accounted for 25.1% and only 38 species accounted for half; the pattern persisted after aggregation to host genera. Family-level diet breadth poorly predicted proportional gain (Spearman rho = 0.008; bootstrap 95% CI -0.111 to 0.128), and removing all 58 butterflies with any resolved Poaceae host left the association near zero (rho = 0.026). Thus globally redistributed grasses contribute substantially to the amount of new opportunity without creating the weak diet-breadth slope.

As a secondary occurrence analysis, introduced-host geography recovered 66 of 115 butterfly species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes, more than expected under region-matched structural nulls and after removal of the largest-contributing species. We interpret this as evidence that contemporary partner geography is relevant to realized butterfly distributions, not as proof of local larval use or causal range expansion.

The biological contribution is first about butterflies: larval host-family breadth and the geography of larval resources are not interchangeable dimensions of specialization under contemporary plant redistribution. One-family butterflies can gain extensive geographic opportunity when their hosts are widely transported, whereas broader diets do not guarantee a larger proportional gain. The broader conceptual implication follows from that butterfly result: **moving interaction partners can reorganize consumer biogeography even when taxonomic interaction breadth is held constant analytically**. Because cultivation, trade and invasion redistribute resources across many interaction systems, the same logic may extend beyond butterflies.

All analyses use public trait, interaction, plant-distribution, occurrence, phylogenetic and climate data. The code, fixed source identities, result receipts and review-package builders are versioned for reproducibility. The manuscript explicitly distinguishes reconstructed resource opportunity from realized occupancy and treats causal mechanism analyses conservatively.

This manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. All authors will approve the submitted version and agree to be accountable for the work.
