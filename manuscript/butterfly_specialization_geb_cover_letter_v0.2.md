# Cover letter — Global Ecology and Biogeography

**Working draft v0.2**

Dear Editors,

Please consider our Research Article, **“Anthropogenic host redistribution expands butterfly resource geography beyond native-host distributions,”** for publication in *Global Ecology and Biogeography*.

Global change redistributes species interactions as well as climate. For herbivorous insects, human transport of host plants can alter the geography of resource opportunity without changing the consumer’s taxonomic diet breadth. We ask how widespread that change is across butterflies, whether broad family-level generalists gain disproportionately, and whether the reconstructed opportunity added by introduced hosts corresponds to independent contemporary butterfly occurrences.

Across 239 butterflies, retaining introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed larval-resource opportunity for 206 species and increased aggregate species × region coverage from 26,530 to 41,083 units (+54.9%). Proportional expansion was essentially unrelated to host-family breadth (Spearman rho = 0.008), and that result persisted after accounting for starting resource breadth, butterfly Family, and regional composition of the dataset.

The key independent validation uses a separately assembled 32-species occurrence panel. Among 115 butterfly species × WGSRPD3 observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes, introduced host ranges recovered 66 (57.4%). This recovery exceeded not only an envelope-size null but also a stricter null preserving the broad regional placement of newly added host-resource units (null mean 49.3, 95% interval 43–56; p = 5 × 10^-6). Thus the contemporary resource reconstruction aligns with butterfly presence more strongly than expected from simply enlarging geographic envelopes.

We then ask what remains unrealized after resources are geographically available. In 24 independently evaluated species, climate-associated filtering was common within contemporary host-resource opportunity (median score 0.801; 23/24 species above the neutral value) and persisted after matching observed and never-observed resource units by geographic distance. A pre-specified prediction that broader host-family diets weaken this filtering was not supported (partial rho = -0.166, one-sided p = 0.2237) and is reported with its broad bootstrap uncertainty rather than interpreted as equivalence.

We believe the manuscript fits *Global Ecology and Biogeography* because it connects three macroecological layers that are usually studied separately: interaction breadth, anthropogenic redistribution of biotic resources, and realized consumer geography. The main result is not simply that introduced hosts enlarge potential ranges, but that this added resource geography is independently aligned with contemporary butterfly occurrence beyond structural overlap expectations, while additional environmental filtering remains.

The manuscript also reports an important robustness correction. An initially striking specialist–generalist contrast in host-contribution concentration largely disappears after exact host-species richness is held constant, so we explicitly demote that pattern from mechanism to structural diagnostic. We view this as strengthening the paper by separating biological signal from index geometry.

All analyses use public or previously released trait, interaction, plant-distribution, occurrence and climate data. A permanent archival snapshot and final DOI will be supplied before submission, while double-anonymous review materials will use a de-identified access route.
