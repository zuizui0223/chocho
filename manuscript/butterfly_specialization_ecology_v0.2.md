# Anthropogenic host redistribution expands butterfly resource geography across the specialization spectrum

**Working manuscript v0.2 — 2026-09-28**

## Abstract

**Aim:** To quantify how redistribution of known larval hosts changes butterfly geographic resource opportunity while host identities are held fixed, whether added opportunity is concentrated among hosts or predicted by taxonomic diet breadth, and whether it aligns with contemporary butterfly occurrence.

**Location:** Global.

**Time period:** Contemporary host distributions; butterfly occurrences 2010–2026.

**Major taxa studied:** Butterflies and larval host plants.

**Methods:** Using a paired native-to-contemporary resource-envelope reconstruction for 239 butterflies, we held known host identities fixed while allowing their geographic distributions to change, decomposed added butterfly × region opportunity across contributing hosts, and tested host-family breadth against proportional expansion using geographic, taxonomic and phylogenetic sensitivities. A separately assembled 32-species climate-stratified panel was reused for secondary occurrence validation.

**Results:** Introduced hosts expanded reconstructed resource geography for 206/239 butterflies (86.2%) and increased aggregate species × region coverage by 54.9%. Contributions were strongly uneven: among 670 host species, the top 10 accounted for 25.1% of added units and 38 species accounted for half; genus-level aggregation retained this concentration. Host-family breadth was nearly unrelated to proportional expansion (Spearman rho = 0.008; bootstrap 95% CI -0.111 to 0.128), although a strict post-hoc ±0.10 equivalence test was narrowly inconclusive (p = 0.078). Introduced hosts recovered 66/115 outside-native species × region observations (57.4%), exceeding region-matched structural expectation.

**Main conclusions:** Human redistribution of known host plants expands butterfly resource opportunity across the specialization spectrum. Much of the gain is carried by a minority of hosts and is poorly predicted by family-level diet breadth. More generally, partner redistribution can reorganize consumer resource geography without invoking host switching or diet expansion; vulnerability and opportunity under globalization therefore depend not only on how specialized a consumer is, but also on which partners it uses and how those partners have been redistributed.

**Keywords:** biotic redistribution, butterflies, ecological specialization, global change, host plants, introduced species, resource geography

## 1. Introduction

In butterflies, larval host specialization is a defining ecological axis. Comparative studies usually express it as a property of the butterfly: how many host taxa it uses, how phylogenetically diverse those hosts are, or how host breadth covaries with the butterfly's own geographic range. Broader diets have been linked to larger ranges and to range-shift dynamics, while recent global work has identified climate and regional plant richness as drivers of larval diet specialization (Slove & Janz 2011; Lancaster 2020; Gross et al. 2026). These approaches establish that **what a butterfly can eat** matters for biogeography. They do not, however, specify **where those larval resources occur**.

That distinction becomes important under global change because interaction partners are redistributed independently. Humans have naturalized more than 13,000 plant species outside their native ranges, and these movements have measurably homogenized regional floras worldwide (van Kleunen et al. 2015; Daru et al. 2021). For butterflies, this plant globalization changes not only floral composition but the geography of potential larval resources. A butterfly restricted to one plant family may use a host that humans have spread across continents, whereas a taxonomic generalist may depend on many hosts whose distributions remain geographically limited. Human transport can therefore change the geographic amount of resource opportunity without changing the butterfly's taxonomic diet breadth at all. Regional studies already show that introduced plants can become larval hosts and sometimes accompany butterfly range expansion or longer flight seasons (Graves & Shapiro 2003). Species-level range-shift studies also show the complementary route in which the butterfly changes: exploiting more widespread hosts or reduced host specialization can accompany butterfly expansion (Braschler & Hill 2007; Neu et al. 2021). Our question is the mirror image. We hold the butterfly's known host list fixed and ask what happens when the **hosts themselves are redistributed**. Host-range studies further show that plant availability can constrain butterfly distributions, particularly for specialists, but typically treat host geography as a current or future constraint rather than ask how anthropogenic plant redistribution has already altered that geography (Rashid et al. 2026).

A second literature documents redistribution of butterflies and other Lepidoptera themselves. Global syntheses show widespread contemporary butterfly range shifts, while non-native Lepidoptera are now established worldwide and global trade produces strongly asymmetric flows among regions (Chowdhury et al. 2026; Couto et al. 2026). More broadly, species introductions can homogenize the composition of ecological interaction networks across regions (Fricke & Svenning 2020). These results establish that globalization changes both species distributions and interaction structure. They do not answer the butterfly-specific geographic question posed here: **how much does moving known larval host plants change where a butterfly has access to resources?** What remains unresolved is this **resource-side biogeography of butterfly specialization under globalization**—the magnitude of anthropogenic change in butterfly resource geography, whether conventional specialization metrics capture that change, and whether the added opportunity is diffusely distributed across hosts or carried by a small set of highly redistributed plants. From an invasion-ecology perspective, this asks how redistribution of alien plants can propagate through pre-existing trophic links to alter the geographic opportunity of resident consumers, rather than evaluating alien plants only by their own ranges or direct community effects.

Butterflies provide an unusually tractable system for addressing this gap because global host-use data can be linked to plant native and introduced distributions. Our unit of inference is therefore not the composition of a local interaction network but the **butterfly-specific resource envelope**: for each butterfly, we project the distributions of its known hosts into geographic opportunity and contrast the envelope implied by native host ranges with the envelope available after introduced ranges are retained. This paired reconstruction separates two quantities that are often implicitly conflated: **taxonomic specialization** (how broadly a butterfly feeds) and **resource geography** (where its known hosts occur). If human redistribution substantially expands resource geography even for narrow-diet butterflies, then specialist–generalist labels alone will not predict how ecological opportunity has been reorganized by globalization.

Here we ask three questions. First, how strongly do introduced host distributions expand butterfly resource geography, and is that added geography spread across many host plants or concentrated in a small subset? Second, does proportional resource gain increase with family-level diet breadth, or can narrow-diet species gain comparable geographic opportunity when their hosts are widely redistributed? Third, does contemporary introduced-host geography recover butterfly occurrences that fall outside native host-resource envelopes more often than structural overlap predicts? Matched-host and plant-prominence analyses are used only as post-hoc diagnostics of possible host-identity and data-structure effects. A smaller pre-specified climate analysis is retained in Supplementary Information as a secondary test of filtering between resource opportunity and realization.

We expected anthropogenic host redistribution to enlarge resource opportunity across much of the specialization spectrum. The key alternative was whether this gain would scale with taxonomic diet breadth or instead reveal a decoupling between the number of host lineages a butterfly uses and the geographic opportunity created when those hosts are moved by people. We also expected contemporary host geography to recover at least some butterfly occurrences missed by a native-only resource envelope.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Conceptual design: larval host breadth, resource geography and butterfly occurrence

We organized the analysis around three related but non-equivalent quantities. **Taxonomic interaction breadth** describes which kinds of larval hosts a butterfly uses, measured here as host-family richness. **Resource geography** describes where those known hosts occur, represented by the union of their geographic distributions. **Realized butterfly geography** describes where the butterfly is actually observed. Human redistribution enters between the first two quantities: moving a known host can alter a butterfly's resource geography without changing its taxonomic interaction breadth.

We therefore first reconstructed each butterfly's resource geography under native-only and contemporary host distributions and measured the difference as anthropogenic resource gain. We then asked whether proportional gain scales with taxonomic diet breadth, which host plants account for the added geography, and whether the contemporary envelope recovers butterfly occurrences missed by the native-only envelope. The climate analysis provides a secondary test of filtering between geographic opportunity and realization.

The LepTraits-derived descriptor panel contained 339 species (Shirey et al. 2022). Resource analyses used 239 species with positive host-family breadth and at least one reconstructed native host-resource WGSRPD3 unit. A conservative subset of 215 species also required the number of resolved host species to equal or exceed LepTraits host-family count. A separate 32-species panel was used for occurrence validation and climate analysis; it had been selected to balance the subsequent climate test, not optimized for validation. Twenty-four species passed the climate quality criteria.

The resource reconstruction is descriptive and uses fixed LepTraits, HOSTS and WCVP inputs. The 32-species panel was assembled separately for the climate test, using eligibility and analysis rules fixed before its ecological responses were evaluated; the occurrence analysis is a secondary use of that panel. The host-contribution decomposition, equivalence diagnostic and later null-model, ceiling, phylogenetic and geographic sensitivities are identified as post-hoc analyses.

### 2.2 Reconstructing native and contemporary resource geography

Larval host records came from HOSTS and plant taxonomy and distributions from WCVP (Robinson et al. 2023; Govaerts et al. 2021). Species-level host names were resolved to accepted WCVP taxa. Under WCVP terminology, introduced distributions are wild non-native records (including casual, naturalised and invasive occurrences), not cultivation records; plants that are only cultivated or merely planted without self-reproduction are not classified as introduced.

For each butterfly, the **native resource envelope** was the union of WGSRPD level-3 units in which any resolved host was native, extant and non-doubtful. The **contemporary resource envelope** additionally retained introduced host distributions. Host identities were held fixed between the two envelopes, so their difference isolates redistribution of already-known resources rather than host switching or expansion of the butterfly's recorded diet. WGSRPD3 is a coarse regional framework (Brummitt et al. 2001), so these envelopes represent geographic resource opportunity rather than local host occupancy.

As a crop-host sensitivity, we repeated the reconstruction after excluding host taxa listed in the FAO Indicative Crop Classification, first by exact botanical binomial and then with an intentionally over-conservative rule that also excluded all congeners when the FAO list specified a crop as spp. Host-family breadth was kept fixed as the specialization predictor; only the reconstructed resource envelopes and host contributions were recalculated (Supplementary Table S7).

We calculated native and contemporary resource breadth, introduced-added units, the contemporary/native ratio, log resource expansion, and the introduced share of contemporary geography. In a post-hoc descriptive decomposition, each butterfly × WGSRPD3 unit newly added by introduced host distributions contributed one unit of credit divided equally among the host species that added that unit. Credits therefore summed exactly to the aggregate number of added butterfly × region units. We summarized the concentration of those credits across host plants using cumulative top-k shares, the Gini coefficient and the inverse-Herfindahl effective contributor number. As a taxonomic sensitivity, contributing plant species were first collapsed to unique genera within each butterfly × region unit and the unit credit was then divided equally among those genera. This decomposition is explicitly contribution-weighted: a plant can accumulate credit by being used by more focal butterflies, by adding more geographic units, or both. We therefore use it to describe which resources generate the reconstructed opportunity, not as a sample of independent plant-level effects.

### 2.3 Robustness analyses for host identity, specialization and taxonomic structure

We used matched-host nulls to test whether apparent host-identity effects could arise from host richness, plant-family composition, starting native range and the prominence of candidate plants in HOSTS. Because conclusions about host-identity excess were sensitive to how strongly network prominence was weighted, we do not interpret these nulls as identifying a butterfly-specific host-choice mechanism; the full parameter grid is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

We tested whether the host-family-breadth association with proportional expansion was sensitive to the finite 369-unit WGSRPD3 support, geographic composition and taxonomic non-independence. Post-hoc analyses progressively excluded broad native resource envelopes, repeated associations within dominant WGSRPD level-1 regions, fitted butterfly Family as a random intercept, and used the Kawahara et al. (2023) time-calibrated butterfly phylogeny for exact species matches. As a descriptive check on whether resource expansion was confined to one butterfly lineage, we also summarized the fraction of species with any introduced-host expansion within each LepTraits butterfly Family and interpreted only families represented by at least 10 focal species. For the phylogenetic sensitivity we report both a fixed Brownian model (lambda = 1) and a Pagel-lambda model in which residual phylogenetic covariance is estimated from the data. Because the estimated lambda was close to zero, we treat the estimated-lambda model as the data-adaptive phylogenetic correction and the fixed Brownian model as a boundary sensitivity rather than privileging lambda = 1.

An exploratory plant-level analysis related HOSTS consumer degree to plant geographic expansion across 8,909 HOSTS-WCVP plants (Supplementary Table S2). Because HOSTS includes moths as well as butterflies, this is a Lepidoptera-wide network measure rather than a butterfly-specific trait. We treat it only as a possible explanatory axis because degree may reflect ecological host prominence, study intensity, and reverse causation if introduced plants acquire additional consumer records after redistribution.

Within-butterfly portfolio-concentration metrics were retained only as a secondary structural diagnostic because effective contributor number and maximum single-host share are bounded by host number. This diagnostic is distinct from the across-plant concentration of total added opportunity described above.

### 2.4 Secondary occurrence validation

Butterfly occurrences for 2010–2026 were obtained from GBIF (GBIF.org 2026) for the separately assembled 32-species panel and mapped to WGSRPD3 units. We counted occurrence units outside each species' native host-resource envelope and asked how many entered the contemporary envelope after introduced host ranges were retained.

Two post-hoc nulls tested whether recovery exceeded geometric overlap expected from larger envelopes. The first preserved native-envelope size, the number of introduced-added units and the number of outside-native occurrence units, but placed added units uniformly among non-native WGSRPD3 units. The second additionally preserved the number of added units within each WGSRPD level-1 region. Aggregate null distributions used 199,999 hypergeometric draws. We then treated butterfly species as the replication unit: 99,999 region-matched null draws were used for the mean and median species-level recovery fractions, 50,000 species-cluster bootstrap resamples quantified uncertainty, and every informative species was removed in turn to assess leave-one-species-out sensitivity.

### 2.5 Secondary climate analysis

A separate pre-specified analysis tested whether broader host-family diets weaken climatic filtering within contemporary host-resource opportunity. Climate covariates were drawn from CHELSA (Karger et al. 2017, 2021), and twenty-four species passed the fixed quality criteria. Because this analysis addresses realization of opportunity rather than the paper's primary resource-redistribution question, its cross-fit design, geographic-matching sensitivities, effect-size precision and full results are reported in Supplementary Methods S1, Supplementary Table S5 and Fig. S1.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Human redistribution broadly expands butterfly resource geography

Introduced host distributions expanded reconstructed resource opportunity for 206 of 239 species (86.2%). Expansion occurred across the full diet-breadth spectrum: 82/97 one-family species (84.5%), 47/59 two-family species (79.7%), 44/50 species using three to five families (88.0%) and all 33 species using six or more families gained at least one resource region. It was also widespread across the five major butterfly families represented by at least 10 species, with 83.5–90.2% of Nymphalidae, Hesperiidae, Pieridae, Lycaenidae and Papilionidae expanding (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, Hesperiidae and Pieridae each had a median host-family breadth of one, yet 90.2% and 86.0% of their focal species expanded. Aggregate species × WGSRPD3 coverage increased from 26,530 under native-only host distributions to 41,083 under contemporary distributions, an addition of 14,553 units (+54.9%; Fig. 1a). Excluding FAO-defined crop hosts still yielded a 47.0% aggregate increase, or 46.0% under the conservative genus-expanded exclusion (Supplementary Table S7).

Those 14,553 added butterfly × region units were generated unevenly across host plants. Fractional attribution identified 670 contributing host species. The top 10 species accounted for 25.1% of total credit, the top 20 for 37.3% and the top 50 for 57.0%; only 38 host species were needed to reach half of all added opportunity. The plant-level Gini coefficient was 0.777 and the inverse-Herfindahl effective contributor number was 92.5 species. The largest contributors were *Medicago sativa*, *Poa pratensis*, *Oxalis corniculata*, *Cynodon dactylon* and *Dactylis glomerata*, and seven of the top ten species belonged to Poaceae. Collapsing contributing species to genera did not remove the concentration: 431 genera contributed, the top 10 genera accounted for 28.7% and the top 50 for 67.0%, 25 genera accounted for half, and the genus-level Gini coefficient was 0.774. Thus expansion had a long tail of contributors but a strongly concentrated upper tier rather than being distributed uniformly across known hosts (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table S6).

Matched-host diagnostics suggested an apparent host-identity excess under a simple same-family null, but that excess was sensitive to how candidate plants were matched on native range and weighted by their use by other Lepidoptera. Because HOSTS network degree may combine ecological prominence, database visibility and reverse causation, we treat network prominence only as a possible explanation for this sensitivity rather than as an identified mechanism; the full null grid and plant-level analysis are reported in Supplementary Information.

### 3.2 Taxonomic diet breadth poorly predicts proportional resource gain

Host-family breadth was nearly unrelated to proportional expansion in the full panel (Spearman rho = 0.008; 49,999-bootstrap 95% CI -0.111 to 0.128) and conservative subset (rho = 0.015; Fig. 1b). A deliberately strict post-hoc equivalence diagnostic using |rho| < 0.10 as the equivalence region was narrowly inconclusive: the bootstrap 90% interval was -0.092 to 0.108 and the approximate Fisher-z TOST gave p = 0.078. We therefore interpret the full-panel association as small and imprecisely bounded rather than as proof of an exact zero effect.

The near-zero relationship persisted after accounting for finite map support and taxonomic structure. Controlling native resource breadth gave a rank association of 0.012. A butterfly-Family random-intercept model estimated a standardized rank effect of 0.021 (95% CI -0.111 to 0.154; p = 0.752). The Kawahara et al. (2023) tree contained exact matches for 124/239 panel species. The estimated-lambda model found very little residual phylogenetic covariance (Pagel lambda = 0.033) and a rank-PGLS effect of -0.051 (95% CI -0.234 to 0.131; p = 0.581). A fixed Brownian lambda = 1 sensitivity gave beta = 0.140 (95% CI -0.036 to 0.317; p = 0.119), leaving moderate positive effects unresolved in that selective tree-matched subset. Restricting analyses to progressively narrower native envelopes produced correlations of 0.028–0.067 (Fig. 3a).

Regional stratification likewise showed that the global result was not solely driven by Northern America. Within dominant native-resource regions, rho was -0.005 in Northern America (n = 102), 0.013 in Africa (n = 38), 0.029 in Temperate Asia (n = 24), 0.051 in Tropical Asia (n = 9) and 0.089 in Southern America (n = 32). Europe showed a modest positive association (rho = 0.233; n = 34; Fig. 3b). The prominence of redistributed grasses did not generate the global near-zero association: excluding all 58 butterflies with any resolved Poaceae host left rho = 0.026 (n = 181), and excluding only the 34 one-family Poaceae specialists left rho = 0.013 (n = 205; Supplementary Information).

### 3.3 Added resource geography aligns with contemporary butterfly occurrence

Thirty-one of 32 species in the separately assembled climate-stratified panel yielded contemporary GBIF records. Across the panel, 115 butterfly species × WGSRPD3 observations occurred outside native host-resource envelopes. Adding introduced host distributions recovered 66 of these units (57.4%; Fig. 2a).

Twenty-three species had at least one outside-native occurrence, and 18 recovered at least one unit after introduced hosts were added. The median species-level recovery fraction was 0.60; eight species recovered all outside-native observed units. Seven of those eight complete-recovery cases involved only 1–4 outside-native units; the exception was *Pyrgus communis*, with 22/22 units recovered.

Recovery exceeded geometric expectation at both pooled and species levels (Fig. 2b). A uniform envelope-enlargement null had median recovery of 38 units (95% interval 30–46), while a null preserving each species' broad regional distribution of added host geography had median 49 (43–56); none of 199,999 draws reached the observed 66.

Treating species rather than species × region units as the replication level gave the same conclusion. Mean species-level recovery was 0.583 versus a region-matched null median of 0.385 (95% interval 0.308–0.470), with no exceedance in 99,999 draws. A species-cluster bootstrap placed the pooled recovery fraction between 0.349 and 0.774 (95% percentile interval).

*Pyrgus communis* contributed 22 of the 66 recovered units, but removing it still left 44/93 units recovered (47.3%) versus a region-matched null median of 30 (24–36), again with no exceedance in 99,999 draws. Removing *Pieris brassicae* left 62/111 (55.9%) versus a null median of 46 (40–52). Recovery was heterogeneous rather than universal: *Erynnis tristis* recovered 0/11 outside-native units and *Historis acheronta* recovered 5/13 (Supplementary Table S3).

## 4. Discussion

This study identifies a resource-side route by which globalization can reorganize butterfly biogeography. Human redistribution of larval host plants increased reconstructed butterfly resource opportunity by 54.9% and expanded the envelope of more than 86% of resource-eligible species, yet proportional gain was only weakly related to family-level diet breadth. The added geography was instead strongly structured by the resources themselves: 38 of 670 contributing host species accounted for half of all added butterfly × region opportunity. A secondary occurrence analysis further showed that introduced hosts recovered 57.4% of contemporary species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes. Together, these results separate **how broadly a butterfly feeds** from **where human activity has made its larval resources available**.

### 4.1 Host redistribution changes what specialization means for butterfly biogeography

For butterflies, larval host specialization is usually treated as a species property: a one-family feeder is a specialist and a species using many plant families is a generalist. Our results show that this label does not map directly onto geographic resource opportunity once host plants have been redistributed by people. Introduced host ranges enlarged resource geography in every diet-breadth class, including 84.5% of one-family butterflies, and the pattern was widespread across Nymphalidae, Hesperiidae, Pieridae, Lycaenidae and Papilionidae, with 83.5–90.2% of species expanding in each major family. The contrast is especially clear in Hesperiidae and Pieridae: both had a median host-family breadth of one, yet most focal species gained introduced-host resource geography. Proportional gain remained only weakly related to host-family breadth after geographic, taxonomic and phylogenetic sensitivities. A butterfly can therefore remain taxonomically specialized while experiencing a large anthropogenic expansion of where its larval resources occur.

This changes the ecological interpretation of specialization in contemporary butterfly biogeography. Narrow diet breadth can still matter for host dependence and vulnerability, but it does not imply geographically narrow resource opportunity when the focal host is itself widely transported. Conversely, using more host families does not guarantee a larger proportional gain from globalization. The full-panel host-breadth association was close to zero; the strict ±0.10 equivalence test was narrowly inconclusive and the fixed-Brownian tree subset retained substantial uncertainty, so moderate effects cannot be excluded under every model. The key butterfly result is therefore not that diet breadth is irrelevant, but that **taxonomic host breadth and anthropogenic host geography capture different dimensions of specialization**.

The butterfly consequences also depend strongly on which host plants are involved. Hundreds of hosts contributed some added opportunity, but a small upper tier contributed disproportionately: 38 species supplied half of all added credit, and only 25 of 431 genera supplied half after taxonomic aggregation. The leading set includes forage, pasture/turf and crop plants—such as alfalfa, Kentucky bluegrass, Bermuda grass, orchardgrass, maize, oat and rice—alongside widespread ruderal hosts such as *Oxalis corniculata*. Importantly, the pattern was not reducible to crops: after FAO crop hosts were removed, aggregate expansion remained 47.0% (46.0% under the conservative genus-expanded rule), and 37–38 host species still accounted for half of added opportunity. Because WCVP introduced records represent wild non-native occurrences rather than cultivation records, the residual signal is consistent with a broader human-mediated flora that includes naturalized forage, turf, roadside/ruderal and escaped plants. This interpretation is ecologically plausible because disturbance and propagule pressure are major drivers of alien-plant naturalization and roadsides commonly facilitate exotic and weedy plant dispersal (van Kleunen et al. 2018; Lázaro-Lobo & Ervin 2019). We did not classify every non-crop host by land-use association or introduction pathway, so the remaining expansion cannot be partitioned quantitatively into weeds, pasture plants, horticultural escapes or other components. Butterflies with any resolved Poaceae host nevertheless had more absolute added geography than non-Poaceae users (median 69.5 versus 54 WGSRPD3 units), while removing all Poaceae users left the diet-breadth correlation near zero (rho = 0.026). Thus managed grasses and disturbance-associated plants are plausible contributors to the amount of new resource geography without explaining the weak diet-breadth slope itself.

Matched-host and network-prominence analyses provide only a bounded explanatory hypothesis. Apparent host-identity excess depended on how strongly candidate plants were weighted by wider HOSTS prominence, and plant degree itself can reflect ecological commonness, cultivation, study intensity and reverse causation. We therefore use these analyses only to show that redistribution is structured rather than random with respect to host prominence; the main ecological conclusion does not depend on assigning that structure a causal mechanism.

### 4.2 Added resource geography is reflected in contemporary occurrence

A resource-envelope analysis can otherwise remain purely potential. This secondary occurrence comparison provides a bridge to realized biogeography.

More than half of species × region observations lying outside native host-resource envelopes were brought inside the envelope by adding introduced host ranges. Crucially, the result persisted when butterfly species were treated as the replication unit and when the largest-contributing species was removed. The validation therefore cannot be reduced to the fact that contemporary envelopes are larger or to a single high-leverage butterfly. This does not prove that the recorded butterfly used the introduced host at that locality, nor that host introduction caused colonization, but it demonstrates that a native-only view of larval resources can miss contemporary geographic opportunities that coincide with butterfly presence.

That distinction matters for butterfly macroecology. When larval host plants have been redistributed, native-only host maps can create apparent butterfly–host mismatches that are partly artifacts of treating present-day resource opportunity as if plant geography were still native.

### 4.3 Implications for invasion ecology, conservation and global-change biogeography

For butterfly biogeography, the main implication is that global change modifies not only where butterflies occur but also where their larval resources occur. Global plant naturalization has already eroded floristic distinctiveness among regions (Daru et al. 2021), and our results show that this redistribution can substantially enlarge butterfly host-resource geography. Native-only host layers can therefore misrepresent contemporary larval-resource opportunity, especially when a taxonomically narrow-diet butterfly uses a plant that has been widely transported.

This distinction also changes how butterfly range shifts should be interpreted. Expansion can arise through changes on the butterfly side, such as broader host use or reduced specialization, but it can also arise because the geography of an already-known host has changed. The two processes are biologically different: one changes the butterfly's recorded interaction breadth, whereas the other changes the spatial availability of the same interaction. Treating both as a single “generalist advantage” risks missing which side of the butterfly–plant relationship actually changed.

The broader implication follows from this butterfly result. Globalization acts on **interaction geography**, not only on focal-species ranges, and introductions can homogenize ecological interaction networks (Fricke & Svenning 2020). Herbivores, pollinators, frugivores, parasites and other consumers increasingly encounter partners whose ranges have been altered by cultivation, trade and invasion. Global-change biogeography should therefore distinguish the taxonomic breadth of interactions, the changing geography of interaction partners, and the subset of that opportunity that becomes realized distribution.

This framing also complicates a simple positive-versus-negative interpretation of alien plants for native butterflies. Exotic hosts can become usable resources, but their demographic consequences range from apparent resource supplementation to reduced larval performance and ecological traps (Yoon & Read 2016; Braga 2023). Our resource envelopes therefore identify **availability**, not host quality, preference or fitness benefit. The persistence of 46–47% aggregate expansion after crop-host exclusion nevertheless shows that the phenomenon extends beyond cultivated crops and places naturalized forage, turf, ruderal and escaped plants within the same biogeographic problem: human redistribution of plants can propagate through established trophic links even when consumer diet identity is held fixed.

The conservation implication is therefore not that introduced hosts should be retained. Rather, vulnerability assessments based only on consumer range size and taxonomic specialization can miss a second axis: the redistribution history of the species on which the consumer depends. Two equally narrow specialists may face very different contemporary resource geographies if one uses a host spread widely by people and the other depends on a geographically restricted host. Conservation planning can therefore pair specialization metrics with partner geography, while restoration or invasive-plant removal can ask which native consumers currently depend on the focal plant and whether suitable native hosts are available. This is an interaction-aware management problem rather than a simple count of native versus non-native species, consistent with recent calls to incorporate species-interaction networks into global-change management (Abdala-Roberts et al. 2025).

The next step for butterflies is temporal and local. Dated plant introductions and locality-level larval host-use records would allow direct tests of whether redistributed hosts precede butterfly colonization, facilitate persistence or induce host switching. Local performance data would additionally distinguish redistributed hosts that function as demographic resources from those that are neutral or detrimental. The present analysis establishes the large-scale host-resource opportunity on which those causal and management tests can be built.


## 5. Limitations

HOSTS is incomplete and geographically uneven, and the conservative filter cannot make host-interaction sampling globally uniform. Regional stratification reduces concern that the main breadth–expansion result is solely a North American artifact, but tropical strata remain small. Species-level PGLS was possible for only 124/239 butterflies directly represented in the Kawahara et al. (2023) tree. Tree-matched species had greater expansion than unmatched species, although host-family breadth was similar and the breadth–expansion correlation was near zero in both subsets; PGLS is therefore used only as a slope sensitivity, not to estimate panel-wide expansion magnitude.

WCVP introduced status identifies contemporary distribution status rather than the timing or pathway of introduction. We therefore infer changed **resource opportunity**, not a causal historical effect of host introduction on butterfly range expansion. The host-contribution decomposition is also not a plant-trait effect analysis: fractional credit combines a plant's use across focal butterflies with the number of geographic units its introduced distribution adds.

Occurrence validation is presence-side and opportunistic: the 32-species panel was constructed for a stratified climate test, not specifically for validating introduced-host opportunity. A butterfly observation in a region covered by an introduced known host does not prove larval use of that host population, and GBIF non-observation is not confirmed absence. The panel also includes species with documented non-native populations, including *Pieris brassicae* (Phillips et al. 2020); excluding that species did not change the occurrence-overlap conclusion.

WGSRPD3 is coarse and imposes a finite geographic ceiling. Sensitivity analyses indicate that this ceiling does not explain the near-zero specialization gradient, but finer spatial data would improve inference.

The secondary climate test, reported in Supplementary Information, contains only 24 informative species. Its broad bootstrap interval means moderate effects remain plausible despite the unsupported directional prediction.

Finally, the matched-host nulls, occurrence-overlap nulls, equivalence diagnostic, host-contribution concentration analysis, ceiling, phylogenetic and spatial sensitivities are post-hoc analyses and are interpreted accordingly. Other-Lepidoptera consumer count is an imperfect joint proxy for ecological host prominence and HOSTS recording intensity. The fixed HOSTS reconstruction also does not identify whether a consumer record predates plant introduction or occurred within the plant's native range, so a pre-introduction/native-area network degree cannot be reconstructed here. Moreover, the 3 × 3 parameter grid showed that attenuation of the simple host-identity excess depends on the strength of degree weighting. We therefore use these nulls to identify prominence as a confounding axis, not to claim that it fully mediates or quantitatively explains the host-identity pattern.

## 6. Conclusions

Anthropogenic redistribution of host plants has substantially expanded reconstructed butterfly resource geography: more than 86% of resource-eligible species gained opportunity and aggregate species × region coverage increased by 54.9%. Host-family breadth was a poor predictor of proportional gain, although a strict ±0.10 equivalence test was narrowly inconclusive. In contrast, the plant-side contribution was strongly uneven: 38 of 670 host species accounted for half of all added butterfly × region opportunity.

The secondary occurrence analysis shows that the added geography is not merely cartographic potential. Introduced host distributions recovered 57.4% of butterfly species × region observations that lay outside native host-resource envelopes, more than expected under species-level region-matched nulls and after removal of the largest contributor.

Together, these results show that **taxonomic host breadth, host geography and realized butterfly occurrence are distinct dimensions of contemporary butterfly biogeography**. Holding known butterfly–host identities fixed analytically, changing only host geography was sufficient to reveal a 54.9% increase in aggregate resource opportunity. That gain was concentrated among a minority of hosts, poorly predicted by family-level diet breadth and only partly reflected in butterfly occurrence. More generally, global change can reorganize consumer opportunity by moving interaction partners even without invoking a broader consumer niche. For vulnerability under globalization, the relevant question is therefore not only **how many partners a consumer uses**, but **which partners it uses and how human activity has redistributed them**.

## Data and Code Availability

Analysis code and the inputs required to reproduce the reported results and figures are versioned in the study repository. Public source datasets include LepTraits, HOSTS, WCVP, GBIF, CHELSA and WGSRPD. The secondary occurrence validation used 53,434 GBIF occurrence records. An exact-ID archival download requested those same records (https://doi.org/10.15468/dl.pp5nc9); GBIF returned 53,144 records, indicating that 290 records used in the frozen analysis were no longer returned by GBIF at archival download time. Exact source identities, retrieval rules, robustness-analysis specifications and execution records are documented in the supplementary reproducibility materials rather than the main text. A stable public archival snapshot of the analysis package will be deposited before publication.
