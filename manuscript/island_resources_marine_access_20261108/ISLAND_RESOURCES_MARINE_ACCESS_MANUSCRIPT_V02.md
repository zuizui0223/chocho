# Islands as reservoirs of potential butterfly host resources: introduced floras, ports and marine connectivity

**Research Article — integrated scientific draft v0.2, 8 November 2026. Intended venue: Journal of Biogeography.**

**Status:** Draft for coauthor review; not submitted. The independent Global Ecology and Biogeography (GEB) manuscript and all frozen source/model results are unchanged. The term “reservoirs” refers to reconstructed *potential* larval-host resource combinations, not demonstrated larval feeding, butterfly establishment, or population growth.

## Abstract

**Aim.** Plant redistribution can increase the resources potentially available to herbivorous insects even where geographical barriers to insect movement remain. We asked whether introduced host plants disproportionately enlarge butterfly resource opportunities on islands, whether indexed ports are associated with a general introduced flora or a host-specific enrichment, and whether resource similarity and marine connectivity provide complementary information about island butterfly assemblages.

**Location.** Worldwide botanical regions, with a separate analysis of 51 original port-indexed island/landmass groups.

**Taxon.** Butterflies and their larval host plants. The regional opportunity reconstruction fixes 239 butterfly species; the separately sourced port/island assemblage analysis retains its original 225-species candidate panel.

**Methods.** We compared native-only and contemporary larval-host opportunity across insular and mainland World Geographical Scheme for Recording Plant Distributions Level 3 regions using fixed HOSTS relationships and World Checklist of Vascular Plants distributions. We related World Port Index counts to introduced vascular flora and introduced larval-host richness with botanical-indexing controls. For 51 source island groups (1,275 non-independent pairs), we compared host and butterfly record similarities and geographically held-out predictions incorporating effective resistance on an independently reconstructed marine graph. We mapped equal-pair electrical current concentration and tested the source specificity of maritime proxies.

**Results.** Potential butterfly–region resources increased by **118.9% in insular regions** and **40.9% in mainland regions**; the area- and broad-biogeography-matched insular excess in added-resource share was **10.7 percentage points**. Across 98 insular regions with botanical-indexing controls, port counts were associated with total introduced flora (β = **+0.2445**) and introduced larval-host taxa (β = **+0.1639**), but not with a positive residual host enrichment after conditioning on all introduced flora (β = **−0.0415**). Candidate host similarity correlated with later-indexed butterfly similarity among 51 source islands (Spearman ρ = **+0.300**). In exploratory leave-one-island-out prediction, hosts added **1.73%** MSE reduction beyond the marine predictors and marine predictors added **6.49%** beyond hosts. A stricter both-islands-unseen check retained smaller positive overall increments, while latitude and geographically local AIS placebos prevented attributing the signal specifically to vessel movement.

**Main conclusions.** Introduced plants have reshaped the geography of *potential* butterfly resources particularly strongly on islands, and port-rich islands contain broader introduced floras that include more host taxa. Resource identity and modelled marine connectivity each contain some retrospective information on butterfly records, but neither the map nor the predictive contrasts establishes ship-mediated arrival, stepping-stone colonisation, or realized food use.

## Introduction

An island can have a suitable climate and an accessible coastline but lack an acceptable larval host, or it can contain a suitable host without a butterfly ever arriving. For herbivorous insects, resource availability and access therefore represent distinct geographical constraints. Human redistribution of plants changes the first constraint without necessarily changing a butterfly's host preferences or flight abilities; natural seaways and human maritime networks may change the second. Yet many broad-scale distribution comparisons treat geographic isolation and environmental suitability without separately examining the geography of the dependent resource.

Plant introductions are especially consequential for island systems, where native host pools may be small relative to introduced floras. A single introduced host recorded at the botanical-region level can create new *possible* butterfly–region combinations, although this is not equivalent to additional living butterflies. The first question is therefore not whether introductions mechanically increase the number of resource combinations—an expected consequence of the definition—but **how strongly their contribution differs between island and mainland systems after accounting for geographical composition and area**.

Ports provide a potentially informative but challenging indicator. A port-rich island can contain many introduced host species simply because it contains many introduced plant species. The apparent host association then reflects the wider assemblage, not selective transport of butterfly food plants. Indexed modern ports also correlate with commerce, settlements, gardens, land use, and botanical sampling; they are not historical shipping records. A host-specific association must consequently be separated from one arising through the entire introduced flora.

A final, ecologically distinct question concerns whether reconstructed resources are associated with the recorded distribution of butterflies across islands. Two island pairs may have similar larval-host lists but differ in effective separation through ocean waters; conversely, they may be readily connected through the modelled marine domain but differ in the identity of the available hosts. Their contributions can be evaluated as **conditional prediction information** without inferring that a vessel carried a particular plant or that a butterfly used a particular stepping-stone island.

We combine three source-defined analytical grains: botanical regions for resource opportunities, effort-annotated insular regions for ports and introduced floras, and physically indexed island/landmass groups for assemblage and marine-circuit comparisons. Our predictions are that: (i) introduced plants account for a disproportionately large fraction of potential host opportunities on islands; (ii) modern ports covary with the introduced flora as a whole, with no necessary positive host-specific excess; and (iii) host composition and marine resistance carry non-identical information about observed butterfly-community similarity. We further ask whether the maritime component can be distinguished from geography alone, using independently defined controls and historical-network sensitivity checks. These are linked observational questions, not a single identified historical causal chain.

## Materials and methods

### Fixed butterfly resources and region classes

The resource reconstruction reuses pinned larval-host links for **239 butterfly species**, accepted vascular-plant identities and native/introduced statuses from the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP), and Level 3 regions of the World Geographical Scheme for Recording Plant Distributions (WGSRPD). We classified **126 regions as strictly insular**, **237 as mainland** and excluded **six mixed** regions. Botanical regions can contain several physical islands and are never treated as individual marine-current terminals.

For butterfly b and botanical region r, a native-only binary opportunity indicates at least one fixed larval-host plant with native status; a contemporary opportunity indicates at least one recorded accepted host in the contemporary regional flora. An introduced-added opportunity is the latter indicator minus the former. We sum these butterfly × region combinations, rather than species abundances, plant individuals or confirmed egg/larval encounters. The fixed network means the analysis attributes changed opportunity to changed plant geography, not to inferred diet evolution.

We report native and contemporary totals, added/native proportional expansion, and added/contemporary share for insular and mainland classes. To test whether the pattern depends only on represented geography and area, we compare region-level added shares within common WGSRPD Level 1 × log-area quartile strata, weighting each by the smaller insular/mainland stratum size. We retain the archived 4,999 within-stratum label permutations as **descriptive**, not geographically exchangeable causal randomisation.

### Limited independent butterfly-index overlap with added resource cells

The original Global Ecology and Biogeography supplementary validation source provides an independently archived GBIF butterfly occurrence download (DOI **10.15468/dl.pp5nc9**). Within a fixed **32-species validation panel**, **31 species** had source records. Those species contributed **920** reconstructed introduced-added butterfly × insular botanical-region opportunities. We counted the number of those candidate cells with at least one butterfly index record in this **limited archival sample**. This operation measures *recorded overlap*, not verified larval feeding, breeding, colonisation or the fraction of all new opportunities that are truly occupied. Missing GBIF records do not represent true absences; the downloaded archive differs from the earlier API count by 290 records.

### Ports and the full introduced flora

Contemporary World Port Index (WPI) counts were overlaid onto WGSRPD Level 3 botanical regions. Of **123 resource-active insular regions**, 91 had at least one of the **982 indexed ports**, while 32 had no indexed WPI port. The latter status does not mean that those islands lacked boats or historical trade. Port exposure is log₂(1 + indexed ports).

We enumerated all accepted introduced WCVP vascular plant taxa and the **distinct introduced plant taxa** participating in the fixed 239-butterfly HOSTS network. This distinction matters: one plant can support multiple butterfly–region opportunity cells but counts only once as a distinct host plant. GBIF all-plant indexing provided an observational-effort proxy. The port–flora models use the **98 insular regions with valid effort data**, adjusting for area, native flora/host baseline, WGSRPD Level 1 and indexing effort. The host-specific model additionally conditions on log(1 + entire introduced flora richness). It is a conditional association, not a mediation design: host plants are a subset of the wider flora, and the flora itself may be related to port-rich landscapes.

### Original island/landmass assemblage network

The separate fixed port-network source contains **51 island or landmass groups**, **194 originally indexed WPI ports**, and **1,275 unordered inter-island pairs**. It preserves its original **225-butterfly candidate panel**; those taxa and units are not silently substituted with the 239-butterfly WGSRPD reconstruction. Candidate introduced-host inventories aggregate plants in the original port queries, using observations pooled over **2001–2024**. Earlier butterfly Jaccard similarity uses **2001–2010** records, while later similarity refers to butterflies first indexed **2011–2022**.

We compare port-host Jaccard composition with earlier and later butterfly Jaccard composition using Spearman correlation and a descriptive partial-rank association controlling geographical distance and earlier butterfly similarity. Pair endpoints repeat: 1,275 pairs do not represent 1,275 independent biological observations. A first GBIF index is not a verified immigration or reproduction date, and the pooled host inventory can contain records later than the butterfly response.

### Ocean conductance, distributed ports and current concentration

The marine network was constructed from GSHHG 2.3.7 coastline geometry, 0.0625° water subcells and 0.5° conductivity blocks. It preserves disconnected water patches within the same block instead of bridging land. The source-defined graph contains **189,550 marine nodes** and **370,996 undirected edges**. The original 194 biological ports remain in the island data; **182 ports** pass a fixed marine-access proximity filter and serve as distributed electrical terminals. All 51 island groups remain represented. Qualification does not prove that a harbour is accessible through a navigable channel at this spatial resolution.

We evaluate three conductance fields on the *same marine topology*: uniform ocean conductance, World Bank/IMF commercial AIS vessel-position density (2015–February 2021), and mean magnitude of NOAA reanalysis 10-m wind vectors over the corresponding source window. These are conductance proxies, not calibrated probabilities of travel or ship-carried propagule flux. Wind speed is scalar and undirected, not directional dispersal. Each original island group contributes one distributed unit of source current across its eligible ports; ports on the same island are not connected by an additional land-transfer or short-circuit edge. For two island source distributions, marine effective resistance is the quadratic energy of their current-injection difference under the graph Laplacian pseudoinverse.

All **1,275 pairwise resistances** were computed with a SciPy sparse solver and checked on selected whole-network cases against actual Julia Circuitscape 6.1.0; the cross-software validation does **not** imply that Julia independently recomputed the whole pair matrix. To visualise *where current concentrates in this defined electrical domain*, we calculate absolute edge current averaged over all 1,275 equally weighted island-pair injections using 50 independent reference-island potential solves. Half of each edge's absolute flow is allocated to each incident water node and aggregated for plotting on the original 0.5° geographic map (Fig. 4A). This is a **geographic circuit diagnostic**, not a measurement of biological traffic through islands.

### Conditional prediction and robustness hierarchy

In the source-frozen exploratory leave-one-island-out (LOIO) comparison, each of the 51 folds trains on the 1,225 other-island pairs and predicts the 50 pairs incident on the excluded island. The geographical reference model includes log distance and its square, original port-count terms, a uniform-ocean circuit, and—for later butterfly records—earlier butterfly similarity. Candidate-host Jaccard adds a resource-composition predictor; native-source AIS and wind resistances add marine predictors; a combined model includes both. Fits use training-only standardisation, ordinary least squares and bounded Jaccard predictions. Performance is summarised as the percentage reduction of held-out mean squared error (MSE), calculated separately for each stated reference model. Counts of islands with improved prediction are descriptive, not independent replicates or confidence intervals.

A subsequent stricter check holds **both** endpoints of every original island pair out from fitting, using 15 longitude-block-pair folds. It also includes pre-2011 botanical-recording proxy counts, original candidate-host marginal richness and other documented geography. Its +/− percentages are **not on the same baseline** as the original LOIO contrasts and cannot be subtracted to infer effect changes. Both sets of model contrasts are exploratory and use the same underlying 51 islands; the stricter comparison is a new split, not new field validation.

To delimit attribution of the marine predictors, we report pre-specified latitude/hemisphere controls in the follow-up models, 199 latitude-band AIS graph-node placebos, and 199 geographically local (at most 750 km) graph-node reassignments. The latter retain 15 immovable island labels, so even their failure to discriminate AIS is not a formally exchangeable transport null. A separate historical sensitivity uses 2006–2010 UNCTAD liner-network connectedness *index* on 17 original island groups with sufficiently complete old port series, evaluating all 136 pairs with both endpoints absent from training. The historical index is not a direct voyage or cargo manifest.

### Scope and reportable inference

Effects of whole-flora transport, port geography, candidate larval resources and modelled marine access are reported at their respective grains rather than pooled in one multilevel causal model. Colonial routes, plant annual-herb trait filtering, undocumented direct voyages, and the chronology of any particular host introduction remain supplementary boundary analyses. Wastewater and nitrogen source attribution are outside this paper and belong to the separate `gesui` research track. All empirical statistics below are observational, with no inferred causal path from ports through plants to butterflies.

## Results

### Disproportionate potential resource expansion on islands

Insular botanical regions contained **4,829 native-only** and **10,570 contemporary** butterfly × region opportunities, an increase of **5,741 (+118.9%)**. The mainland counts were **21,215 native-only** and **29,901 contemporary**, an increase of **8,686 (+40.9%)**. The proportion of contemporary candidate opportunities attributable to introduced-added geography was **54.3%** on islands compared with **29.0%** on mainland regions. Within shared Level 1 × area strata, the area- and region-matched insular contrast was **+0.107** in added share, or **10.7 percentage points**, with a descriptive within-stratum permutation value of 0.0002. The corresponding broader Level 1 weighting alone yielded +0.144. Ten insular botanical regions had contemporary opportunity but zero native-only opportunity in this **fixed butterfly panel**; this is not a declaration that those physical islands contain no other natural larval resources.

### Only a small fraction of sampled added opportunities overlap indexed butterflies

In the independent validation archive, butterflies were indexed in **13 of 920** candidate introduced-added island opportunity cells (**1.413%**) among the **31** species with available GBIF records. This is a descriptive *sampling/record-overlap* proportion in a selected taxonomic panel, **not** a realised-resource or colonisation rate for the 239-species regional analysis. The 907 remaining cells are not demonstrated to be unoccupied; their butterfly observations may simply be missing from the indexed subset.

### Ports coincide with entire introduced floras, not an additional positive host-specific excess

In the **98 effort-annotated insular regions**, log₂(1 + indexed WPI ports) was positively associated with introduced WCVP flora richness (**β = +0.2445**, descriptive permutation p = **0.0012**) and with the number of distinct introduced host plants (**β = +0.1639**, p = **0.011**) under their respective native-baseline, geographic, area and GBIF-effort controls. The host model additionally conditioning on the entire introduced flora did **not** show a positive residual port–host association (**β = −0.0415**, p = **0.093**). This supports the narrower interpretation that host taxa accompany broader introduced-flora accumulation at port-rich places; it does not prove the plants travelled on ships, or that the reduced conditional coefficient is a mediated causal effect.

### Host assemblages and butterfly records have corresponding island geography

Among the original **51 landmass groups / 1,275 dependent pairs**, similarity of candidate port-associated hosts was positively correlated with similarity of later-indexed butterflies (Spearman **ρ = +0.2998**). The descriptive partial-rank correlation controlling distance and earlier-butterfly similarity was **+0.2154**. Host correspondence with **earlier** butterflies was stronger (**ρ = +0.4178**), arguing against treating this cross-sectional relationship as distinctive proof of new arrivals. No evidence here establishes actual host use or a temporal sequence of plant introduction followed by butterfly establishment.

### Resource composition and modelled marine resistance add different prediction information

Under the original LOIO specification, the joint host-plus-AIS/wind model had MSE **0.00589635** for later butterfly Jaccard similarity. Adding hosts to the marine model reduced MSE from 0.00600023 to 0.00589635 (**+1.7312% improvement; 31/51 held islands improved**). Adding AIS and wind marine terms to the host model reduced MSE from 0.00630545 to 0.00589635 (**+6.4880%; 39/51 improved**). These contrasts use the same final joint model and do not represent independent replications; neither percentage is a causal fraction attributable to one source.

In the stricter double-endpoint-unseen split, the overall MSE improvements were **+0.8808% for host composition beyond marine terms** and **+4.4744% for AIS/wind marine terms beyond hosts and controls** across all original pairs. However, the host increment between longitude groups was only **+0.1417% MSE**, with a negative between-group MAE increment (**−0.3255%**). The earlier 2001–2010 butterfly comparison was adverse for the marine term under these controls. The geography-specific sensitivity limits any statement that both resources and marine covariates generalise with the same strength to every island system.

### The mapped current field identifies marine corridors, not relay islands

The geometry-only uniform-ocean current map (Fig. 4A) displays current concentrations along modelled over-water corridors and near concentrated source/sink terminals. It is calculated on the full **189,550-node / 370,996-edge** marine network, equally averaging all **1,275** island-pair electric flows; the computation reproduces three archived pair energies to numerical tolerance. By design, the graph has **no transfer operator through land or across ports within an island**, so a bright coastline or nearby ocean cell cannot identify a stopover or stepping-stone island. The map is evidence for spatial structure in the *defined electrical geometry*, not for actual dispersal paths or biological hubs.

### AIS and historical network information do not identify ship-specific effects

After the four latitude/hemisphere controls in the strict double-unseen setup, the addition of AIS alone retained a **+1.0020%** MSE improvement for later butterfly records, but the wind-only term was adverse (**−4.6618%**); adding AIS and wind jointly was also adverse (**−4.0918%**). Thus the earlier unconditional joint marine gain does not survive that latitude specification as a universal combined source effect.

An AIS graph-label placebo constrained only by latitude was less predictive than the true AIS assignment for the all-pair AIS-only contrast in most of 199 surrogates (**5/199** at least as good). **But**, with geographical swaps restricted to 750 km, **100/199** surrogates equalled or exceeded the real AIS improvement (+1.0020%, versus a local-placebo median +1.0600%). This contrast demonstrates sensitivity to the spatial null; neither null is a calibrated randomised trial of voyages.

A historically earlier port-network index also failed to provide stable full-sample discrimination: the 2006–2010 liner connectedness index worsened 2011–2022 later-butterfly prediction by **−2.9233% MSE** across **17** qualifying island groups and **136** pairs. A predeclared 16-island sensitivity omitting one composite group yielded **+8.1461%**, but post-result diagnostics showed that improvement required excluding the group from **training as well as testing**. This sensitivity is not evidence that historical ships brought plants or butterflies. The supported conclusion is limited to modest, model-dependent maritime predictor information without identification of its ship-traffic-specific component.

## Discussion

### Resource opportunity and accessibility are separable layers of island biogeography

The strongest result is the substantial, area-and-biogeography-adjusted excess contribution of introduced plants to insular *potential* butterfly larval resources. It measures a real difference in the reconstructed resource landscape, conditional on the fixed host network, not a measured rise in butterfly numbers. Human-associated redistribution can change the environment encountered by an arriving herbivore; it does not itself demonstrate that an herbivore arrived. This distinction offers an ecological interpretation of otherwise easily overstated resource-map gains.

The positive host-community correspondence and conditional marine-model prediction both matter because they concern separate informational axes. The resource layer is assembled from plant identities; the access layer is assembled from coast geometry and modern environmental/traffic proxies. Their joint predictive utility for retrospective butterfly records does not imply they are two proven independent forces. Both may retain unmeasured biogeography, climate, observer effort and regional history. The stricter geographic split confirms that the sizes and signs of increments depend on the test geography.

### Ports index broad assembly rather than preferential butterfly food transport

Port-associated flora richness is consistent with broad plant redistribution including candidate larval hosts, not positive host-specific enrichment above the whole flora. This is an informative distinction for conservation and biosecurity: the entire pool of introduced plants matters for identifying *potential* consumer resources, not just those labelled as known butterfly hosts. But the study does not assign which introductions passed a port, nor does it justify treating a modern WPI count as a shipment rate. Botanical indexing, floristic richness, urbanisation, horticulture and islands' historical economies may still explain part of the observed associations.

### Physical bottlenecks are not demonstrated ecological stepping stones

The visual prominence of maritime currents does not establish that butterflies or host propagules moved along them. The current map's equal-pair forcing intentionally places sources and sinks at the 51 terminal groups; bright coastal patches partly reflect this demand geometry. More importantly, electrical current cannot pass through an island as a biological relay in the defined graph. A true relay test would need transport through (and possible persistence on) a stopover island, a source-resolved chronological sequence, and a movement model allowing directed transfer and source competence. We therefore use the term **modelled over-water corridor**, never **verified island hub**.

The AIS results sharpen rather than erase this distinction. Modern vessel-density resistance has small positive additional predictive information in some geographically held-out models, but local placebos and the independent earlier port index do not isolate actual shipping activity from the geography that also determines ports. Wind speed is undirected and not a measure of butterfly flight altitude or windborne trajectories. Consequently we do not interpret the opposing earlier/later target signs as verified changes in an arrival mechanism.

### Potential resources are not automatically realised

The study cannot identify the proportion of the reconstructed new opportunities that yielded persistent butterfly populations. A source-locked GBIF validation subset does nevertheless demonstrate a strong difference between potential plant-defined combinations and observed butterfly indexing: **13/920 added island resource cells (1.4%)** had an overlapping record in the **31-species sampled archive**. This supports a *recorded-overlap* description, not the assertion that 98.6% of resources were biologically unused. Presence of one accepted plant somewhere in a botanical region does not establish accessible host quality, sufficiently long host phenology, oviposition, larval survival or co-occurrence with an adult butterfly. Likewise, a first GBIF occurrence reflects indexing and survey effort as well as biology. Claims about “unused resources” are therefore qualitative potential-versus-observation contrasts, not population-level estimates of unoccupied ecological capacity or a quantified colonisation bottleneck.

### Boundaries, relation to the GEB paper and next tests

The shared **239-butterfly** network and global reconstruction are also used in the related GEB resource-distribution study. The worldwide **+54.9%** resource expansion is shared background, **not** a second independent discovery. This article's distinctive results are (a) an island–mainland resource contrast with geographic/area controls; (b) the distinction between entire introduced floras and distinct host taxa at ports; and (c) island-pair host/marine conditional predictions with independent source validation and explicitly adverse transport-specific sensitivity findings. Editors and coauthors should review conceptual overlap before submission.

A decisive biological test would recover date-validated island-specific host lists and documented plant arrival history *before* a matched butterfly colonisation or larval-breeding series, along with observed directed shipping links and independent ecological covariates. No such global panel is reconstructed here. Colonisation, phenology, dispersal pathway, colonial-era plant carriage and first-record dates remain outside what the current evidence can identify.

## Conclusions

Introduced flora creates disproportionately large *potential* larval-host resource gains in insular botanical regions, and port-rich regions accumulate entire introduced floras that include butterfly hosts without an additional positive host-specific port association. Candidate-host geography and marine effective resistance offer complementary but geographically variable information about the similarities of recorded island butterfly assemblages. Their combination supports a **two-layer descriptive biogeography of resources and access**, not a demonstrated ship-mediated stepping-stone colonisation process.
