# Candidate ecological reframe: plant globalization rewires butterfly resource space

Status: exploratory candidate framing. Do not merge into the submission manuscript until the post-hoc inference boundary is accepted.

## Ecological question

Does anthropogenic redistribution of larval host plants merely enlarge individual butterfly resource envelopes, or does it reorganize the ecological structure of resource space by:

1. homogenizing the butterfly-resource assemblages available among regions,
2. increasing interspecific overlap in shared larval resources, and
3. concentrating new opportunity on a small, non-redundant set of redistributed host plants?

This reframes the study from a metric question about specialist-generalist labels to a community-ecological question about how plant globalization changes resource landscapes and potential interaction exposure among consumers.

## Main results already supported

### 1. Regional resource assemblages converge

Across 355 WGSRPD3 regions with native butterfly resource opportunity, mean pairwise Jaccard similarity increased from 0.277 under native host ranges to 0.462 under contemporary host ranges: a 66.8% increase.

A fixed-margin swap null preserves both:
- each butterfly's number of added regions, and
- each region's number of added butterfly opportunities.

The observed contemporary mean Jaccard (0.4621) exceeded the null median (0.4535; 95% interval 0.4533–0.4536; p = 0.002).

Thus homogenization is not only a mathematical consequence of adding the same amount of resource opportunity.

Sensitivity:
- exact FAO crop-host exclusion: +58.2% regional similarity,
- conservative crop-genus exclusion: +58.0%,
- exclusion of all Poaceae hosts: +66.5%.

### 2. Butterfly resource niches overlap more strongly

Across all 28,441 butterfly pairs, mean overlap in resource geography increased from 0.222 to 0.328 (+47.4%). This exceeded a fixed-margin null (null median 0.3215; p = 0.002).

Exact shared-host geography also expanded strongly. Counting each butterfly-pair × region once when the pair shares at least one exact larval host present in that region:

- native shared-resource exposure: 62,473 pair × region units,
- contemporary: 141,885,
- novel exposure created by introduced host ranges: 79,412,
- increase relative to native: +127%.

Of the novel shared-resource pair × region units, 41,543 (52.3%) connect butterflies from different families.

Interpretation: plant redistribution creates new opportunities for potential resource co-use across substantial phylogenetic/taxonomic distances. This is interaction exposure, not evidence of realized competition.

### 3. Newly created resource space is more crowded

At native butterfly × region resource opportunities, a focal butterfly shared at least one exact host with a mean of 4.71 other focal butterflies (median 3).

At introduced-added opportunities, the corresponding mean was 5.71 (median 4).

The zero-overlap fraction fell from 18.3% in native resource opportunities to 9.9% in introduced-added opportunities.

Prediction: if resource overlap translates into competition, apparent competition, or other density-dependent interaction effects, the newly created opportunity should be less demographically equivalent to an isolated resource expansion than envelope area alone implies.

### 4. Expansion is simultaneously concentrated and fragile

Of 14,553 added butterfly × region opportunities, 8,574 (58.9%) are supported by only one contributing introduced host in that region.

Targeted removal of introduced-range contributions, while retaining all native host ranges and any alternative known introduced hosts, gives strongly non-random losses:

| 1 | 446 | 3.1% | 14 | 0 | 1 |

For every targeted scenario above, 499 random host-removal draws gave Monte Carlo p = 0.002 for losses at least as large as observed.

The first ranked host, *Medicago sativa*, uniquely supports 446 added butterfly × region units and contributes to 17 focal butterfly species across four butterfly families.

### 5. A small upper tier is not the whole homogenization mechanism

The 38 host species accounting for half of fractional added opportunity raise regional mean Jaccard from 0.277 to 0.406 when considered alone.

The remaining long tail raises it to 0.404 when considered alone.

Thus:
- magnitude of added opportunity is strongly concentrated,
- but homogenization is distributed across a broader host network.

This argues against a simple 'few super-hosts cause everything' explanation.

## Biological interpretation

The candidate ecological mechanism is:

plant globalization
→ repeated appearance of shared larval hosts across regions
→ convergence of regional butterfly resource assemblages
→ expansion of shared resource space among butterfly species
→ increased potential for coexistence, competition, apparent competition, or other indirect interactions
→ a contemporary resource network that is geographically broader but also more homogenized and dependent on non-random host nodes.

The data directly establish the first three arrows as reconstructed resource opportunity. They do not establish realized competition, abundance effects, or fitness consequences.

## Conservation use

The management result is not 'retain invasive plants'.

A defensible implication is network-aware removal/restoration screening:

1. identify introduced hosts whose redistributed ranges uniquely support many butterfly × region opportunities,
2. identify butterflies whose added opportunity has low host redundancy,
3. check local realized host use and demographic quality before removal,
4. where removal is necessary, restore or provide suitable native host resources before or alongside removal,
5. prioritize locality-level validation because WGSRPD3 is too coarse for site-specific prescriptions.

This converts the analysis into a testable conservation workflow rather than a positive valuation of alien plants.

## Next empirical predictions

P1. Contemporary butterfly occurrence in introduced-resource regions should be higher where introduced host support is redundant across multiple known hosts, after controlling geographic sampling.

P2. If interspecific competition matters, realization of new resource opportunity should decline with the number of co-occurring focal butterflies sharing the same hosts, conditional on resource supply.

P3. If shared natural enemies generate apparent competition, highly homogenized host-resource regions should show stronger negative associations among host-sharing butterflies than equally resource-rich but compositionally distinct regions.

P4. Local invasive-host removal should have larger short-term effects on butterflies whose regional opportunity is single-host supported; replacement with suitable native hosts should buffer those effects.

P5. The strongest biogeographic responses should occur where host redistribution creates new resource space without proportionally increasing competitor exposure; this defines a testable facilitation-versus-crowding trade-off.

## Claim boundaries

- Resource overlap is not realized competition.
- GBIF occurrence is presence-side and cannot establish absence or abundance.
- Host quality and larval performance are not measured.
- Intraspecific competition cannot be estimated without abundance/density.
- Removal simulations are coarse global counterfactuals, not site-level management prescriptions.
- All homogenization and removal analyses are post-hoc and must be labelled accordingly.
