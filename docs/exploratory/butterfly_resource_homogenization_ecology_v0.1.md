# Butterfly resource homogenization: ecological extension v0.1

**Status:** post-hoc exploratory extension; not yet part of the submission manuscript.

## Ecological question

The primary manuscript asks how anthropogenic redistribution of known larval hosts changes butterfly resource geography. A stronger ecological extension asks whether plant globalization does more than enlarge individual resource envelopes:

> **Does redistribution of shared host plants homogenize the geographic resource niches of butterflies and spatially multiply opportunities for interspecific resource overlap?**

This is not guaranteed by the definition of resource expansion. Introduced hosts could add different butterflies to different regions and preserve or increase regional differentiation, or the same redistributed hosts could repeatedly add overlapping sets of consumers and homogenize resource opportunity.

## Resource-assemblage homogenization

Using the same 239 butterflies, fixed HOSTS interaction identities, fixed WCVP distributions and the same 14,553 introduced-added butterfly × WGSRPD3 units as the manuscript:

- mean pairwise Jaccard similarity among 355 native-resource regional butterfly assemblages: **0.2770**;
- contemporary-resource similarity: **0.4621**;
- absolute change: **+0.1851**;
- relative change: **+66.8%**.

A fixed-margin swap null preserves both (i) each butterfly's number of added regions and (ii) each region's number of added butterfly resource opportunities, while preventing placement into native-resource cells. Its median contemporary similarity was **0.4535** (95% null interval 0.4533–0.4536). The observed value was higher (**p = 0.002; 499 swaps**).

Thus most homogenization reflects the large and geographically concentrated amount of resource redistribution itself, but the actual placement of butterfly resource opportunities produces a small additional excess beyond those margins.

## Shared hosts span butterfly lineages

The 38 host species accounting for half of all added fractional resource credit are not restricted to single butterfly clades:

- **26/38** contribute added opportunity to butterflies in at least two families;
- **8/38** contribute to at least three families;
- **2/38** contribute to at least four families;
- **71.5%** of the top-38 fractional credit comes from hosts serving butterflies in at least two families.

Examples include *Medicago sativa* (17 focal butterflies across four families) and *Robinia pseudoacacia* (7 butterflies across four families).

This supports a cross-lineage resource-homogenization interpretation rather than a pattern confined to one butterfly radiation.

## Spatial multiplication of potential resource overlap

Keeping butterfly–host identities fixed, host redistribution changed the geography of exact shared-host opportunity:

- native butterfly-pair × region shared-resource units: **62,473**;
- contemporary units: **141,885**;
- increase: **79,412 (+127.1%)**;
- **920/986** host-sharing butterfly pairs gained at least one novel shared-resource region;
- novel shared-resource units within butterfly families: **37,869**;
- novel shared-resource units between butterfly families: **41,543**.

No new butterfly–host link is required for this effect. Human redistribution spatially replicates existing trophic links and therefore increases where consumers with shared resources could encounter one another.

This metric is **potential resource-sharing exposure**, not realized competition.

## Are newly opened resource regions competitively empty?

No, at least within the focal 239-butterfly network.

For each butterfly × region resource opportunity, the number of other focal butterflies sharing at least one exact host in that region was:

| Resource geography | Mean | Median | Q25–Q75 | No focal shared-host consumer |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Native | 4.71 | 3 | 1–7 | 18.3% |
| Introduced-added | 5.71 | 4 | 2–8 | 9.9% |

Thus introduced-added resource space is, on average, **more shared**, not more competitor-free, than native resource space. This predicts greater opportunity for co-occurrence and biotic interaction, but not necessarily stronger exploitative competition.

Regional-scale butterfly work has found host sharing to be associated more strongly with resource-niche filtering and co-occurrence than with competitive exclusion. The present result therefore motivates a test of whether plant globalization increases butterfly co-occurrence by spatially replicating shared resource filters.

## Counterfactual host-removal simulations

We removed only the **introduced-range contribution** of hosts, retaining their native ranges and all other known hosts. Hosts were ranked by their unit-preserving fractional contribution to the 14,553 added butterfly × region units.

| Removed highest-contribution hosts | Added units lost | Fraction lost | Butterflies affected | ≥50% of added opportunity lost | All added opportunity lost |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 446 | 3.1% | 14 | 3 | 0 |
| 5 | 1,816 | 12.5% | 40 | 17 | 6 |
| 10 | 2,893 | 19.9% | 61 | 24 | 7 |
| 20 | 4,199 | 28.9% | 93 | 36 | 9 |
| 38 | 5,812 | 39.9% | 123 | 56 | 16 |
| 50 | 6,615 | 45.5% | 133 | 66 | 20 |
| 100 | 8,781 | 60.3% | 164 | 101 | 33 |

For comparison, removing 38 randomly chosen contributing hosts had a median loss of only **432** added units in 499 randomizations. This comparison is a scenario/robustness diagnostic, not an independent inferential test, because the focal hosts were ranked using the same contribution data.

## Existing anthropogenic resource dependence

Across the 239 species:

- 142 have at least 25% of contemporary resource geography contributed by introduced host ranges;
- **56** have at least 50%;
- 8 have at least 75%.

Among 97 one-family butterflies, **25** have at least half of their contemporary resource geography contributed by introduced host ranges.

These are potential resource-geography fractions, not demographic dependence.

## Conservation use

The analysis can support a **screening** framework for invasive-plant management:

1. identify a candidate plant for removal;
2. identify butterflies for which its introduced range creates unique or weakly redundant resource opportunity;
3. flag regions where removal could create a host-resource gap;
4. verify local larval use, host quality, abundance and butterfly demography;
5. where appropriate, restore suitable native known hosts before or during removal.

This is not an argument to retain invasive plants. At WGSRPD3 resolution the results cannot justify site-level management decisions.

## Testable ecological predictions

1. **Resource homogenization prediction:** regions receiving similar sets of redistributed hosts should converge in the set of butterflies for which larval resources are available.
2. **Co-occurrence prediction:** host-sharing butterfly pairs should gain co-occurrence disproportionately in regions where their shared hosts have been introduced.
3. **Interaction-exposure prediction:** newly host-enabled regions should expose butterflies to more shared-host consumers (and potentially shared enemies) than native-resource regions.
4. **Management prediction:** removing high-leverage introduced hosts without restoring alternative native hosts should produce larger resource gaps than removing low-leverage introduced hosts.
5. **Dependency prediction:** butterflies with high introduced-share resource geography should show greater sensitivity to future changes in management of anthropogenic host populations.

## Boundaries

- No abundance data are available, so **intraspecific competition cannot be estimated**.
- Shared-host geography is not evidence of realized interspecific competition.
- Host presence is not host abundance or host quality.
- Resource opportunity is not population growth or fitness.
- The homogenization and removal analyses are post-hoc.
- Local conservation decisions require finer-resolution validation.
