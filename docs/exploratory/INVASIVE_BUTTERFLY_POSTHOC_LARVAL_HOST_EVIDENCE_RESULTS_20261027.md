## 1. Evidence is more complete, not a different biological sample

The original *first literature pass* covered 13 previously selected butterfly×invasion-region events in eight species (only five belong to the original global 239 species). It found **3 events** with original event-site larval/egg host reports: **Chilades pandava/Guam**, **Thymelicus lineola/Canada**, **Papilio xuthus/Hawaiian Islands**. That original first-pass result and 15 study records remain unchanged.

Subsequent original/source search uncovered **two more site-resolved source reports**:
1. ***Pieris brassicae* × Chile:** An INIA biological-control historical monograph says newly recorded invaders in Viña del Mar–Concón in **1971** were feeding as **wild larvae on introduced *Tropaeolum majus*** (Tropaeolaceae, native Peru; Kew documents introduced **Chile Central**). Around one year later the species expanded into cabbage/cauliflower agricultural systems at Quillota. This is a **retrospective account**; an independently accessioned 1971 larval specimen, field experiment, or plant individual is not available. [Historical Chile agricultural source](https://books.instituto-idema.org/sites/default/files/Control%20Biologico%20de%20Plagas%20en%20Chile.pdf); [Kew species accepted distribution](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:310974-2).
2. ***Pieris rapae* × New Zealand:** Cameron & Walker (2002) sampled naturally occurring butterfly larvae **on cabbage (Pukekohe in 1993–1994)** and broccoli (Pukekohe in 1995–1996) fields, and monitored/reared larvae to examine parasitoid mortality. This is a direct **field crop/larva** record. Cabbage and broccoli are cultivars of *Brassica oleracea*, which Kew lists as introduced to New Zealand North, but original field paper uses common crop names rather than an independently identified plant voucher. It **does not** establish completion of the whole butterfly life cycle by every field-collected larva. [Original Environmental Entomology study](https://academic.oup.com/ee/article/31/2/367/347896); [Kew *B. oleracea*](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:279435-1).

Thus **5/13 original recipient events have site-resolved field stage + host evidence**, spanning five distinct butterfly species, but **only three** of these five are among the original 239 macro study butterflies. An additional two countries have published host-immature observations **without an independently verified event-specific observation locality in the available study extract**:

3. ***Papilio demoleus* × Puerto Rico:** Segarra-Carmona et al. (2010), *Journal of Agriculture of the University of Puerto Rico*, DOI [10.46429/jaupr.v94i1-2.2592](https://doi.org/10.46429/jaupr.v94i1-2.2592), described multiple larval stages of the new pest. Fernández & Minno (2015) specifically attribute **several hundred eggs and larvae collected from *Citrus × aurantiifolia*** in Puerto Rico to that original study. Kew accepts this hybrid (a cultigen of E Himalayan origin) and documents it as introduced Puerto Rico. But exact field collection site and year of those individuals were not confirmed in the indexed excerpt. [2015 host-site account](https://www.troplep.org/TLR-25-1-Hernandez.pdf); [Kew *Citrus × aurantiifolia*](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:59599-2). This is **country-level field evidence only** until original methods locality is independently recovered.
4. ***Papilio demoleus* × Dominican Republic:** Wehling, Núñez & Glassberg, [*American Butterflies* lime swallowtails natural history](https://legacysite.naba.org/pubs/ab142/ab142lime_swallowtail_in_the_new_world.pdf), describes direct author observation of caterpillar foodplant ***Triphasia trifolia*** in the Dominican Republic, but the indexed account does not give independently extracted specimen site/year. Kew documents the plant as native Bangladesh to Papuasia and **introduced Dominican Republic**. [Kew *T. trifolia*](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:258565-2). Not evidence that the 2004 Dominican first-captured adults were on that plant.

The resulting coverage is **5/13 site-resolved**, **7/13 at least recipient-country resolved**, and **6/13 without a source-qualified immature×host field record in this curated set**. These numbers are *literature screening completion counts* in a purposively selected cohort, **NOT a prevalence estimate, not 7 established host switches, and not evidence of biological non-use in the remaining 6**. The original second-pass analysis never substitutes unavailable original individual observation dates with publication years.

## 2. New multi-consumer perspective: an invasive and native butterfly share an introduced plant

Olivares & Hormazábal (1998), [*Revista Chilena de Entomología* 25:5–9](https://www.insectachile.cl/rchen/pdfs/1998v25/Olivares_Hormazabal_1998.pdf) studied indigenous Chilean pierid ***Tatochila autodice blanchardi*** and described its larvae co-occurring on ***Tropaeolum majus*** with larvae of invasive ***Pieris brassicae***. Their original sampled native larvae are documented from **January 1993 Concepción**, though the exact calendar date/site of each shared plant observation cannot be assigned from the account. Kew documents the plant as native to Peru and introduced **Chile Central**, but the botanical source unit of the co-occurrence site cannot be confirmed from the study text; its precise WCVP-local status remains **unverified** rather than presumed introduced.

This identifies an ecologically more interesting possibility than simply moving an invader's host portfolio: **a newly arrived consumer enters an existing local consumer–plant resource link involving an introduced plant**. The plant is alien to Chile, the native consumer is local, and the introduced butterfly also feeds there. The original paper discusses asymmetry in oviposition and parasitoid exposure, but did **not** experimentally establish competition, suppression or displacement. Therefore this is **documented host sharing; competition is a separate falsifiable hypothesis**, not a positive finding.

## 3. Same plant may be utilized inside butterfly-native geography: the chronology trap

Negi & Joshi (2018), [*Journal of Threatened Taxa* 10(6):11815–11817](https://www.threatenedtaxa.org/index.php/JoTT/article/view/3900), reported ***Pieris brassicae nepalensis*** larval stages on ornamental ***Tropaeolum majus*** in **Uttarakhand, India**, where that butterfly lineage is part of the native regional fauna. Kew identifies *T. majus* as a Peru-origin cultigen and introduced in **some** Asian WCVP regions, but does not establish the specific original Uttarakhand site as a separate WCVP introduced distribution record; treat local WCVP status as **unverified**, while non-native origin and ornamental cultivation remain plausible. Hence an insect native to a location can use an **introduced plant**, and the same plant may be used by a butterfly after invading another region.

But that native-range observation was **2018**, long *after* Chile's invasion in 1971. It cannot prove that the same pair was used in the source population *before* Chile's colonization. Distinguish host documentation chronology from the actual evolutionary origin of its ability.

## 4. How the source evidence corrects stronger earlier claims

- The first HOSTS database had 156 WCVP plant-introduced-area documented butterfly–accepted plant pairs, only 11 also documented in plant-native areas, less than source-exchangeability expectations. WCVP-unlisted generic documentation showed the same deficit. Missing native records are **not** evolved host adoption.
- The independent field literature now documents real receiving-area feeding across **two different biological routes**: *Chilades pandava* feeding on an indigenous Guam cycad, and immigrant/introduced butterflies (*Thymelicus lineola*, *Pieris brassicae*, *Pieris rapae*, *Papilio xuthus*, *Papilio demoleus*) using transferred cultivated or introduced plants. However *Papilio xuthus* source Citrus is genus-only, and *P. demoleus* cases only country-localized in this addendum.
- The historical Chile sequence suggests distinct spatial/temporal **resource portfolios**, while the native Chilean *Tatochila* shared-host observation suggests an under-tested **indirect interaction network**. Neither has yet been quantified as abundance growth, direct competitive effect or realized climatic niche change.
- The new WCVP source-metadata test in the same PR also found original HOSTS `Lab Rearing=Y` in only **1/179** source-introduced-host-region triples, versus 8/399 plant-native triples; `Damage` annotations in 50/179 introduced host triples. Those original HOSTS column flags are sparse and not an external larval survival validation. [Source CI #40465323311](https://github.com/zuizui0223/chocho/actions/runs/40465323311).
