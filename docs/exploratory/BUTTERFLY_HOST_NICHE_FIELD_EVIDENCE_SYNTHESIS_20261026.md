# Butterfly host-niche filtering versus competition — integrated macro-to-realized evidence (2026-10-26)

**Current verdict:** Potential larval-resource geography changed greatly in the original 239-butterfly `chocho` project. A new **independently acquired** 2000–2020 U.S. adult butterfly-count dataset supports three different levels of observational questions, but **no global or Illinois-wide causal host-introduction population growth or interspecific competition effect is established**. The only tentative signal is within-site **synchronous adult count-rate fluctuations** among butterflies sharing documented hosts; it is secondary, marginal and *not robust to treating all three exploratory endpoints as one family*.

The source adult field dataset is [Henry et al., *Scientific Data* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-025-05513-8), from [Figshare `10.6084/m9.figshare.27934602.v1`](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.27934602.v1), 55,066,096-byte ZIP verified MD5 `3161b1c8802a0d67771b2044e18da928`, CC BY 4.0. It contains 1,321,882 species×survey count rows across 19 original programmes and 21 years, with only **121 exact-name overlap** among the original `chocho` 239 butterflies and 47 species in eligible Illinois monitoring panels. Surveys are of adults, not larvae; their count/hour values are detection-effort indices, not absolute population density.

## Why an apparently positive annual synchrony is not a biological confirmation
- Three related endpoints (binary phi, Jaccard and within-site annual fluctuation correlation) were examined. A nominal random-graph right-tail **0.030** for the secondary endpoint does not pass a simple three-endpoint .05/3 = **.0167** boundary, even assuming independent valid permutations; graph reshuffling is a **posthoc source conditional** null, not a randomized field intervention.
- Species may share host plants because they share butterfly family/genus, habitat preference, climate response or flight phenology. The family-stratified degree null handles some—but not all—of those dependencies, and no local host plant survey, larval stage, phenological host biomass, climate anomaly or explicit detection model was included.
- Source reporting conventions require non-detection proxies: the lagged Illinois panel assigned **7,501 implied zeros** among 16,454 species×site×year cells (~45.6%), and those are not independently verified absences. Even with positive butterfly adult counts, a positive synchronized rate trend can reflect shared source weather and monitoring effort rather than a plant-mediated mechanism.
- The original host graph is a literature-based global accepted-plant inventory; it does not establish that a shared plant species actually occurs at each field monitoring site or is an edible resource to the local butterfly populations.
- The first lagged network null changed focal inclusion from 7,070 to 10,423 transitions across draw states (original 9,178); a fixed original-pair co-detection null was subsequently used for the independent field synchrony question, not to rescue that imperfect competition test.

## Why competition can coexist with resource filtering in nature
At broader scales, a shared larval-host distribution can act as a positive environmental filter, making two butterflies potentially suitable in the same region. That says little about competitive suppression unless co-occurring caterpillars overlap simultaneously in suitable **leaf biomass × instar × light microhabitat × seasonal window**. The specific [Nakadai et al. (2018) Japanese 232-butterfly](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00442-018-4062-1) biogeographical test found host sharing compatible with less geographic exclusivity. A [Hashimoto & Ohgushi (2023) larval density manipulation](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.10164) found **asymmetric** negative effects of invasive `Sericinus montela` on native `Atrophaneura alcinous` survival/development on shared `Aristolochia`. And [Jang et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.5141/jee.26.017) found fine riverbank **light-gradient niche partitioning** by the same nominal host-sharing butterflies. These source studies demonstrate that competition and local partitioning *can* occur in selected systems, not that either dominates globally.

## Testable new ecological hypothesis (for a separate original study, NOT the GEB current manuscript)

**Microenvironment- and time-dependent opportunity→interaction transition.** As hosts expand into regions, different butterfly consumers may track common environment/host productivity, giving synchronized adult fluctuations. Locally, competition can arise when simultaneous larval demand exceeds **effective edible plant production** and microhabitat/phenological partition is weak; otherwise, shared host geography may promote co-presence with little competition. This is a falsifiable, scale-dependent conceptual alternative to the trivial "more hosts = more butterflies" claim.

Decisive independent test requires:
1. Time-stamped local *introduced and native host abundance/biomass* surveys before and after host arrival, repeated adult/larval counts with detection effort.
2. Verified eggs, caterpillar feeding, instar, host leaf age/light microhabitat and annual survival to adult, not merely HOSTS records.
3. Experimental host-leaf biomass × competitor larval density × microhabitat sunlight treatments with both species' developmental performance; controlled heterospecific removal and plant quality measurements.
4. Hierarchical dynamic occupancy/abundance modelling that explicitly includes imperfect detection, abiotic covariates, phylogeny, species-site and year effects. Do not interpret coefficient on competitors as causal without design-identifying evidence.
