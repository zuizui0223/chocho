# When is an introduced host a new resource? Geographic novelty in butterfly larval-host geography

**Separate blinded alternative full Research Article, 17 October 2026 — one possible replacement storyline for existing GEB paper; author-review only. No edits to the frozen submitted PR #38.**

**Running title:** Novelty of butterfly host geography

## Structured abstract

**Aim:** Introduced host plants need not create geographically novel resources for consumers. We distinguished new butterfly larval-resource regions from overlapping candidate host supply and evaluated the sensitivity of this distinction to host-inventory completeness.

**Location:** Global; 365 WGSRPD3 geographic regions.

**Time period:** Native and introduced WCVP v13 plant distributions (without introduction dates); CHELSA 1981–2010 climate sensitivity.

**Major taxa studied:** 239 butterfly species and recorded larval host plants.

**Methods:** We joined accepted host–butterfly associations to native and contemporary plant regions and partitioned introduced host×butterfly×region incidences into native-supported, multiply introduced-supported and uniquely new butterfly×region resources. Plant-range nulls preserved host links, plant-family and regional margins, climate bins and local proximity. A separately curated European checklist added 1,027 candidate host links to 81 butterfly species.

**Results:** Of 75,346 introduced-host candidate incidences, 46,387 (61.6%) occurred inside butterfly native-host resource envelopes; 14,406 (19.1%) provided additional support within new cells and only 14,553 (19.3%) uniquely opened regional opportunity. Augmenting 81 butterfly host inventories with European literature associations shifted the native-overlap fraction to 65.5%. Critically, only 3,225 of 6,445 butterfly×region cells called *new* under either catalogue agreed between catalogues (Jaccard 0.500), although their aggregate number changed by only 414. An original-HOSTS climate- and 1,500-km-distance-matched exploratory null retained a small native-overlap excess (271–276 candidate incidences; 0.36 percentage points).

**Main conclusions:** Potential larval-host redistribution often overlaps pre-existing regional opportunity. More importantly, which places qualify as *new* depends strongly on recorded host relations; mapped supply does not establish realized local feeding, colonization or fitness.

**Keywords:** biogeography, butterflies, host records, introduced plants, larval resources, resource novelty, species interactions, specialization

## 1. Introduction

The movement of a plant and the movement of its herbivore are different processes. A larval host plant can arrive in a region where a butterfly is absent; conversely, a butterfly can occur in a region that has more than one candidate host. Even when consumers retain their host preferences, the global geography of their potential resources can change as plants are transported and established. Host plants are an important filter on butterfly ranges (Braschler & Hill, 2007; Slove & Janz, 2011; Lancaster, 2020; Neu et al., 2021), while human redistribution has changed floristic similarity worldwide (van Kleunen et al., 2015; Daru et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021).

Introduced plants sometimes provide larval resources for butterflies, but host acceptance and larval success are conditional on the species and setting (Graves & Shapiro, 2003; Braga, 2023). Observed plant–animal interaction networks can become more geographically similar following introductions (Fricke & Svenning, 2020). These observations do not establish that an introduced host species creates a new resource *region* for every butterfly linked to it. Expansion of a union of host distributions is largely mechanical when more host range cells are added. The ecological distinction is whether the consumer already had a different native host in that region, whether several introduced hosts contribute to one newly accessible region, or whether an introduced host first opens a new region in the focal consumer's mapped resource geography.

A second problem concerns the known host repertoire. Butterfly–plant databases combine observation and literature records of unequal geographic and taxonomic completeness. A native plant that already supported a butterfly could be absent from a catalogue, making an introduced host plant in the same region appear to open new geographic access. A newly documented host with introduced range cells can instead reveal previously unrecognised novel access. Evidence-filtered regional compilations such as Clarke (2024) make it possible to assess source dependence without inventing host links. A species may appear to gain the same number of resource regions under two catalogues while the *identities* of those regions are different.

Here we analyse a fixed 239-butterfly host network across WGSRPD3 plant-distribution regions. We ask **(i)** how the introduced host-incidence budget is divided among already native-supported resource geography, additional introduced host supports within newly accessible regions, and unique new butterfly×region access; **(ii)** whether the geographic identity of those new regions is stable under an independent Europe-derived larval foodplant catalogue; and **(iii)** whether observed placement in native-supported areas exceeds plant-range counterfactuals preserving progressively more taxonomic, climate and local geographic structure. Diet breadth and a separately selected butterfly occurrence panel provide context rather than causal identification.

The host-inventory and increasingly strict plant-range null analyses were developed after the first expansion analyses. We treat them as **post-hoc evidence and sensitivity checks**, not as pre-registered predictions of plant introduction or butterfly establishment. Our target is regional *potential* larval-host availability, not feeding or demographic production.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Conceptual design: larval host breadth, resource geography and butterfly occurrence

We organized the analysis around three related but non-equivalent quantities. **Taxonomic interaction breadth** describes which kinds of larval hosts a butterfly uses, measured here as host-family richness. **Resource geography** describes where those known hosts occur, represented by the union of their geographic distributions. **Realized butterfly geography** describes where the butterfly is actually observed. Human redistribution enters between the first two quantities: moving a known host can alter a butterfly's resource geography without changing its taxonomic interaction breadth.

We therefore reconstructed each butterfly's resource geography under native-only and contemporary host distributions and measured the difference as anthropogenic resource gain. We then asked whether redistribution changes the **structure** of resource space: similarity among regional butterfly resource assemblages, overlap among butterfly resource envelopes, and the geography over which butterfly pairs share exact host species. Host-family breadth was retained as a secondary modifier of proportional gain, and contemporary butterfly occurrences provided a secondary check on realization of the added opportunity.

The LepTraits-derived descriptor panel contained 339 species (Shirey et al. 2022). Resource analyses used 239 species with positive host-family breadth and at least one reconstructed native host-resource WGSRPD3 unit. A conservative subset of 215 species also required the number of resolved host species to equal or exceed LepTraits host-family count. A separate 32-species panel was used for occurrence validation and climate analysis; it had been selected to balance the subsequent climate test, not optimized for validation. Twenty-four species passed the climate quality criteria.

The resource reconstruction is descriptive and uses fixed LepTraits, HOSTS and WCVP inputs. The 32-species panel was assembled separately for the climate test, using eligibility and analysis rules fixed before its ecological responses were evaluated; the occurrence analysis is a secondary use of that panel. Resource homogenization, shared-resource exposure, host-removal stress tests, the host-contribution decomposition and later null-model sensitivities are post-hoc analyses and are interpreted accordingly.

### 2.2 Reconstructing native and contemporary resource geography

Larval host records came from HOSTS and plant taxonomy and distributions from WCVP (Robinson et al. 2023; Govaerts et al. 2021). Species-level host names were resolved to accepted WCVP taxa. Under WCVP terminology, introduced distributions are wild non-native records (including casual, naturalised and invasive occurrences), not cultivation records; plants that are only cultivated or merely planted without self-reproduction are not classified as introduced.

For each butterfly, the **native resource envelope** was the union of WGSRPD level-3 units in which any resolved host was native, extant and non-doubtful. The **contemporary resource envelope** additionally retained introduced host distributions. Host identities were held fixed between the two envelopes, so their difference isolates redistribution of already-known resources rather than host switching or expansion of the butterfly's recorded diet. WGSRPD3 is a coarse regional framework (Brummitt et al. 2001), so these envelopes represent geographic resource opportunity rather than local host occupancy.

As a crop-host sensitivity, we repeated the reconstruction after excluding host taxa listed in the FAO Indicative Crop Classification, first by exact botanical binomial and then with an intentionally over-conservative rule that also excluded all congeners when the FAO list specified a crop as spp. Host-family breadth was kept fixed as the specialization predictor; only the reconstructed resource envelopes and host contributions were recalculated (Supplementary Table S7).

We calculated native and contemporary resource breadth, introduced-added units, the contemporary/native ratio, log resource expansion, and the introduced share of contemporary geography. In a post-hoc descriptive decomposition, each butterfly × WGSRPD3 unit newly added by introduced host distributions contributed one unit of credit divided equally among the host species that added that unit. Credits therefore summed exactly to the aggregate number of added butterfly × region units. We summarized the concentration of those credits across host plants using cumulative top-k shares, the Gini coefficient and the inverse-Herfindahl effective contributor number. As a taxonomic sensitivity, contributing plant species were first collapsed to unique genera within each butterfly × region unit and the unit credit was then divided equally among those genera. This decomposition is explicitly contribution-weighted: a plant can accumulate credit by being used by more focal butterflies, by adding more geographic units, or both. We therefore use it to describe which resources generate the reconstructed opportunity, not as a sample of independent plant-level effects.

### 2.3 Counting overlapping and geographically novel introduced host supply

For butterfly b with its recorded accepted host set S, we let its native regional envelope be the union of extant and non-doubtful regions in which at least one plant in S was native. Its contemporary envelope is the union of native and introduced distributions of the same plants. A newly supplied butterfly×WGSRPD3 region is present in the contemporary but not the native envelope.

We distinguish this butterfly×region cell from an introduced host×butterfly×region **incidence**. Let H count all such candidate incidences for host plants whose ranges include an introduced occurrence. A counts candidate incidences in butterfly×region cells that are already supported by a recorded native host plant. U counts one first-support incidence for each unique newly supplied butterfly×region cell. R counts all further introduced host incidences supplying these novel resource cells. Thus **H = A + U + R** exactly; the identity of the first host does not imply historical priority. Fractions A/H, U/H and R/H are descriptive *shares of candidate host-incidence volume*, not probabilities of successful larval development or butterfly colonisation.

### 2.4 Independent larval-host source sensitivity and geographical identity

We reused the exact, independently archived Butterfly Conservation Europe foodplant pages derived from Clarke (2024), retaining evidential classes 1–3 and resolving accepted plant names against the same WCVP v13 source. The earlier study's 239-butterfly panel had 92 exact butterfly-name matches, 83 with foodplant records, and **81 species with 1,027 candidate additional butterfly–host links representing 679 accepted plant IDs** absent from their original HOSTS interactions. All candidates were added together; no link was selected based on the response. The 158 other butterfly host inventories were held unchanged.

Both source scenarios were projected through identical frozen native/introduced plant distribution tables, and all original 239-species resource totals and separately archived BCE host-sensitivity totals were required to agree before the results were accepted. The BCE source is **Europe-derived rather than globally representative**: an accepted larval association in a European source was projected across the worldwide geographical ranges of the linked plant species as a permissive sensitivity, not independently verified in each region.

Among the 81 modified host inventories, we compared sets of *introduced-added butterfly×region cells*, calculating retained cell identities, cells formerly considered new that became supported by another newly documented native host, and previously unrecognised new cells. Geographic source agreement was expressed as intersection/union Jaccard. Species with empty unions had undefined Jaccard and were excluded from the within-species median. All 158 species without augmented host lists would have artificially high source agreement by design, so the modified species were reported separately.

### 2.5 Coarse climate- and geography-constrained plant-range nulls

In an exploratory plant-level counterfactual, native host distributions and recorded butterfly–host identities were fixed. We performed degree-preserving introduced plant×region two-edge swaps within plant family×WGSRPD Level-1 continent, retaining the number of introduced host regions of each plant and the introduced plant-family richness in each region. No swap could introduce a plant into a region in which it was already native. The total candidate incidence count H remained constant while native overlap A and unique additions U could change. Two independently initialised baseline chains retained 499 configurations each.

Climate-matched nulls further restricted swaps to within-continent bins of CHELSA V2.1 mean annual temperature and annual precipitation, using an archived 1981–2010 regional climate summary covering all 365 focal regions. Two chains retained 299 configurations each. A geographically local version required paired regions to be within **1,500 km** of each other and required each new placement to lie within 1,500 km of at least one of that plant's *original* introduced regions, measured using WGSRPD3 polygon representative points. These distances are sensitivity scales, not butterfly dispersal estimates.

The preliminary local chains mixed poorly; their nominal permutation-style tail probabilities were rejected. A finite longer-chain qualification repeated only the primary 1,500-km comparison with two new random seeds, 30× total-edge burn-in proposals, 20× total-edge proposals between 99 recorded configurations per chain, and predeclared *numerical* lag-one, approximate effective-sample-size, early/late mean and between-chain-median reliability gates. The gates passed but were specified after seeing the preliminary numerical weakness: they do not transform this post-hoc design into a confirmatory test. Crucially, this spatial null has **not** been rerun on the BCE-augmented interaction graph; its result applies to the original HOSTS source only.

### 2.6 Secondary ecological-context analyses

We retained the original Spearman comparison between host-family breadth and proportional resource-envelope expansion, with bootstrap uncertainty, finite-support and phylogenetic sensitivity reported in Supporting Information. Regional potential resource-assemblage Jaccard similarity was computed across the 355 WGSRPD3 regions with non-empty native resource opportunity, with a fixed added-incidence margin null. These are *potential resources*, not observed butterfly community assemblages.

We separately inspected the 32-species GBIF climate panel for butterfly×region occurrence cells from 2010–2026 lying outside the species' native larval-host envelope. We measured their overlap with introduced-host-added opportunity and compared it with a level-1-region matched geometric null. This use of the climate-selected panel as a butterfly validation sample was a **secondary exploratory** analysis. Occurrence alone cannot verify local larval-host use or that host introduction caused range expansion.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 | Widespread larval-resource increase masks considerable overlap

**Panel scope:** 239 butterflies; 365 geographically delineated regions; fixed HOSTS records and WCVP v13 native/introduced statuses; 670 plant species supply at least one distinct new resource unit.

Observed native butterfly×region resource cells **26,530**; contemporary **41,083**, an increase of **14,553 (+54.9%)**. Species gaining any extra region: **206/239 (86.2%)**. These are the scale and context, **not** the lead surprise.

To unpack the geographic novelty of candidate introduced host distribution:

\[
H = A + N,\qquad N = U + R
\]

where `H=75,346` introduced host×butterfly×region incidences, `A=46,387` incidences falling in regions already within the butterfly's native larval-resource envelope, `N=28,959` incidences falling into novel butterfly×region resource cells, `U=14,553` distinct novel butterfly×region cells and `R=14,406` extra supporting host incidences beyond one per new resource cell. **H=A+R+U is a bookkeeping identity with one incidence counted as the first support of each new cell, not a ratio of population growth or resource biomass.**

The last two terms are not independent: highly overlapping introduced plants can supply multiple supporting incidences within one new cell. None of these counts measures edible plant biomass, insect acceptance or reproductive success.

### 3.2 | Host-inventory completeness materially changes what counts as new opportunity

In a **source-completeness sensitivity**, we re-used the previously archived BCE-derived version of Clarke (2024) rather than newly selecting host taxa by result. The source had 92 exact matching butterfly names, 83 with source foodplant records, and 1,027 *candidate* additional accepted-WCVP-ID larval-host associations for 81 focal butterflies (679 plant species). Candidate relations were added **together** to the frozen HOSTS list and the original WCVP v13 native/contemporary plant maps were rebuilt. The original 239-species baseline reproduced exactly.

Across all 239 butterflies, reconstructed *native* resource cells increased **26,530→29,852**, contemporary cells **41,083→44,819**, and net new resource cells **14,553→14,967**. The fixed-host-list candidate introduced host×butterfly×region incidence pool `H` changed **75,346→103,349**. Its partition changed substantially:

| Accepted-host inventory | Native-overlap `A` | Extra supports in newly opened cells `R` | Unique newly opened cells `U` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Frozen HOSTS | 46,387 (**61.6%**) | 14,406 (**19.1%**) | 14,553 (**19.3%**) |
| HOSTS + 1,027 BCE candidate links | 67,740 (**65.5%**) | 20,642 (**20.0%**) | 14,967 (**14.5%**) |

Percentages are proportions of `H` **within each source inventory**, not independent observations, ecological production or intrinsic probabilities of plant utilization. While the new source records add **1,817** previously unsupported butterfly×region cells, they also reclassify **1,403** previously introduced-added cells as natively supported, giving a **net +414** unique additions. Among 81 augmented butterflies, **36 gain**, **35 lose**, and **10 do not change** in the number of regions *classified* as introduced-additional. This shows that aggregate geographic gain can appear stable while the regional classification within butterfly species shifts substantially.

**Geographic identity turns over much more than the total count.** Among the **81** butterflies with additional BCE candidate host links, the original and augmented inventories classified **4,628 and 5,042** butterfly×WGSRPD3 region cells as introduced-added. Their *intersection* contains only **3,225** cells, with **1,403** old additions reclassified as native and **1,817** newly identified introduced additions; the union contains **6,445** cells. Thus the *set-level identity agreement* of new-resource regions is only **Jaccard = 3,225 / 6,445 = 0.5004**, with almost half of the union classified differently. The per-species median Jaccard is **0.570** among the 77 butterflies with at least one new-resource cell under either catalogue; four have no such cells, for which Jaccard is undefined. Of all 81 butterflies, **54** simultaneously lose old introduced-new classifications through native reclassification and gain other newly supported cells. Do not report the high **full-239 Jaccard = 0.803** without noting that the other 158 butterflies have identical inventories by design.

Species cases make the ecological interpretation concrete without supplying independent proof: *Lycaena dispar* changes from **0 to 146** candidate new-resource regions, *Minois dryas* from **157 to 47** (125 old-new cells reclassified as native, 15 previously unsupported newly discovered), and *Thymelicus lineola* remains **65 to 65** but has **27** old cells reclassified and **27** different new cells added (set Jaccard **0.413**). These are **deterministically selected extreme/hidden-turnover examples**, not independently sampled effects or observed range shifts. All values were rederived from the [source-validated 81-species archive](../../provenance/reviewer_defenses/results/butterfly_bce_added_region_identity_turnover_v0.1.json).

The source sensitivity is **Europe-biased and only one-directional**, and BCE/Clarke evidence comes from documented European feeding/oviposition records, not independent worldwide larvae sampled at every WCVP locality. It cannot establish a globally corrected 65.5% or demonstrate that the earlier small *conditional spatial-null* excess remains after the added host links. Its importance is instead to quantify a real constraint on the **interpretation and completeness** of host-resource geography.

### 3.3 | Introduced ranges are more likely to coincide with native-resource geography than the constrained structural null would predict — but only slightly

The initial null preserves planted-host degree and continental plant-family region margins. Of the fixed 75,346 candidate incidences, observed native-envelope overlap is **46,387**, versus **45,301/45,298** in two 499-draw null chains (**+1,086/+1,089**).

CHELSA temperature/precipitation-bin matching reduces the excess to **+552/+558**. A more stringent *locally tethered* comparison (1,500 km, original introduced host footprint, the same plant-family × continent × 2×2 climate bins, two independent longer chains) retains **+271/+276**, or **+0.36 percentage points** of the fixed candidate incidence budget. Independent-chain simulation diagnostic thresholds were satisfied: autocorrelation lag-1 ≈ 0.06–0.10; estimated ESS ≈ 75–85 for the primary A response; chain median difference 5 units. Distinct new resource additions remain **−264/−267** lower than the respective null medians (**14,817/14,820**).

These are **small conditional differences** in a coarse mapped resource system and **have not been re-estimated under the augmented BCE host inventory**. The inventory-sensitivity change in the native-overlap share (+3.98 percentage points) is numerically much larger than the original-inventory local-null excess (~0.36 percentage points), but they are different contrasts and must not be compared as if they were exchangeable effect estimates. Long-chain numerical qualification does not convert a post-hoc comparison into a confirmatory permutation test or into identification of climate, geography or introduction mechanisms.

The short-chain local null (199 serial draws per seed) had lag-1 around 0.78 at 1,500 km and ESS ≈ 9–10, and its nominal p-values were rejected as evidentially unreliable. The longer-chain sensitivity was run once with a finite frozen mixing gate; results were stable, not chosen by repeated threshold fishing.

### 3.4 | Specialization does not capture geographic novelty well

Across focal butterflies, the association between host-family breadth and proportional introduced resource gain is **rho=0.008 (bootstrap 95% CI −0.111 to 0.128)**. This does **not** prove a mathematically exact zero effect; a post-hoc narrow ±0.10 equivalence margin was not met (approximate TOST p=0.078). Host-family breadth and native geographic resource breadth are only partially correlated (**rho=0.276** full sample). In the more host-taxonomy-adequate 215-species subset, **18 one-family host specialists** nevertheless occupy the top quartile of geographic resource opportunity.

For the introduced-added cells themselves, **8,574/14,553 (58.9%)** are supported by a single introduced host, but the proportion is not unusually high relative to a plant-range null with relevant margins. More host species typically allow more redundant added opportunities; **taxonomic host-family breadth by itself does not capture that spatial portfolio structure**.

### 3.5 | Evidence that resource opportunity is biologically relevant is partial, and conservation claims are deliberately bounded

A **secondary, non-prospective** 32-species climate-stratified GBIF panel included **115** butterfly outside-native resource-region occurrence units from 23 informative species; **66** coincided with introduced host-resource gains versus a geography/region-matched null median **49**. Species sensitivity and uneven GBIF coverage warrant careful reporting; the result is **not** proof of introduced-host larval use or successful colonization.

Among the reconstructed regions, regional butterfly **potential resource assemblage** Jaccard similarity increased **0.277→0.462** (+66.8%), but a null fixing both butterfly-specific and region-specific additions predicted about **0.453**; the residual is only **+0.0086**, falling to **+0.0036** under continental margin controls. It is inaccurate to transfer the raw +66.8% to *observed butterfly community homogenization*, or claim the new shared-host exposure caused competition. Likewise, 920 of 986 host-sharing butterfly pairs acquire new shared-host regions, even though **at least 300 pairs** must have gained shared-host geography while their normalized host×region overlap declined; these are distinct quantities.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 What does a host introduction actually add to a consumer's geography?

Many introduced host incidences in this focal butterfly network fall where the butterfly already has at least one native resource, or provide extra candidate hosts within a new butterfly×region cell. An additional plant occurrence need not therefore represent a novel region of potential larval development. The arithmetic decomposition is elementary but addresses a substantive mismatch between the usual size of a host range union and the ecological question of *new consumer access*. This distinction matters because expanding geographic opportunity, increasing host options within existing habitat and increasing actual population carrying capacity are different processes.

Overlap is not evidence that an introduced host is ecologically redundant in function. Additional foliage may change leaf phenology, resource biomass, nutritional chemistry or enemy exposure. Likewise a host plant mapped in a newly added region may be rare, unsynchronised with butterfly development or unacceptable to larvae. This study cannot identify those possibilities from coarse region-level occurrence statuses. These are hypotheses for independent fieldwork, not outcomes demonstrated by our resource maps.

### 4.2 The geography of novelty depends on documented host links

The supplementary host inventory yields a more striking interpretive consequence than the small structural-null residual. The source-augmented panel detects 1,817 butterfly×region cells not counted as introduced-added by the original HOSTS network, yet reclassifies 1,403 originally new cells as already native-supported. Consequently the total new-cell count changes little compared with the replacement of *which cells* are counted. Among the 81 augmented butterflies, only about half of the union of source-classified novel cells receives the same classification under both inventories. Even a butterfly with unchanged regional count can show substantial underlying geographic reassignment.

These are classification changes between two evidence sources, not ecological shifts through time or independent estimates of true butterfly host use. Clarke's (2024) European evidence records are uneven by species and region. Extending their candidate host relationships globally through WCVP distribution maps does not confirm feeding across the entire plant range. The 81 species with extra records were not sampled as an independent global replication cohort, and the 158 unchanged butterflies cannot be used to dilute their disagreement. Nevertheless, the analysis shows why host-inventory completeness affects the very denominator and regional interpretation of apparent resources.

### 4.3 Modest geographic overlap beyond a restricted counterfactual

The original host-plant range null suggested that introduced hosts coincide with already native-supported butterfly regions slightly more often than expected under taxonomic and geographic margins. The effect shrank when climate classes and local geographical proximity were held more nearly constant, leaving about 0.36 percentage points of the candidate incidence budget unexplained by these particular rearrangements. That residual cannot identify a mechanism of plant transport, climatic matching, local consumer habitat or competitive filtering. Fine-scale climate and vegetation, introduction pathways, geographic sampling effort and phylogenetic differences remain unresolved. Most importantly, this residual was **not tested under the alternative host list**, so it is not an inventory-robust result.

### 4.4 Implications for comparative and conservation ecology

The secondary finding that host-family diet breadth was nearly unrelated to proportional mapped expansion does not prove strict equivalence or ecological independence. Host taxonomy and the geography of actual host species encode different dimensions of a butterfly's potential niche. The separate occurrence panel found some correspondence between added host maps and real butterfly presence, but not verified local feeding on the introduced host. Introduced plants can be useful alternative resources or ecological traps depending on habitat and development (Graves & Shapiro, 2003; Singer & Parmesan, 2018; Braga, 2023). We therefore do not infer a general conservation benefit of non-native plants or recommend keeping invasive plants solely on the basis of potential host-range overlap.

A decisive next ecological test would link candidate host range records to local oviposition, larvae, development to adults, seasonal leaf accessibility and resource abundance—ideally contrasted with native host alternatives. Such measurements would distinguish a host that is merely **present** in the mapped region from one that **produces additional butterflies**. Rather than multiplying further post-hoc spatial nulls, the main contribution here is a transparent regional-resource estimand and the demonstration of its dependence on independent host-link information.

## 5. Limitations

WCVP native versus introduced labels do not supply introduction dates, nor do WGSRPD3 distribution ranges describe site-scale plant abundance and climatic suitability. The fixed species-level HOSTS graph assumes that known interactions are geographically transferable. The Europe-derived BCE sensitivity improves the documentation of some candidate links but is not globally representative; the selected host-inventory contrasts do not define a confidence interval around a true worldwide network. The nulls are post-hoc, conditioned on their stated margins and subject to finite MCMC mixing, and the source-augmented map has not been subjected to a new matched local null. The GBIF occurrence panel does not contain verified local larval host records. We cannot infer reproduction, host quality, competition, host switching, colonisation history or a causal ecological effect of introduced plants.

## 6. Conclusions

Moving a known host and opening a new butterfly resource region are not equivalent events. In the original 239-species reconstruction, only 19.3% of candidate introduced host-incidence volume accounts for the first candidate host in a unique newly added butterfly×region cell; most other incidences overlap a native-supported region or add further support inside an already newly opened region. Importantly, supplementing the known host repertoire replaces many of the geographic identities classified as newly available while changing their total count comparatively little. The robust conceptual result is therefore **the source-conditional geography of potential larval-resource novelty**, not an estimate of the number of butterflies generated by plant introductions.

## Data and Code Availability

Analysis code and the inputs required to reproduce the reported results and figures are versioned in the study repository. Public source datasets include LepTraits, HOSTS, WCVP, GBIF, CHELSA and WGSRPD. The secondary occurrence validation used 53,434 GBIF occurrence records. An exact-ID archival download requested those same records (https://doi.org/10.15468/dl.pp5nc9); GBIF returned 53,144 records, indicating that 290 records used in the frozen analysis were no longer returned by GBIF at archival download time. For the post-hoc European host-source sensitivity in Supplementary Table S9, a separate anonymized source-data and reproducibility addendum accompanies the main anonymous review archive; the appended checklist is derived from Clarke (2024) via Butterfly Conservation Europe rather than new independent feeding observations. A stable public archival snapshot of the analysis package will be deposited before publication.

The alternative results, analysis programs, synthetic tests and provenance receipts are retained separately in draft PR #43.
