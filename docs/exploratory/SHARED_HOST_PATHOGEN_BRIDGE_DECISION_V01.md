# Can a shared introduced host change butterflies from potential resource partners into pathogen-linked competitors?

**2026-10-10 | EXPLORATORY HYPOTHESIS AND PRIOR-ART GATE | NO NEW CROSS-SPECIES INFECTION RESULT**

Repository isolation: `exploration/realized-host-window-v01` only; do NOT modify frozen GEB submission PR #38.

## Scientific change of direction

The chocho submission measures **potential exact-shared-host geography**, not simultaneous local use or ecological interactions. Increased mapped shared hosts (+127.1% butterfly-pair × region exposure in GEB) cannot by itself establish actual butterfly competition. Nor can further random network swaps. A discriminating biological alternative:

> When butterflies use the same human-introduced larval host plant, does shared plant use transfer a pathogen between the butterfly species, offsetting or reversing the plant's benefit as an expanded larval resource?

This is **pathogen-mediated apparent competition / dilution** under real resource sharing, distinct from depletion competition. It can be positive, negative, or null. Even if both species use the same plant and both are permissive hosts for the same pathogen, transmission between them is **not** established.

## Prior art checked (do not rediscover as ours)

1. Muchoney et al. (2022), [Ecology and Evolution](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.8723), wild *Euphydryas phaeton* using exotic *Plantago lanceolata*: poorer cellular immunity / higher field JcDV loads, but no strong survival difference. The 2016 and 2017 endpoints differ, so do not assign one universal sign to exotic-plant fitness.
2. Muchoney et al. (2023), [Ecology Letters](https://doi.org/10.1111/ele.14162), *Anartia jatrophae*: experimentally virus-challenged larvae had lower viral burden and better survival on exotic *P. lanceolata* than native *Bacopa*. Thus "exotic host = disease trap" is already falsified as a universal rule.
3. Muchoney et al. (2024), [Journal of Invertebrate Pathology, PMID 39159850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39159850/): experimental viral dose × foodplant responses in *E. phaeton* and *A. jatrophae* already quantified. Cross-species differences on the same exotic host are **prior art**, not our result.
4. Christensen et al. (2024), [Ecology](https://doi.org/10.1002/ecy.4282): natal-cluster **within-species** horizontal and vertical transmission in *E. phaeton* and host-dependent survival already shown. It did not establish transmission from one butterfly species to another on one wild host plant.
5. [Disease from leaves to landscapes (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11858745/): field viral hotspots within *E. phaeton* are associated with spatial arrangement and host chemistry. Pathogen landscape heterogeneity alone is not a new discovery.
6. [Crowding reduces per-capita parasite infection risk in a butterfly (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12419877/): more conspecific caterpillars on shared leaves can LOWER each individual's risk. Thus "more shared host geography means more per-capita infections" cannot be assumed.
7. De Roode et al. (2011), [Ecology Letters](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1461-0248.2011.01604.x): aphids sharing a milkweed can change monarch parasite virulence via plant chemistry; indirect shared-host effects are **known**.
8. Sanaei & de Roode (2025), [Biological Reviews](https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.70003): the insect hostplant–pathogen literature calls for direct identification of mechanisms. A multi-species correlation cannot isolate pathogen transfer from plant chemistry.

**The new empirical target is actual heterospecific transfer and associated recipient viability, not differential pathogen susceptibility of separately challenged caterpillars.**

## Source-grounded but currently hypothetical focal community

*Junonia coenia* (common buckeye) and *Anartia jatrophae* (white peacock) are **both present in the frozen chocho 239-butterfly metric panel**. In Florida, both butterflies occur at regional scale. A University of Florida fact sheet documents *J. coenia* feeding on introduced *Plantago lanceolata*; original Muchoney experimental work confirms *A. jatrophae* **can** use *P. lanceolata* under laboratory conditions. JcDV is named after *J. coenia* and experimentally infects *A. jatrophae*.

**Open fatal eligibility gaps:**

- Florida regional sympatry is NOT confirmed contemporaneous local host sharing;
- the published experiments do NOT show wild *A. jatrophae* larvae on *P. lanceolata* in the same sites as *J. coenia*;
- no verified JcDV transfer *between these species* through the same individual plant exists in the sources reviewed;
- near-sympatric other *Junonia* are taxonomically complex in Florida, and must not be silently assigned to *J. coenia*;
- neither paper supports estimating per-host fitness or extinction from chocho WGSRPD3 mapped resource count.

*Euphydryas phaeton* is **not in the 239-species frozen metrics** despite being informative published experimental prior art. Do not present it as a mapped chocho focal species.

If any field eligibility gate fails, STOP this exact species pair and register a new biologically justified candidate **before** inspecting cross-species effects. Do not choose species because the pathogen bridge estimate looks positive.

## Three strict gates before any independent ecology claim

### Gate A: independent local co-use and pathogen occurrence

Collect or access data documenting exactly identified *Plantago lanceolata*, larval feeding by **each** butterfly species, site and plant ID, calendar dates / cohort overlap, observer effort and host plant negative controls. Require at least 3 independently observed field sites over 2 seasons, with both species' larval use on the host documented at >=2 sites. The field use need not be on an identical plant individual for *sympatry* but an identical-plant transfer claim demands independently confirmed same-plant use.

Separate sensitive observations: molecular detection of JcDV in a caterpillar is not proof the detected virus was acquired there, and viral DNA on a leaf is not evidence the virus is infectious. Document the original source study, exact location/time and assay contamination controls. If geography, stage timing, taxonomic ID or original source provenance fails, classify as a **laboratory-only candidate**.

### Gate B: randomized transfer pathway (subject to institutional approvals)

Compare larval recipients placed in standardized, independent experimental plants/enclosures that have a heterospecific donor-use history against matched controls without infectious donor exposure. Use separate recipients and donors and a shared plant substrate to isolate the possible transfer route. Match larval instar, numbers, plant biomass/phenology, handling and environment. Randomize at **plant/enclosure** level and keep source family/population balanced. Obtain appropriate institutional pathogen handling / insect movement permits before work. This is an analytical design, **not** an operational viral propagation protocol.

Main response: verified *new recipient infections* among initially negative larvae per assigned plant/enclosure, with independently checked pathogen identity and noninfectious exposure controls. Include all exposed recipients, including deaths. **Primary estimand:** recipient infection risk difference for heterospecific-donor-exposed vs donor-free condition on the same plant species; positive, zero and negative signs are ecologically meaningful.

A donor–recipient association without infectious-source verification cannot distinguish plant trait induction, immune differences, disturbance or pathogen transmission.

### Gate C: demography, not just positive PCR

Track **flight-capable adult emergence per originally assigned recipient cohort**, including zeros, and distinguish infection-mediated change from diet effects. Measure resource depletion and accessibility; include exposure-free plant controls, all assigned offspring, larval and plant stage, enemies and phenology. Site/plant/enclosure is the experimental replicate, not every larva or PCR well. A decrease in adult output under heterospecific donor exposure, with independently verified cross-species acquisition, would be evidence consistent with pathogen-mediated negative indirect interaction *under those conditions*. If only qPCR differs but adult output does not, do **not** claim a demographic trap or apparent competition.

## The most decisive counterfactual

Can two regions with the same live *Plantago* host supply have **opposite consumer payoffs** when heterospecific infected donors are present vs absent, while plant quantity is held fixed? This tests something that host-range unions and more geographical null models cannot mechanically determine.

**Do not convert this into a global law from one pair.** Independent species/population replication and recipient demographic endpoints would be required before claiming widespread cross-species effects.

## Immediate research decision

- Existing field + experiment evidence supports **biological plausibility only**, not a new effect.
- No public currently reviewed dataset jointly measures local co-use + verified donor identity + cross-species pathogen transfer + viable recipient adult output.
- Existing GEB results remain separate and scientifically unchanged.
- Next meaningful measurement is **contemporaneous direct local feeding and pathogen identity** for the candidate pair; if unavailable, prioritize field survey/experimental system rather than static distribution simulation.
